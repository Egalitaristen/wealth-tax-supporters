## 2. Wealth-tax history and live proposals (2010 onwards)
- Hungary has no net wealth tax. The Orbán governments never proposed one.
- 2020-05: opposition MPs (Szél, Hadházy, Szabó Szabolcs) tabled a special levy on recipients of state money; rejected in committee. Out of definition (see exclusions).
- 2023-10-11: six Párbeszéd MPs tabled T/5575 "A szolidaritási vagyonadóról" (1.7% / 2.1% / 3.5% above HUF 600m).
- 2025-08-30: Péter Magyar (Tisza) pledged a wealth tax above HUF 5bn; the Tisza programme later set 1% above HUF 1bn.
- 2025-10-03: Párbeszéd tabled T/12722 "A milliárdosok vagyonadójáról" (1% above HUF 1bn).
- 2026-04: Tisza won the election. A government decision of 14 May 2026 (1147/2026) ordered preparation of the law [verify: seen in a search summary only].
- 2026-10-06: the government announced 1% above HUF 1bn and 1.5% above HUF 100bn from 1 January 2027, first payment by 31 August 2027, and put the draft out for public consultation. Whether Parliament has voted is not checked.
- Business federations (MGYOSZ, MKIK, VOSZ and others) commented on the draft and want carve-outs; recorded as exclusions, not supporters.

## 7. Run R005 (14 November 2026)

The Mi Hazánk 2026 election programme explicitly calls for a wealth tax on the richest. The party criticised the separate government T/930 proposal in the November debate; that criticism does not establish abandonment of the party’s own narrower policy. Gergely Karácsony personally endorsed his 2017 programme, which proposed 1% annually on a broad range of assets above HUF 100 million with specified exceptions. His 2021 interview instead described a tax on individually high-value assets; this is not used as qualifying evidence. Economist Ferenc Büttl personally endorsed a wealth tax, and László Botka proposed a tax on wealth above HUF 100 million in 2017, while leaving the rate and recurrence open.

A cached official committee report for T/11920/5/1 reproduces a 2025 DK-led amendment with 16 named submitters and a 1% annual levy on combined assets above HUF 1 billion. The original amendment was blocked by CAPTCHA, and the June roll call concerns a package containing other tax changes. The report and the vote identify promising follow-up leads, not independently verified votes on a standalone wealth tax. The party’s 2011 proposal and its leader’s opposing statement in 2017 also need the intervening and later policy timeline established before treating the party’s current stance as settled.

## 9. Run R007 (14 November 2026)

The Democratic Coalition’s own 2026 election programme, page 85, recommends a tax on billion-forint fortunes and separately mentions inheritance; the text does not set a rate or specify recurrence. It is included for the stock-of-wealth component, not the inheritance measure. The party’s previous 2011–17 positions need a fuller chronology. A 2026 speech by Klára Dobrev criticised using wealth tax in place of prosecution for stolen wealth; that does not by itself retract support for a separate tax on lawful wealth.

The EU Official Journal roll call for 2 April 2025, section 1.50, lists Dobrev and Csaba Molnár under votes in favour of amendment 82 to A10-0042/2025. That standalone amendment asked the Commission to propose a 3% minimum tax on wealth above EUR 100 million as an EU own resource; it was rejected 145–529, not enacted. Their individual affirmative votes, rather than the final vote on the overall budget resolution, support their entries. The amendment wording is reproduced by AmendEU, and Telex explained the rejected vote on 11 April 2025.

The search-indexed committee report T/11920/10 associates the 16 names with point /5/1 and reproduces a broad wealth-tax provision, but the live official PDF still returns CAPTCHA and the original amendment is unavailable. A different DK tax-package PDF produced a mismatch between rendered content and retrieved text; for this reason the amendment sponsors remain leads until the report or original is independently checked as a real PDF. No supporters were inferred from the bundled vote. Civil organisations’ consultation requests sought changes but did not clearly endorse the tax itself.

## 13. Run R011 (14 November 2026)

Tisza parliamentary group leader Andrea Anna Bujdosó endorsed the billionaires’ wealth tax in her 4 November speech, referring to the preceding day’s T/930 debate. Although she also used the phrase luxury tax, the specific measure debated was the broad net-wealth proposal; the statement is not treated as support for a property-only tax. ## 14. Run R012 (14 November 2026)

Mihály Varga, founder of the KÉSZ construction group, said in a Forbes interview republished by his company that he basically supported Tisza’s wealth-tax plan, while questioning how someone with wealth tied up in a business would pay it. This is qualified support; he is not the former finance minister of the same name.

A 22 January 2017 Párbeszéd statement signed by spokesperson Bence Tordai independently documents the party’s earlier proposed annual 1% wealth tax on wealth above HUF 100 million. It lists financial assets, business holdings and other assets, with a home exemption; it does not specify debt treatment. The party and its named author were already in the register, so these are earlier evidence rows, not new actors.

A closer reading of László Botka’s March and April 2017 interviews confirms that he proposed taxation above HUF 100 million in wealth while denying that he proposed a general wealth tax. The interviews do not specify the full taxable asset base, whether liabilities count, the rate or recurrence. His existing row has been clarified and a lead added to check the programme; the threshold must not be treated as proof of an aggregate net-wealth base. Jámbor’s separately recorded 2017 support for that proposal should be read with the same qualification; he also has independent 2023 sponsorship evidence.

## 15. Run R013 (14 November 2026)

Szeged.hu reproduces László Botka’s signed 6 April 2020 solidarity programme. Separate from his proposed contribution on companies’ earlier public-contract proceeds, he proposed a percentage contribution based on the capital wealth of politically connected beneficiaries, with thresholds of HUF 1 billion, 10 billion and 100 billion. This is additional evidence for an existing actor, not a new supporter. The statement does not establish the full asset base, treatment of debts, whether rates are marginal, or whether the charge recurs; it is not treated as proof of the design of his separate 2017 proposal. A 2017 street-forum report distinguishes a wealth threshold from a separate charge on expensive cars, but still does not define the full wealth base.

Former central-bank governor András Simor’s 2019 interview says a wealth tax could be useful in some circumstances but explicitly says he was neither for nor against it; a 2026 report describes him as rejecting the proposal. The positive headline alone is not evidence of support. Tax consultant Zsolt Nyári discussed a potentially justified tax and a broad base, but the source is conditional and does not establish that he advocates adopting it; he remains a lead.

A 12 November report said debate on committee reports and votes on the T/930 bill were scheduled for the week beginning 23 November. As of this run the bill is not recorded as enacted.
