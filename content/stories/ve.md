A person who owns US$40 million in Venezuela and has been classed as a special taxpayer owes about US$100,000 a year on it, 0.25% of the fortune.[4][5] The law lets the government charge six times as much, 1.5%, which would be US$600,000. It has never done so: the rate has been 0.25% since the first period, 30 September 2019.[1][4] The tax is in force. The Seniat opened its filing period in October 2024,[6] and a tax adviser's guide of September 2026 treats the return as due this autumn.[7] No one has published what it raises.

The reason to tax these owners is the same everywhere. A fortune that grows is never counted as income, and its owner can borrow against it and spend without selling, so a wage earner is taxed more heavily than the holder of a fortune; the [pillar page](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#unrealised) follows that chain step by step. Andrés Eloy Méndez, who chaired the economy commission of the Constituent Assembly that made the law, put the idea in one line in 2019: whoever has the most pays the most, and whoever has the least pays nothing.[2] He also said the money should let Venezuela cut regressive taxes such as the 16% value added tax.[2]

The tax has two weak points: its rate sits at the floor, and the authority publishes no revenue. Both can be fixed without a new law, because the Executive sets the rate and the Seniat sets the forms. As of 14 November 2026 a tax reform has been promised for this year, with no text and no mention of this tax.[16]

## What the law says {#law}

The tax falls on individuals and companies that the Seniat has classed as special taxpayers and whose net wealth is 150 million tax units or more.[1] Net wealth is what a person owns on 30 September, minus debts and encumbrances.[1] Residents are taxed on assets at home and abroad, and non-residents on what they hold in Venezuela.[1] The tax cannot be deducted from income tax.[7]

The 150 million line is the corrected one. The text published on 3 July 2019 set two lines, 36 million tax units for individuals and 100 million for companies, which the press put at about EUR 250,000 and EUR 650,000.[2][3] The reprint of 16 August 2019 replaced both with a single line, 4.2 times the first for individuals and 1.5 times the first for companies.[1]

{{chart: units}}

The rate is set by the Executive, between 0.25% and 1.5%, and the Executive may set progressive scales by wealth.[1] The law's transitional provisions fix 0.25% until it does.[1][7] What counts is wide: real estate at home and abroad, shares, vehicles, yachts, aircraft, jewellery, art, gemstones and securities.[3][6] Listed shares count at their 30 September price.[5]

Some things stay out. The main home is exempt with no value limit, and so are household goods, labour benefits, farm assets in primary agriculture and an artist's own unsold work.[4][7] A family's house, furniture and wages are never touched.

## What the threshold means {#threshold}

In October 2023 the tax unit was Bs 9 by Mazars' count, which put 150 million units at Bs 1.35 billion, about US$40 million.[5] On 2 June 2025 the tax unit rose from Bs 9.80 to Bs 43, and the line stood at Bs 6.45 billion.[17][7] The law counts in tax units, so the line moves with the unit and follows prices.

At the 2023 dollar value, the lowest rate takes 0.25% of US$40 million, US$100,000 a year, and the ceiling of 1.5% would take US$600,000.[1][5] Nobody with a salary, a flat, a car and a shop gets near this. Méndez said when he presented the first text, with its lower lines, that it would cover about 2% of the companies that issue invoices, which hold close to 90% of the country's assets.[2] That is his claim, not a measured count, and the corrected line is higher, so the group is smaller and richer.

## Where it came from {#origin}

In January 2017 President Maduro proposed a 1% tax on large capital inside an income tax reform. On 7 September 2017 he told the Constituent Assembly that taxes on large fortunes would be created.[11] In June 2018 a paper by 25 authors recommended a tax on unproductive assets and on large fortunes.[15] The Assembly passed the law in one vote on 2 July 2019, without debating it article by article.[2] The Gaceta published it the next day.[3]

The aim was money outside oil. Méndez said the law would raise non-oil revenue to close the deficit.[2] The size of the pool is clear from the Seniat's own total: its domestic taxes brought in Bs 679 billion in September 2026, US$810.54 million, in one month.[10] A fortune tax draws a share of that money from the people with the most to give. The page on [what it would raise](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#revenue) shows why a small group can carry a large sum.

## Who is on the list {#list}

The tax follows the Seniat's list of special taxpayers. On 21 October 2026 acting president Delcy Rodríguez announced that the list had been cut from more than 132,000 names to 44,000. Small shops that never qualified came off, and large companies that had avoided the category went on.[9]

{{chart: list}}

Only a name on the list can owe the tax.[4] A shorter list aimed at the largest firms and fortunes suits a threshold of about US$40 million. It also means the list is now the tool: every large owner who belongs on it must be on it. The announcement did not mention the wealth tax.[9]

## Who backs it {#who-supports}

The tax was made by the movement in power. Besides [Méndez](/ve/A0002), the register lists [Orlando Camacho](/ve/A0008), the constituent who presented the law and said large fortunes exist in Venezuela and should contribute to the nation.[3] The first and second vice-presidents of the Assembly, [Tania Díaz](/ve/A0005) and [Gladys Requena](/ve/A0013), and its president, [Diosdado Cabello](/ve/A0006), signed the law as reprinted.[1] [Maduro](/ve/A0011) announced it in 2017.[11]

Behind the law stood economists of the same movement. The 2018 paper of 25 authors that recommended the tax names, among others, [Tony Boza](/ve/A0014), [Juan Carlos Valdez](/ve/A0015), [Luis Salas](/ve/A0016), [María Alejandra Díaz](/ve/A0017), [Mario Silva](/ve/A0018) and [Pasqualina Curcio](/ve/A0010).[15] They put their names to a recommendation, with no rate or threshold. The register grades each as a supporter on that basis, several at grade B, and counts none of them as a defender of the law as it stands. Curcio listed the tax on large fortunes again in November 2020, among measures for the National Assembly elected that year, and named Joseph Stiglitz and Thomas Piketty as economists who argue for taxes of this kind.[12]

One name has left the list. Deputy [Luis Eduardo Martínez](/ve/A0003) announced a wealth tax bill for Venezuelans abroad on 9 May 2023, mainly to register their assets.[13] On 1 June 2023 he told a fact-checker that the plan was dropped and that his group would not vote for a similar measure.[14] The register records that as a withdrawal, and so does this page.

## The objections, answered {#objections}

### "It will not raise much" {#revenue}

In 2017 the economist José Guerra said that taxes other than VAT and income tax are costly and low-yield.[11] The answer is the base. Méndez put 90% of the country's assets in the hands of 2% of invoicing companies,[2] and the law lets the rate rise from 0.25% to 1.5%.[1] At 0.25% an owner at the Bs 6.45 billion threshold pays Bs 16.1 million a year.[7] What the tax raised is unknown: the Seniat's notice on the 2024 filing gives no figure.[6] A tax should say each year how many filed, how many paid and how much came in. The silence is the weak point, and it is for the Seniat to end.

### "Seniat decides who is a target" {#selective}

Acceso a la Justicia argued in July 2019 that because the Seniat names the special taxpayers one by one, the tax could be aimed at political enemies, and that valuing assets in a hyperinflation would be arbitrary.[8] A rule written so that the name does not matter removes the first: every owner above 150 million units files, listed or not.[1] The October 2026 clean-up shows the list can be tightened to the very large.[9] On valuation the law takes the highest of the cadastral, market or updated purchase value,[5] and the Seniat has to publish the rule for updating purchase prices, which Mazars noted in October 2023 it had not yet done.[5] The page on [valuing wealth](/arguments/you-cannot-value-it) shows how other countries manage.

### "It came from the wrong assembly" {#assembly}

Critics note that the Constituent Assembly, not the National Assembly, passed the law.[8] A vote in the National Assembly settles that: the tax is judged by its threshold, its rate and its exemptions, which here are a very high line, a 0.25% rate and a protected home.[1][7]

### "It will scare off investment" {#investment}

The same 2019 criticism said the tax would be passed on to consumers and discourage investment.[8] A tax on net wealth is paid by the owner on assets held on 30 September. An owner at the threshold pays about US$100,000 on US$40 million.[5] The pages on [investment and growth](/arguments/hurts-growth) and on [owners who leave](/arguments/they-will-leave) answer the claim with figures from other countries, and an owner who leaves can still be charged on assets in Venezuela.[1]

## What a better Venezuelan wealth tax would do {#design}

1. **Publish the take.** Say each year how many filed, how many paid and how much was raised.[6]
2. **Use the rate band.** The law allows up to 1.5% and the rate has been 0.25% since 2019. The Executive can raise it without a new law.[1][4]
3. **Keep the line fixed in units.** Everyone above 150 million units files, whether or not the Seniat has listed them.[1]
4. **Publish the valuation rule.** The updated purchase price needs a rule.[5]
5. **Fund the swap.** Méndez named the goal himself: cut the value added tax as wealth taxes grow.[2] The [minimum tax](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#minimum) argument is the reason.
