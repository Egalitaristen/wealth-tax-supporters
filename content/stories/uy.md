Uruguay has a wealth tax that raised UYU 30.9 billion in 2024, about US$700 million, 0.95% of GDP, counting what public companies pay.[13] Companies paid almost all of it: UYU 29.05 billion. Individuals paid UYU 360 million, about US$8 million: companies paid more than 80 times as much.[13] The tax was built to reach the richest people and has been cut until it barely touches them. Dollar figures here use UYU 44.07 per dollar, the rate at the end of 2024.[9]

{{chart: who-pays}}

Why does that matter? Because the richest 1% hold about 37% of the country's wealth, and the poorest half hold 14.5%.[5] Their fortunes grow as shares, land and company profits, and none of that counts as income until it is sold. The [pillar page](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#unrealised) follows the money step by step. Counting every tax, the poorest half of Uruguayans pay 31%, the top 1% pay 23%.[5]

In 2025 the PIT-CNT, the national union confederation, proposed a surcharge of up to 1.5% on wealth above US$1 million.[3] An economist's brief in April 2026 adds a minimum tax of 2% on fortunes above US$100 million.[5] The government says not in this term.[8]

## A tax on its floor {#tax}

Título 14 of the tax code creates an annual tax on the net wealth of individuals, family units and estates, and of companies.[1] Individuals pay only on the part above a minimum that the government resets every year with the cost of living. For 2024 it was UYU 6,381,000, about US$145,000, and a family unit gets twice that.[1][2][9] Companies pay 1.5% and banks 2.8%. Farm assets pay a separate surcharge of 0.70% to 1.50%, by size.[1]

For individuals the law's scale runs from 0.70% on the first band to 2.75% on the last. The law cuts it every year from 2008, and from 2016 by 0.10 points a year in every band, with a floor of 0.10%. That floor is the rate in force.[1][3] Someone with UYU 40 million of taxable wealth, about US$910,000, owes 0.1% of what exceeds the minimum: UYU 34,000, or US$760.[1][2][9]

The individuals' tax now raises 0.01% of GDP, against 0.6% in the 1990s.[3] It falls mostly on real estate at cadastral values, which sit well below market prices.[3] That is the tax a surcharge would build on.

## Who sits at the top {#top}

About 25,000 adults, the richest 1%, hold between US$1 million and a little over US$1 billion each.[3] Their share of all wealth is 37% to 39% in tax records of 2016, and 37% in the April 2026 brief.[3][5] Two economists of the Universidad de la República put Uruguay's concentration with the United States, Colombia and Brazil, and far above the United Kingdom or France.[4]

The country has three billionaires with US$6.5 billion between them, and 40 people above US$100 million with US$14.9 billion.[5] Meanwhile 17.3% of people and 32.2% of children under six lived below the poverty line in 2024.[3]

## The union plan {#surcharge}

In November 2025 a commission convened by the PIT-CNT published its proposal: an annual surcharge on the existing personal wealth tax, on net wealth above US$1 million, the approximate threshold of the top 1%.[3] Business assets that already pay income or equity tax are left out, so the charge falls on passive wealth. The home keeps a deduction of 50% of its value, capped at a quarter of the minimum. A specialized unit of the tax office would audit the payers, and the proceeds would go to a state fund against child poverty.[3]

The commission gives three example scales, each applied band by band like the law's own, with bands from US$1 million to US$11 million.[3] The first charges 0.1% from US$1 to 2 million, 0.5% to 3 million, 1.0% to 6 million, 1.2% to 11 million and 1.5% above. The third charges 0.5% from US$1 to 2 million, 1.0% to 3 million and 1.5% above.[3]

{{chart: scales}}

At US$5 million the third scale takes 0.9% of the fortune. At US$20 million it takes US$270,000, 1.35%.[3] The [calculator](/calculator?w=5000000&b=1000000:0.5,2000000:1,3000000:1.5&c=US$) holds the third scale. An effective 1% arrives between US$6 and 14 million, inside the top 1%, and fortunes just above the line pay far less than those at the top.[3]

## What it would raise {#revenue}

The economists De Rosa and Vilá wrote to the PIT-CNT president on 2 July 2025. They put the wealth that a tax could reach at about GDP, some US$80 billion, after the minimum, exemptions and evasion of about 17% of the yield. A 1% tax on the top 1% would raise 0.75% to 1% of GDP, US$600 to 800 million.[4] That is 70 to 100 times what individuals pay now.[4][13] The [pillar page](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#revenue) sets out why a tax on the top slice raises real money, and the page on [whether it raises enough](/arguments/not-enough) answers the other side.

## A floor for the ultra-rich {#minimum}

An April 2026 brief by Vicente Silva of the International Tax Observatory, with the Friedrich Ebert Stiftung, adds a second tool.[5] A minimum tax charges the difference when the income and wealth taxes a fortune already pays fall below a floor. A rate of 2% on fortunes above US$100 million would raise about US$260 million, 0.4% of GDP. A rate of 3% would raise US$409 million, 0.65%.[5] Silva presents it as a partner to the surcharge: the surcharge widens the base, and the minimum covers the very top.[5] The pillar page shows [how a minimum tax is built](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#minimum).

## Leaks and answers {#objections}

Two gaps stand out. The first is a ten-year exemption on foreign assets and income for new residents who stay 90 days, which reaches about 2,300 people. Only 287 of them are inside the wealth tax's scope, and a 2025 law changed the conditions for entry.[5] The second is valuation: real estate is taxed at cadastral values, below market.[3]

The common objections are about evasion and flight. The economists reviewed the studies: people respond to a wealth tax mostly by changing what they declare, not what they do, and the effect on emigration in Scandinavia was slight.[4] Their yield estimate already counts 17% lost to evasion, and they flag foreign accounts as the part to watch.[4] The proposal sets up an audit unit for that.[3] The pages on [whether the rich will leave](/arguments/they-will-leave) and [growth](/arguments/hurts-growth) take up the evidence country by country.

## Where the government and the party stand {#politics}

The idea has been in the Frente Amplio for years. In March 2025 six senators, among them [Óscar Andrade](/uy/A0010) and [Alejandro Sánchez](/uy/A0011), now Secretary of the Presidency, reintroduced a bill from 2022 for a 25% surcharge on the wealth tax, to feed the national housing fund. In November 2023 Housing Minister Raúl Lozano had rejected it, citing the coalition's pledge of no new taxes.[6] The bill is in the Senate housing committee. Its last recorded hearing was on 9 September 2025, and it has had no vote (the register read the Parliament's record on 12 November 2026). The Frente Amplio programme, as Montevideo Portal quoted it in 2024, says the party will strengthen taxation of income, big capital and wealth.[10]

The executive has said no. On 10 June 2026 Economy Minister Gabriel Oddone repeated that the tax is not for this term.[8] President Yamandú Orsi said in August 2025 that programmes are guides.[11] The party has moved. On 18 October 2026 the press reported that the Frente Amplio congress approved a motion, negotiated among all its sectors, that values the PIT-CNT proposal as a contribution to tax justice and says the tax debate cannot be taboo for the left. It aims at agreements in 2027 to bring to the government.[7]

## Who supports it {#who-supports}

In August 2025 the Usina de Percepción Ciudadana ([poll page](/resources/uy-poll-usina-de-percepcion-2025)) found that 51% of Uruguayans would support an extraordinary tax on the sectors with the greatest wealth, and 77% of Frente Amplio voters.[14] Support also comes from the labour movement, the left of the Frente Amplio and the economists who built the numbers. The [PIT-CNT](/uy/A0001) and its president [Marcelo Abdala](/uy/A0002) lead the campaign. The [Partido Socialista](/uy/A0004) insisted at its 51st congress, and Senator [Gustavo González](/uy/A0005) promotes a bill for a tax on the richest 1%. He told the press that without money the programme cannot be carried out.[12] Communist deputy [Bruno Giometti](/uy/A0029) said in August 2025 that the programme does not allow doing nothing on taxes.[11] The [Frente Amplio](/uy/A0016) congress endorsed the debate.[7] On the evidence side, [Mauricio De Rosa](/uy/A0019) and [Joan Vilá](/uy/A0022) sit on the commission, and [Vicente Silva](/uy/A0026) wrote the brief on the minimum tax.[3][5]

## What Uruguay shows {#lessons}

### The machinery exists {#machinery}

Uruguay does not need a new tax. It has an annual wealth tax, a minimum with a family rule, a farm surcharge and a tax office that collects UYU 30.9 billion a year from it.[1][13] The tax office's [2024 statistics](/resources/uy-boletin-estadistico-2024) are on the Resources page. The economists say any new design should be built on the existing tax.[4] A surcharge above US$1 million does that and leaves the rate for everyone below it at 0.1%.[3]

### The rates fell by design {#floor}

Nobody repealed the tax. The law cut it year after year until individuals paid 0.10% and the tax raised 0.01% of GDP.[1][3] This site argues for reversing the cuts for the top 1%, where the tax once collected 0.6% of GDP.[3] The page on [Europe repealing the tax](/arguments/europe-repealed-it) shows what a full repeal cost elsewhere.

### The government is the obstacle {#obstacle}

University economists, a union confederation and the congress of the governing party all back the surcharge. Oddone's answer on 10 June 2026 was that the tax is not contemplated for this term.[8] The party's own 2027 timetable is the next date to push.[7]
