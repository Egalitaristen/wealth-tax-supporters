In 2019 Spaniards with more than €30 million of net wealth paid a wealth tax equal to 0.21% of it. Those with €3 million to €5 million paid 0.43%.[5] The top rate on the scale is 3.5%.[1] The richest paid the lowest share of any band above €3 million. The tax exempts business shares, caps the bill against income tax and lets regions rebate it, and the sections below show those three at work in the 2024 returns.

{{chart: effective-rates}}

Why tax wealth at all, when Spain taxes income? Because the largest fortunes are shares. Of every €100 of wealth declared in the 2024 returns, €68.70 was shares and holdings, and only €17.60 was property.[11] An owner's profit stays inside the company, so there is no income to tax until the owner chooses to sell or draw it out. The [pillar page](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#unrealised) follows the money step by step.

Spain has tried giving the tax away and taking it back. It gave everyone a 100% credit in 2008, brought the tax back in 2011, and in 2022 added a second tax that the regions cannot rebate.[3][4][2] In 2024 the two taxes raised €2.18 billion from 185,031 payers.[8]

## The wealth tax {#wealth-tax}

The Impuesto sobre el Patrimonio began in 1977 as an extraordinary tax and became a permanent one in 1991.[3] Each year you add up what you own at 31 December and subtract your debts.[1] A main home is exempt up to €300,000. So are pension rights, and so are business assets and shares in active companies, if you hold at least 5% of the company (20% with close relatives), manage it, and earn more than half of your work income from it.[1] Where a region sets nothing, the first €700,000 is untaxed and the rest meets a scale from 0.2% to 3.5%, the top rate applying to the part above €10.7 million.[1] Catalonia, for one, sets its own scale and a minimum of €500,000.[12]

One rule works against the tax. Wealth tax and income tax together cannot take more than 60% of a person's income-tax base, and the cap can cut the wealth tax by up to 80%.[1] A fortune that grows in shares produces little income, so this is the rule that protects it. The section on [valuation](#valuation) gives the 2024 sum.

In 2008 the government left the tax on the books but gave every taxpayer a 100% credit.[3] The 2011 decree that brought it back says the tax had come to hit mostly middle-sized fortunes. It restored the tax for 2011 and 2012, raised the minimum to €700,000 and set the main home exemption at €300,000, so that it reached what the decree calls a relatively small number of taxpayers with special economic capacity.[4] The page on [thresholds and spreading](/arguments/it-will-spread) has the payer counts before and after.

## The solidarity tax {#solidarity}

A law of 27 December 2022 created the solidarity tax.[2] It covers anyone with net wealth above €3 million and exempts the same things as the wealth tax. The base is net wealth less €700,000, and the first €3 million of base carries a 0% rate, so the first euro of tax falls at €3.7 million of net wealth. Above that the rates are 1.7%, 2.1% and 3.5%, the last above a base of €10.7 million. Wealth tax already paid comes off the bill.[2] The law gives two aims: to raise money from the largest fortunes in a year of energy and price crises, and to narrow the gap in burden between regions that cut the wealth tax and regions that did not.[2]

It was meant to last two years. A decree of 27 December 2023 extended it until wealth taxation is reviewed in the reform of regional financing, and it still applied to 2025.[2][12]

Regions that used to rebate the whole wealth tax had to change the rebate. Madrid, Andalusia, La Rioja and Murcia now rebate only the amount by which the wealth-tax bill exceeds the solidarity bill. Cantabria keeps a 100% rebate up to €3.7 million of net wealth and follows the same rule above it.[12] Here is what that does. Take two people with no exempt assets, and an income-tax cap that does not bite. At €20 million of net wealth a Madrid resident pays about €453,000 in all, and so does a Catalan: about €435,000 of Catalan wealth tax and €18,000 more on the solidarity return.[2][12] At €5 million the Madrid resident pays €22,100, which is 0.44% of their wealth, and the Catalan pays about €59,800.[2][12] Below about €17.5 million Madrid still charges less; above it the two bills are equal.

## What it raises {#revenue}

The first solidarity returns, for 2022, came from 12,010 payers, who paid €623 million. Madrid supplied 10,302 of them and €555 million.[6] Together with the wealth tax the two raised €1.88 billion for 2022, €1.96 billion for 2023 and €2.18 billion for 2024, an 11% rise on 2023.[10][9][8]

{{chart: revenue}}

In 2024 the tax agency counts 227,424 wealth-tax returns, of which 185,031 owed something. The €2.18 billion is the total on the wealth-tax returns and the separate solidarity returns together.[8] The wealth-tax returns alone show €2.15 billion payable, and the difference of about €24 million is what the separate solidarity returns add.[7][8] That split moved when the rebate rules changed: in Madrid, Andalusia, La Rioja and Murcia the wealth-tax bill is now cut only to the solidarity amount, so most of the solidarity tax is paid through the wealth-tax return.[12] It is why the €623 million of solidarity tax in 2022 cannot be set beside the 2024 split. The tax agency's figures cover Spain outside the Basque Country and Navarre, which collect their own.[11]

## The regions undercut each other {#competition}

Where a region rebates the tax, only people above the solidarity threshold pay anything. In 2024 Catalonia had 94,875 returns and 92,487 payers, who paid €768 million, an average of €8,307. Madrid had 25,524 returns and 11,448 payers, who paid €650 million, an average of €56,808. Madrid's payers were 6% of the country's and paid 30% of the money.[8] In a region that rebated the wealth tax, the tax on the largest fortunes still arrives.

Undercutting costs more than the tax itself. When the tax returned in 2011, every region except Madrid levied it. The moves of wealthy people to Madrid cost the regions that lost them about six times as much income-tax revenue as the direct wealth-tax loss, and Madrid itself could raise more by agreeing to a common positive rate.[22] A state floor answers a problem that regions cannot solve one by one. The page on [whether the rich will leave](/arguments/they-will-leave) takes up the wider claim.

## What Spain shows {#lessons}

### Valuation {#valuation}

The largest hole in Spain's tax is exemption. In 2024 €435 billion of the €987 billion declared, 44%, was exempt, and €374 billion of that was unlisted shares in exempt companies, 86% of all exempt wealth.[7] Alongside it sits the cap on wealth tax and income tax together. The scale added up to €6.23 billion in the 2024 returns. The cap cut that to €3.11 billion, a cut that fell on 27,627 filers, about €113,000 each. Regional rebates took off another €0.95 billion, and €2.15 billion was payable.[7] The cap and the rebates together took €4.1 billion off a €6.2 billion bill.

{{chart: scale-to-payable}}

The exemption has a history. The share of closely held stock that Spain's top 0.01% of wealth holders claimed as exempt went from 15% in 1994 to 77% in 2002, while the total they held stayed the same, and the page on [family firms](/arguments/family-firms) tells it in full. Catalan returns for 2011 to 2015 show the same thing after the tax came back. When the average rate rose by 0.1 percentage point, taxable wealth fell 3.2% over four years. Savings did not fall. Taxpayers moved money into exempt business assets and used the income-tax cap.[23] The page on [what a tax has to count](/arguments/you-cannot-value-it) explains how Spain values shares.

The fix is in the design. Sumar's 2026 bill drops the general exemption for business assets and shares.[15] Count the shares at their value and the biggest fortunes meet the scale like everyone else. The pillar page shows [how a minimum tax is built](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#minimum).

### Flight {#flight}

The Spanish evidence on moving is about moving between regions, and it points to the fix. With no exempt assets, a resident of Madrid and a resident of Catalonia owe the same from about €17.5 million of net wealth, and below that the Catalan pays more, as the €5 million case shows.[2][12] A floor that started lower would end the gain from moving for the rest. Before the solidarity tax, Madrid's rebate meant a resident above the threshold could pay nothing at all. In 2024 Madrid's 11,448 payers gave €650 million.[2][8]

### Revenue {#revenue-lesson}

Combined revenue rose each year from 2022 to 2024.[10][9][8] The scale on paper came to nearly three times the amount payable.[7] The page on [whether it raises enough](/arguments/not-enough) and the pillar page on [what it would raise](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#revenue) take up the size of the prize.

## The courts {#courts}

Madrid's government asked the Constitutional Court to strike the solidarity tax down. On 7 November 2023 the court dismissed the appeal in full. It rejected every claim, from the way the tax was added to a bill by amendment, through ability to pay, to legal certainty, and held that the tax does not interfere with the regions' powers over the wealth tax. Four of the eleven judges dissented.[5] On ability to pay the court looked at the tax a person actually pays as a share of net wealth, and not at the scale or at what some assets earn on the market, because the tax falls on holding assets, not on their return. It printed the effective rates in the chart above.[5] The page on [constitutional objections](/arguments/unconstitutional) sets the ruling beside the other countries' courts.

## The Basque Country and Navarre {#foral}

The Basque territories collect their own taxes, and the Basque agreement treats the solidarity tax as one they regulate themselves. Álava passed its version on 15 December 2023.[13] The tax agency's tables leave these territories out.[11]

## The political fight {#politics}

In June 2022 the Congreso refused to consider the Unidas Podemos bill for a tax on fortunes above €10 million, 58 votes for and 277 against.[14] The Socialist and Unidas Podemos groups then put the solidarity tax through as an amendment to another bill, and it passed that December.[5][2] The People's Party's 2023 programme promises to abolish the tax on large fortunes.[17]

The PP's tone has moved. At an Oxfam Intermón conference in Madrid on 25 May 2026 the Finance Committee spokespeople of the PP, PSOE and Sumar were reported by elDiario.es to agree that the very largest fortunes should be taxed. [Pedro Puy](/spain/A0112) of the PP said his party would support taxing the large fortunes created by technology and giant companies, and that other mechanisms could work better than the wealth tax.[18]

On 30 June 2025, at a conference in Seville, [Pedro Sánchez](/spain/A0062) asked for international taxation that guarantees fair taxation of large fortunes.[19] On 6 February 2026 Minister [Pablo Bustinduy](/spain/A0064) met the economist [Gabriel Zucman](/spain/A0084) in Paris to study a 2% annual tax on wealth above €100 million.[20] On 16 September 2026 [Sumar](/spain/A0061) filed a bill for a 3% yearly levy on net wealth above €50 million, with the money going to a child protection fund. The bill calls the levy a state payment of a non-tax kind, and it would apply with no general exemption for business assets. The Congress board admitted it on 22 September and sent it to the government. It was not debated.[15] The Cortes were dissolved on 5 October, and the bill lapsed with them. Spain votes on 29 November 2026.[16]

## Who supports it {#who-supports}

In August 2022, 71% of the 1,078 adults polled by Simple Lógica for elDiario.es backed a tax on fortunes above €10 million, and the Unidas Podemos bill was that tax. Among People's Party voters it was 49% in favour and 48% against.[21] The register lists the [Socialists](/spain/A0083), whose group introduced the solidarity tax, with [Sumar](/spain/A0061) and [Podemos](/spain/A0071). [Carlos Martín Urriza](/spain/A0110) leads Sumar on the Finance Committee and signed the 3% bill.[15] [Oxfam Intermón](/spain/A0067) published the May 2026 report that proposes a 3% minimum above €50 million.[24] The pillar page lists [who backs the tax around the world](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#who).
