Sweden is the case people cite when they say a wealth tax was tried and given up. The facts are narrower. The tax ran from 1911 and a new centre-right government abolished it with effect from 2007.[2][5] The government's reasons are on the record, and so are the exemptions and valuation rules that gave those reasons their force.[1][2] Those rules are the lesson. The Left Party and the Greens now propose a new tax built without them.[8][10]

## What the tax was {#tax}

Lag (1997:323) charged households a flat 1.5% on net wealth above a threshold. The last version set the threshold at SEK 1.5 million for a single person and SEK 3 million for couples taxed jointly.[1] Researchers who used the tax records describe the climb: SEK 800,000 in 1991, SEK 3 million for couples in 2006. In 1999 every household above the 92nd percentile of wealth paid. By 2006 a couple had to be in the top 2%, and a single person in the top 8%.[6]

A worked example from the law's own scale: a couple with taxable net wealth of SEK 5 million in 2006 paid 1.5% on SEK 2 million, SEK 30,000, or 0.6% of the fortune. A single person with the same wealth paid 1.5% on SEK 3.5 million, SEK 52,500.[1] The [calculator](/calculator?w=5000000&b=3000000:1.5&c=kr) gives the couple's figure.

Revenue was never large. Du Rietz and Henrekson, who wrote the history of the tax, find that it typically brought in 0.5% to 1% of central government tax revenue from the early 1970s to 2006. It averaged 0.2% of GDP after the war, never above 0.4%, and came to 0.16% of GDP in 2006.[5]

## Who paid {#payers}

The government expected about 267,000 people to pay for the 2007 income year. The 100 largest payers owed about SEK 400 million together, about 8% of the take.[2] The Social Democrats cite a count by the Riksdag's research service: 60% of the tax came from the top income decile, 63% from men, and 44% from Stockholm County, which has 20% of the population.[3] For households in the top 2% of wealth, the tax averaged about 0.5% of net wealth and made up about half of all their capital taxes.[6]

## Why the Alliance repealed it {#repeal}

The government's reasons are in proposition 2007/08:26. The tax hindered enterprise and the supply of risk capital. Its "oenhetliga värderingsregler och många undantag skapar kryphål och uppmuntrar till skatteplanering": uneven valuation rules and many exemptions that create loopholes and invite tax planning. Many owners of small houses had come to pay because assessed values rose fast. And capital was mobile: the Tax Agency had estimated that SEK 500 billion might be held outside the country, a figure the bill calls hard to pin down.[2]

The 2007 budget bill, the first of the Alliance government elected in 2006, said the tax should go during the term. A February 2007 memorandum proposed halving the rate to 0.75%. The spring bill then announced full abolition from 1 January 2007, and the government rejected a tax amnesty.[2] The law was applied for the last time at the 2007 assessment, for 2006.[2]

Most consultation bodies were positive or had no objection. LO, the trade union confederation, opposed the bill and wrote that it was better to remove the exemptions than to remove the tax.[2] We agree with LO.

## The vote {#vote}

The tax committee put the cost of the repeal at SEK 6.89 billion in 2007 and raised SEK 1.3 billion by capping the pension-saving deduction at SEK 12,000.[3] The Riksdag decided on 17 December 2007: 142 for the committee, all from the four Alliance parties, 101 for the Social Democrats' reservation, and 33 abstentions from the Left Party and the Greens.[4]

The Green reservation, by Helena Leander, concedes the weakness: "Den belastar bara vissa typer av tillgångar, och det är förhållandevis lätt att kringgå skatten genom att lägga sin förmögenhet i icke beskattningsbara tillgångar." It adds that the tax raised SEK 5 to 6 billion a year. The Left Party's Marie Engström wrote that the tax should stay, and that a review could try other methods for a more stable base.[3]

## What the design got wrong {#design}

The exemptions were written into the law. Listed shares counted at 80% of their quoted value, Swedish property at its assessed value and foreign property at 75% of market value. Pension insurance, personal chattels and pension savings accounts were outside the base. A main owner of a long-listed company, with a quarter of the votes, paid nothing on those shares.[1] Business assets were exempt, and unlisted shares were exempt in effect.[6][7]

The Equality Commission puts the result plainly: an exemption for unlisted shares "har dock en kraftigt regressiv effekt, då de största förmögenheterna i Sverige till stor del består av onoterade aktier."[7] The tax landed on bank accounts, listed funds and houses, and the owners of the largest private companies sat outside it.

The second fault was a cap. A separate law limited wealth tax and state income tax to 60% of income, with at least half of the wealth tax always due. It cut tax by about SEK 1.2 billion for about 19,000 people.[2] A tax on stock layered on a tax on income needs a limit, but a limit tied to income lets a rich household with little taxable income pay only half. The third fault was the threshold. By 2006 it had risen until only the top 2% of couples paid, which left a narrow base for every exemption to erode.[6]

## What happened after {#after}

Jakobsen, Kleven, Kolsrud, Landais and Muñoz used Swedish administrative data and found that the repeal cut the propensity of wealthy residents to leave by about 30%. About a third of departures among the top 2% before 2007 were caused by the tax. They also find the aggregate effect small: each point on the top rate shrinks the stock of wealthy taxpayers by about 2% and lowers value added by 0.10%.[6] Our reading is that a wealth tax moves some people and does not empty a country.

The Equality Commission reported in 2020. It found that private wealth in Sweden had doubled in 20 years and that 0.001% of the population owned 6% of it.[7] It called reintroduction desirable on distribution grounds, named the valuation of unlisted companies and cross-border moves as the two practical obstacles, and said the second had shrunk since the OECD and G20 work against tax avoidance.[7] Its assessment: "Återinförande av en förmögenhetsskatt bör utredas inom ramen för en större skattereform."[7]

## The case today {#today}

The Left Party moved a costed design in 2021: a household tax of 1% on net wealth from SEK 6 million to 10 million, 3% to 20 million and 5% above, worth SEK 10.9 billion a year from 2023.[8] It says in principle all assets belong in the base.[8] Its 2026 spring motion asks for an inquiry into a billionaire tax and notes that Sweden's property taxes bring in 1% of GDP, against about 2% across the OECD.[9] The Green motion of the same year asks for "en ny skatt för miljardärer" and cites Gabriel Zucman's minimum tax for the super-rich.[10]

Each lesson from 2007 points the same way. Tax all assets at market value, with no exemption for the largest holdings. Set the threshold at the top. Where valuation is hard, a minimum tax charges the difference between what a fortune already pays and a floor.[10]

## Who supports it {#who-supports}

The Left Party and the Green Party sponsor the proposals, Social Democratic districts and associations have asked for them, and economists have written them. The [Left Party](/se/A0001) and its leader [Nooshi Dadgostar](/se/A0016) carry the motions. The [Green Party](/se/A0002) and its spokesperson [Daniel Helldén](/se/A0033) moved theirs in April 2026. [Oxfam Sverige](/se/A0003) and the think tank [Arena Idé](/se/A0055) argue for a tax, and the [Equality Commission](/se/A0144) recommended a study.[7]

## What Sweden shows {#lessons}

### The flaws were in the law {#flaws}

Exemptions, soft valuation and a cap tied to income made the tax look unfair, the government's own charge in the bill.[2] We argue for a tax that has none of them.

### Repeal followed an election {#election}

The tax went because a new government wanted it gone and had the votes, 142 to 101.[4]
