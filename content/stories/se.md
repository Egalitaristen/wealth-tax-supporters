Sweden is the country people point to when they say a wealth tax was tried and failed. The record says something else. The tax ran from 1911, and in 2007 a new centre-right government deleted it instead of fixing it.[2][5] The holes were in the rules: generous exemptions and soft valuations that let the biggest fortunes pay little.[1][2] The Left Party and the Greens now propose a new tax built without those holes.[8][10]

## What the tax was {#tax}

Lag (1997:323) charged households a flat 1.5% on net wealth above a threshold. The last version set the threshold at SEK 1.5 million for a single person and SEK 3 million for couples taxed jointly.[1] The threshold climbed over the years: SEK 800,000 in 1991, SEK 3 million for couples in 2006. In 1999 every household above the 92nd percentile of wealth paid. By 2006 a couple had to be in the top 2%, and a single person in the top 8%.[6]

A couple with taxable net wealth of SEK 5 million in 2006 paid 1.5% on SEK 2 million, which is SEK 30,000, or 0.6% of the fortune. A single person with the same wealth paid 1.5% on SEK 3.5 million, SEK 52,500.[1] The [calculator](/calculator?w=5000000&b=3000000:1.5&c=kr) gives the couple's figure.

The tax never ran big. From the early 1970s to 2006 it brought in 0.5% to 1% of central government tax revenue. It averaged 0.2% of GDP after the war, never above 0.4%, and came to 0.16% of GDP in 2006.[5] That is a thin slice, and it was thin because the rules let wealth slip out of the base.

## Who paid {#payers}

The government expected about 267,000 people to pay for the 2007 income year. The 100 largest payers owed about SEK 400 million together, about 8% of the take.[2] A count by the Riksdag's research service, cited by the Social Democrats, found that 60% of the tax came from the top income tenth, 63% from men, and 44% from Stockholm County, which has 20% of the population.[3] For households in the top 2% of wealth, the tax averaged about 0.5% of net wealth and made up about half of all their capital taxes.[6]

## Why the government scrapped it {#repeal}

The government gave two reasons in its 2007 bill. The valuation rules were uneven and the exemptions many, which opened loopholes and invited tax planning. And money was mobile: the Tax Agency had estimated that SEK 500 billion might be held abroad, a figure the bill itself calls hard to pin down. It added that many owners of small houses had been pulled in because assessed values rose fast.[2]

Both were problems of rules. The government deleted the tax and left the loopholes. Its own trade union confederation, LO, said so: it opposed the bill and wrote that it was better to remove the exemptions than to remove the tax.[2] We agree with LO.

The 2007 budget bill, the first of the government elected in 2006, said the tax should go during the term. A February 2007 memo proposed halving the rate to 0.75%. The spring bill then announced full abolition from 1 January 2007, and the government turned down a tax amnesty.[2] The law was applied for the last time at the 2007 assessment, for 2006.[2]

## The vote {#vote}

The tax committee put the cost of the repeal at SEK 6.89 billion in 2007. It raised SEK 1.3 billion of that back by capping the deduction for private pension saving at SEK 12,000.[3] The Riksdag decided in December 2007, with the four governing parties voting for the repeal and the Social Democrats against.[4]

The Greens' reservation, by Helena Leander, said the tax hit only some kinds of assets and was fairly easy to avoid by parking wealth in assets that were not taxed. It added that the tax raised SEK 5 to 6 billion a year. The Left Party's Marie Engström wrote that the tax should stay, and that a review could look for methods that give a steadier base.[3]

## What the design got wrong {#design}

The exemptions were written into the law. Listed shares counted at 80% of their quoted value, Swedish property at its assessed value and foreign property at 75% of market value. Pension insurance, personal belongings and pension savings accounts were outside the base. A main owner of a long-listed company, holding a quarter of the votes, paid nothing on those shares.[1] Business assets were exempt, and unlisted shares were exempt in effect.[6][7]

The Equality Commission, a government inquiry, put the result in one line: because the biggest fortunes in Sweden are mostly unlisted shares, exempting them hits hardest the people at the very top.[7] The tax landed on bank accounts, listed funds and houses. The owners of the largest private companies sat outside it.

The second fault was a cap. A separate law limited wealth tax and state income tax to 60% of income, with at least half of the wealth tax always due. It cut tax by about SEK 1.2 billion for about 19,000 people.[2] A rich household with little taxable income could pay half. The third fault was the threshold. By 2006 it had risen until only the top 2% of couples paid, which left a narrow base for every exemption to erode.[6]

## What happened after {#after}

When the tax went, some rich Swedes who might have left stayed. Researchers using Swedish tax records found that repeal cut the chance that a wealthy resident would leave by about 30%. A third of departures among the top 2% before 2007 traced back to the tax.[6] That is a bill for the design. An exit tax on people who leave with big fortunes, and an end to the exemptions that made leaving worth it, would have met the problem head on. The same study finds the overall effect small: each percentage point on the top rate shrinks the stock of wealthy taxpayers by about 2% and lowers value added by 0.10%.[6] Against that, the state gave up SEK 6.89 billion in a single year.[3] A rich resident who stays and pays nothing adds little to the treasury.

The Equality Commission reported in 2020. It found that private wealth in Sweden had doubled in 20 years and that the richest 0.001% of the population owned 6% of it.[7] It called a return of the tax desirable on grounds of fairness, named the valuation of unlisted companies and cross-border moves as the two practical obstacles, and said the second had shrunk since the OECD and G20 work against tax avoidance.[7] Its conclusion: reintroduction should be studied as part of a larger tax reform.[7]

## The case today {#today}

The Left Party moved a costed design in 2021: a household tax of 1% on net wealth from SEK 6 million to 10 million, 3% to 20 million and 5% above, worth SEK 10.9 billion a year from 2023.[8] It says all assets belong in the base.[8] Its 2026 spring motion asks for an inquiry into a billionaire tax and notes that Sweden's property taxes bring in 1% of GDP, against about 2% across the OECD.[9] The Green motion of the same year asks for a new tax on billionaires and cites Gabriel Zucman's minimum tax for the super-rich.[10]

Each lesson from 2007 points the same way. Tax all assets at market value, with no exemption for the largest holdings. Set the threshold at the top. Where valuation is hard, a minimum tax charges the difference between what a fortune already pays and a floor.[10]

## Who supports it {#who-supports}

The Left Party and the Green Party sponsor the proposals, Social Democratic districts and associations have asked for them, and economists have written them. The [Left Party](/se/A0001) and its leader [Nooshi Dadgostar](/se/A0016) carry the motions. The [Green Party](/se/A0002) and its spokesperson [Daniel Helldén](/se/A0033) moved theirs in April 2026. [Oxfam Sverige](/se/A0003) and the think tank [Arena Idé](/se/A0055) argue for a tax, and the [Equality Commission](/se/A0144) recommended a study.[7]

## What Sweden shows {#lessons}

### The flaws were in the law {#flaws}

Exemptions, soft valuation and a cap tied to income made the tax look unfair, which was the government's own charge in the bill.[2] A tax without them answers the charge.

### Repeal followed an election {#election}

The tax went because a new government wanted it gone and had the votes.[4]
