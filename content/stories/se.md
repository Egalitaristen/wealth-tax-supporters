When Sweden scrapped its wealth tax in 2007, the state gave up SEK 6.89 billion of revenue.[3] The biggest fortunes in Sweden are shares in private companies, and the tax already exempted them.[1][7] Nineteen years later the Left Party counts about four dollar billionaires for every million Swedes, twice the American rate, and puts their fortunes at about 30% of GDP, up from under 10% in 2000.[9]

Why tax wealth at all? Because private wealth in Sweden had doubled in 20 years by 2020 and stood at its highest, against national income, in 200 years. The richest 0.001% of the population owns 6% of it.[7] Fortunes of that size grow inside companies, and a company that keeps its profit pays out no income for anyone to tax. The [pillar page](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#unrealised) follows that chain step by step.

The Swedish tax was thin because the law exempted so much: it brought in 0.16% of GDP in its last year.[5] The government of 2007 listed the holes in its own bill, then deleted the tax instead of closing them. The union confederation LO told the government to close the exemptions and keep the tax.[2] The Left Party and the Greens now propose a new tax built without the holes.[8][10]

## What the tax was {#tax}

Lag (1997:323) charged households a flat 1.5% on net wealth above a threshold. The last version set the threshold at SEK 1.5 million for a single person and SEK 3 million for couples taxed jointly.[1] Modern Swedish wealth taxation began in 1911.[5] The threshold climbed over the years, from SEK 800,000 in 1991 to SEK 3 million in 2006. In 1999 every household above the 92nd percentile of wealth paid. By 2006 a couple had to be in the top 2%, and a single person in the top 8%.[6]

A couple with taxable net wealth of SEK 5 million in 2006 paid 1.5% on SEK 2 million, which is SEK 30,000, or 0.6% of the fortune. A single person with the same wealth paid 1.5% on SEK 3.5 million, SEK 52,500.[1] The [calculator](/calculator?w=5000000&b=3000000:1.5&c=kr) gives the couple's figure.

For the households it reached, the tax counted. Households in the top 2% of wealth paid an average of about 0.5% of their net wealth each year, and that made up roughly half of all the taxes they paid on capital.[6] Yet from the early 1970s to 2006 it brought in only 0.5% to 1% of central government tax revenue. It averaged 0.2% of GDP after the war, never above 0.4%, and came to 0.16% in 2006.[5] The tax was thin because the rules let the largest holdings slip out of the base.

## Who paid {#payers}

The government expected about 267,000 people to pay for the 2007 income year. The 100 largest payers owed about SEK 400 million together, about 8% of the take.[2] A count by the Riksdag's research service, cited by the Social Democrats, found that 60% of the tax came from the top income tenth and 63% from men. Stockholm County has 20% of the population and paid 44% of the tax.[3] The tax reached the richer tenth, though the very top of that tenth sat largely outside it, as the next section shows.

## What the design got wrong {#design}

The exemptions were written into the law. Listed shares counted at 80% of their quoted value, Swedish property at its assessed value and foreign property at 75% of market value. Pension insurance and pension savings accounts were outside the base. A main owner of a long-listed company, holding a quarter of the votes, paid nothing on those shares.[1] Wealth held in a private business where the owner held more than 25% was fully exempt.[6]

The Equality Commission, a government inquiry, put the result in one line: because the biggest fortunes in Sweden are mostly unlisted shares, exempting them hits hardest the people at the very top.[7] The tax landed on bank accounts, listed funds and houses. The owners of the largest private companies sat outside it.

Valuation was not what broke the tax. Banks and other third parties reported most wealth components to the tax agency every year, so the base was easy to check.[6] The law left out the holdings that are hardest to value, private businesses. The page on [what the tax has to count](/arguments/you-cannot-value-it) shows how countries value them.

The second fault was a cap. A separate law limited wealth tax and state income tax to 60% of income, with at least half of the wealth tax always due. It cut tax by about SEK 1.2 billion for about 19,000 people.[2] A rich household with little taxable income could pay half. The third fault was the threshold. By 2006 it had risen until only the top 2% of couples paid, which left a narrow base for every exemption to erode.[6]

## Why the government scrapped it {#repeal}

The government gave two reasons in its 2007 bill. The valuation rules were uneven and the exemptions many, which opened loopholes and invited tax planning. And money was mobile: the Tax Agency had estimated that SEK 500 billion might be held abroad, a figure the bill itself calls hard to pin down. The bill added that many owners of small houses had been pulled in because assessed values rose fast.[2]

Both were problems of rules. The government deleted the tax and left the loopholes. LO said so: it opposed the bill and wrote that it was better to remove the exemptions than to remove the tax.[2] We agree with LO.

The 2007 budget bill, the first of the government elected in 2006, said the tax should go during the term. A February 2007 memo proposed halving the rate to 0.75%. The spring bill then announced full abolition from 1 January 2007, and the government turned down a tax amnesty.[2][3] The law was applied for the last time at the 2007 assessment, on wealth held at the end of 2006.[2]

## The vote and what it cost {#vote}

The tax committee put the cost of the repeal at SEK 6.89 billion of revenue. It won SEK 1.3 billion of that back by capping the deduction for private pension saving at SEK 12,000.[3] The Green Party's motion in the committee report said the tax raised SEK 5 billion to 6 billion a year.[3]

The Riksdag decided on 17 December 2007. The four governing parties voted for the repeal, 142 votes. The Social Democrats voted against, 101. The Left Party and the Greens abstained, 33 together.[4]

## What happened after {#after}

When the tax went, some rich Swedes who might have left stayed. Researchers using Swedish tax records found that repeal cut the chance that a wealthy resident would leave by 30%. A third of departures among the top 2% before 2007 traced back to the tax.[6] That cost came from the design. An exit tax on people who leave with big fortunes, and an end to the exemptions that made leaving worth it, meets the problem head on. The same study puts the drag on the whole economy from this migration at 0.10% of value added for each percentage point of tax on the top 2%.[6] Against that, the state gave up SEK 6.89 billion of revenue in one decision.[3] A rich resident who stays and pays nothing adds little to the treasury. The pages on [they will leave](/arguments/they-will-leave), [hurts growth](/arguments/hurts-growth) and [what happened where Europe repealed](/arguments/europe-repealed-it#sweden) lay out the evidence.

The Equality Commission reported in 2020. It called a return of the tax desirable on grounds of fairness, named the valuation of unlisted companies and cross-border moves as the two practical obstacles, and said the second had shrunk since the OECD and G20 work against tax avoidance.[7] Its conclusion: reintroduction should be studied as part of a larger tax reform.[7]

Since 2007 Sweden has not counted wealth at all. The Green motion of 2026 points out that there has been no wealth register since the tax went, so nobody knows exactly how many billionaires live in the country.[10]

## The case today {#today}

The Left Party moved a costed design in 2021: a household tax of 1% on net wealth from SEK 6 million to 10 million, 3% to 20 million and 5% above, worth SEK 10.9 billion a year from 2023.[8] It says all assets belong in the base.[8] Its 2026 spring motion asks for an inquiry into a billionaire tax and notes that Sweden's property taxes bring in 1% of GDP, against about 2% across the OECD.[9] The Green motion of the same year asks for a new tax on billionaires and cites the research service for revenue of as much as SEK 50 billion. It builds on Gabriel Zucman's minimum tax for the super-rich, which charges the difference when a fortune's income and capital taxes fall below a floor.[10] The [pillar page](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#minimum) explains how the minimum works.

Neither motion became law. The finance committee recommended rejecting both on 4 June 2026.[13] As of 14 November 2026 the Riksdag's open data shows no motion on a wealth or billionaire tax since the election. Sweden voted on 13 September 2026 and has not yet formed a government: Ulf Kristersson's caretaker government stays in office, and on 3 November the Riksdag rejected the first candidate for prime minister, Elisabeth Thand Ringqvist, by 324 votes to 25.[12] The Left Party holds 30 seats and the Greens 22.[12]

## Who supports it {#who-supports}

The Left Party and the Green Party sponsor the proposals. The [Left Party](/se/A0001) and its leader [Nooshi Dadgostar](/se/A0016) carry the motions. The [Green Party](/se/A0002) and its spokesperson [Daniel Helldén](/se/A0033) moved theirs in April 2026. [Oxfam Sverige](/se/A0003) and the think tank [Arena Idé](/se/A0055) argue for a tax, and the [Equality Commission](/se/A0144) recommended a study.[7]

In May 2026, 53% of Swedes said a billionaire tax is a good proposal, and 77% of the voters of the four opposition parties said so, in a Verian poll for Oxfam Sverige.[11] The poll has its own page: [Verian for Oxfam Sverige](/resources/se-poll-verian-2026).

## What Sweden shows {#lessons}

### The flaws were in the law {#flaws}

Exemptions, soft valuation and a cap tied to income made the tax look unfair, which was the government's own charge in the bill.[2] A tax without them answers the charge.

### Repeal followed an election {#election}

The tax went because a new government wanted it gone and had the votes.[4]

### What a new tax needs {#needs}

Each lesson from 2007 points the same way. Tax all assets at market value, with no exemption for the largest holdings, since that exemption is what made the old tax fall on the wrong people.[7] Set the threshold at the top. Where valuation is hard, a minimum tax charges the difference between what a fortune already pays and a floor.[10] Bring back a register of wealth, so the tax agency can see who owns what. Add an exit tax for people who leave with large fortunes. The pages on [owners with no cash](/arguments/no-cash) and [family firms](/arguments/family-firms) answer the objection that a tax on business wealth ruins the owners.
