India's wealth tax did not fail because wealth is hard to tax. It failed because the law left out almost everything the rich own. In 2015 the government scrapped it and did not fix it.[2][3] Since then the top 1% has come to hold 40% of the nation's wealth.[6] A lakh is 100,000 and a crore is 10 million, so ₹30 lakh is ₹3 million.

## What the tax charged {#tax}

The Wealth-tax Act of 1957 charged 1% a year on net wealth above a threshold. The threshold was ₹15 lakh and became ₹30 lakh from the 2010 assessment year.[1] The tax counted only a short list of assets: houses, motor cars, jewellery and bullion, yachts and aircraft, urban land, and cash above ₹50,000.[1] Shares, bank deposits and mutual funds were outside it. A committee led by Raja Chelliah had advised that in 1992, to push savings into "productive" assets.[3][4]

One house or a plot of up to 500 square metres was exempt, and so were commercial and rented property.[1][4] Here is a worked case from the Act's own scale. A family with ₹2 crore of listed assets owed 1% of ₹1.7 crore, which is ₹1.7 lakh. A family with ₹1,000 crore in shares owed nothing.

## What it raised, and why {#raised}

The government reported ₹788.67 crore in 2011-12 and ₹844.12 crore in 2012-13, from about 1.15 lakh assessees in 2011-12.[3] That is about ₹69,000 per payer in 2011-12. The Budget speech put the 2013-14 figure at ₹1,008 crore.[2] A parliamentary committee compared 2009-10 and 2010-11, ₹505 crore and ₹682 crore, with personal income tax of ₹1.32 lakh crore and ₹1.45 lakh crore, and called the yield "measly".[4]

Look at why. The base was narrow by law. The finance ministry's own memorandum says the listed assets, "jewellery, luxury cars", are "difficult to be tracked", which invited under-reporting and under-valuing, and that owners needed registered valuers for jewellery.[3] The ministry told the committee that a large number of assets were left out.[4] A tax built to skip shares will raise little. That is a drafting fault, and drafting can be changed.

In 2009 the ministry had itself proposed a wider tax: 0.25% on all assets, financial ones included, above ₹50 crore. By 2010 the Direct Taxes Code kept the narrow list at 1% above ₹1 crore, and the committee asked for slabs of 0.5%, 0.75% and 1% above ₹5 crore.[4] None of it passed.

The Budget speech said a tax "which leads to high cost of collection and a low yield" should go, but gave no figure for the cost.[2] The memorandum spoke of compliance and administrative burden, also without a number.[3] We found no published cost figure and make no claim about it.

## What repeal replaced it with {#repeal}

On 28 February 2015 the Finance Minister abolished the tax from assessment year 2016-17 and raised a surcharge on incomes above ₹1 crore, calling it an extra 2%. He expected about ₹9,000 crore against the ₹1,008 crore given up.[2][3] Asset details were to be moved into the income tax return.[2]

The swap changed what is taxed. A surcharge is a percentage of income tax paid on income above ₹1 crore.[2] It reaches pay, profits and dividends. It does not reach a fortune that grows in value and is never sold. The 2019 Budget raised the surcharge again for incomes of ₹2 crore to ₹5 crore and above ₹5 crore, to lift effective rates by about 3 and 7 points.[5] If the surcharge could do the job, it would not need raising.

## What happened to wealth since {#since}

The World Inequality Lab puts the top 1% at 40.1% of wealth in 2022-23, against 12.5% in 1980, and the top 0.001% at 17%. The rise since 2014-15 was sharpest at the top.[6] Oxfam India reported in January 2023 that the top 5% hold more than 60% of wealth and the bottom half about 3%, and that billionaires rose from 102 in 2020 to 166 in 2022.[7] The 2026 Wealth Tracker counts 1,688 people with ₹1,000 crore or more, holding ₹166 lakh crore, nearly half of GDP.[9]

## The proposals today {#today}

The Lab's 2024 package is a 2% annual tax on net wealth above ₹10 crore, about €1.2 million at market rates, plus a 33% inheritance tax on estates above that. It touches the top 0.04% of adults and would raise 2.7% of GDP. The top 0.04% hold wealth equal to 125% of GDP.[6] The Tax the Top campaign asks for 2% to 6% on the 1,688, plus a one-third inheritance tax, worth ₹10.63 lakh crore a year.[9] Five Left parties asked in February 2025 for a 4% tax on the 200 dollar billionaires.[8]

The Budget speech of 1 February 2026 does not mention wealth.[10] We checked it on 9 November 2026. No bill is pending, and the register has found no wealth tax motion in Parliament since 2015.

## What a modern tax would use {#modern}

The old tax relied on paper returns and valuers. India now has Aadhaar for an estimated 134.8 crore people, 94.6% of the population in June 2026.[11] CDSL alone holds over 17 crore demat accounts, 80% of the market, so listed shareholdings are on record.[12] The tax department's Annual Information Statement already gathers reported transactions against each PAN.[13] Land registries and GST filings can be linked in the same way. These records make a wide base with market values easy to build, which was the one thing the 1957 Act lacked.
