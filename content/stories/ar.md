In 2020 Argentina asked its richest people for a single payment: 2% to 3.5% of everything they owned, at home and abroad. About 10,000 people paid it, and it raised AR$247.5 billion within a year.[1][6] The money went to vaccines, small firms, student grants, housing in poor neighbourhoods and gas drilling.[1]

The yearly tax on the same fortunes tells the opposite story. Argentina has taxed personal assets since 1991, and a 2024 law cuts that tax every year: the top rate is 0.75% for 2026 and one flat 0.25% for 2027.[2][3] In 2024 the 515 returns with more than AR$5 billion of assets, about US$4.8 million, declared 82% of those assets abroad.[7][9]

{{chart: abroad}}

Why does that matter? Great fortunes grow as shares, land and deposits, and none of that growth counts as income until it is sold. The [pillar page](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#unrealised) follows the money step by step. A tax on income misses it, and a wealth tax that cannot see the assets abroad misses most of what the top holds. The 2020 levy could see them, and the yearly tax is being cut instead.

## A tax that was meant to last nine years {#bienes-personales}

Ley 23.966 created the Impuesto sobre los Bienes Personales in 1991 as an emergency tax for nine years. Congress has renewed it ever since, and it is in force today.[2] It counts what you own on 31 December. Residents pay on everything, at home and abroad. Non-residents pay on what they own in Argentina.[2] Farmland owned by individuals, national public bonds and bank deposits are exempt, and so is a main home worth up to AR$350 million.[2][3] The minimum for 2024 was AR$293 million, about US$280,000.[7][9]

The biggest payers are companies. They pay on their shareholders' behalf a flat 0.5% of the share value, and that payment is final.[2] In 2025 this channel brought in AR$1.2 trillion, 62% of the AR$2.0 trillion collected.[7]

{{chart: channels}}

## The 2024 cut {#reform-2024}

Ley 27.743, published on 8 July 2024 under the government of President Javier Milei, sets the scale for 2023 to 2027. The top rate falls from 1.5% for 2023 to 1.25%, then 1%, then 0.75% for 2026, and for 2027 one flat 0.25% applies above the minimum.[3] The tax stays on the books. It is shrinking by law, on a timetable that ends in 2027.

{{chart: top-rate}}

The law also sold a deal, the REIBP. A taxpayer prepays five years at once: 0.45% on five times the assets held on 31 December 2023 after the minimum, which is 2.25% of that base for 2023 to 2027. In return the taxpayer gets fiscal stability on wealth taxes until 2038, with the burden capped at 0.45% through 2027 (0.50% for those who also used the asset amnesty) and at 0.25% from 2028.[3] Anyone who adheres is shielded until 2038 from a new wealth tax or surcharge that would raise that burden. People who had left Argentina before the end of 2023 could rejoin as residents and take the deal.[3]

Take AR$900 million of assets above the minimum at the end of 2023, about US$1.1 million.[10] The 2023 scale charges AR$12.0 million a year. The REIBP charges AR$20.25 million for five years, AR$4.05 million a year. A flat 0.25% charges AR$2.25 million, 81% less than the 2023 scale.[3]

In 2024 the prepayment brought in AR$1.14 trillion, half of the AR$2.29 trillion collected under the tax. In 2025 the line for personal returns, advances and withholding fell 19% in pesos, from AR$483 billion to AR$392 billion.[7]

## The 2020 contribution {#aporte}

During the pandemic, Congress passed the Aporte Solidario y Extraordinario. It was charged once, on assets held when the law came into force, at home and abroad.[1] Anyone with AR$200 million or less owed nothing, about US$2.4 million.[1][8] Above that line the minimum no longer came off: the scale applied to the whole fortune, in bands from 2% up to 3.5% for the part above AR$3 billion.[1]

The law closed the doors before the rich could reach them. It valued assets under the Bienes Personales rules but kept that tax's exemptions out, so farmland, bonds and deposits counted. It counted shares and stakes in companies too. Assets held abroad faced a second table of 3% to 5.25%, unless the owner brought at least 30% of the value of the financial assets home within 60 days. Residence was fixed at 31 December 2019, a year before the law. The tax authority could undo changes made in the 180 days before the law if they looked designed to dodge it.[1]

A fortune of AR$1 billion in Argentina paid AR$24.75 million, 2.5%. A fortune of AR$201 million paid AR$4.02 million, 2%.[1] The tax office offered payment plans: 20% up front and up to five monthly payments, then 30% up front and two payments.[6] The [page on cash-poor owners](/arguments/no-cash) takes up the objection that the rich have assets but no money to pay.

## The vote {#vote}

The Cámara de Diputados passed the bill in the early hours of 18 November 2020, 133 votes to 115, after a 13-hour debate. The Senado followed on 4 December, 42 votes to 26.[19][5] [Carlos Heller](/ar/A0001), who co-wrote the bill, told the chamber that fewer than 9,300 people, 0.02% of the population, would pay, and that 252 of them would carry nearly half of the roughly AR$300 billion expected.[4]

The opposition called the levy confiscatory and said it doubled up on Bienes Personales.[4] It was one payment, charged once, on the top 0.02%. The page on [double taxation](/arguments/double-taxation) answers that objection, and the one on [constitutional limits](/arguments/unconstitutional) covers the court cases below.

## What it raised {#revenue}

By December 2021 the Ministry of Economy counted AR$247.5 billion from about 10,000 payers, all in the top income tenth of the country.[6] Heller had hoped for AR$300 billion.[4] The law sends 20% to health and vaccines, 20% to small and medium firms, 20% to Progresar student grants, 15% to housing in poor neighbourhoods and 25% to natural gas projects. By 20 December 2021 the government had spent AR$191 billion of it, 77%.[1][6] The [pillar page](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#revenue) sets out why a tax on the top slice raises real money.

## The courts {#courts}

Judges have split. In August 2022 a federal court in Corrientes struck the levy down for one taxpayer, whose income from his assets did not cover the bill.[14] In May 2024 the federal appeals court in Bahía Blanca upheld the law. The levy came to 2.19% of that taxpayer's assets, and the old rulings on confiscation were about taxes that recur every year, not a single payment.[13] In July 2026 one chamber of the federal administrative appeals court upheld it, because the taxpayer had not proved confiscation. On 3 September 2026 another struck it down for one taxpayer, two votes to one, and the tax authority can appeal.[16][15]

In July 2025 the Salta appeals court sent the Supreme Court the case Douthat c. AFIP-DGI, which challenges the levy.[12] Each ruling decides one taxpayer's case, and the Supreme Court has the last word.

## Who supports it {#who-supports}

In September 2020 the Analogías poll ([poll page](/resources/ar-poll-consultora-analogias-2020)) found 64% for the one-off levy and 28% against. 60% said the fortunes it targeted should keep paying every year.[20]

[Carlos Heller](/ar/A0001) wrote the bill and presented it to the chamber.[4] President [Alberto Fernández](/ar/A0207) promulgated the law, and Economy Minister [Martín Guzmán](/ar/A0005) countersigned it.[1] The economist [Claudio Lozano](/ar/A0031) wants a tax on everyone with wealth above US$1 million, at graduated rates. He puts the yield at US$4.6 billion, more than half from the 50 largest fortunes, falling on 0.07% of the population.[18] Any new tax has to reckon with the REIBP, which shields those who adhered until 2038.[3] The [Instituto de Pensamiento y Políticas Públicas](/ar/A0004) proposed in 2024 a surcharge on Bienes Personales for the 50 largest fortunes and puts the yield at US$2 billion.[17]

## What Argentina shows {#lessons}

### Valuation {#valuation}

The 2024 returns list AR$95 trillion of assets.[7] The standing tax leaves out farmland, bonds and deposits, and it takes shares at a flat 0.5% paid by the company, so the owner of a small stake and the owner of a conglomerate pay the same rate.[2] The 2020 levy counted all of it, which is why it caught fortunes the yearly tax misses.[1] The fix is a shorter list of exemptions and a progressive scale on shares.

### Money abroad {#abroad}

A quarter of declared assets, AR$24 trillion or about US$23 billion, are held outside Argentina.[7][9] Among the 515 returns above AR$5 billion the share is 82%. Those returns are fewer than one in a thousand of 655,729, and they carry 32% of all tax determined: AR$109 billion of AR$343 billion.[7]

Argentina already has the tools. The 2020 law taxed foreign assets at up to 5.25% unless 30% came home.[1] The 2024 amnesty brought US$31 billion into view, declared by more than 278,000 taxpayers after two of its three stages. The first US$100,000 was free, and the rate above it was 10% in stage 2 and 15% in stage 3.[11] A country that can make US$31 billion visible can tax it at the same rate as money at home. We argue for that, and against an amnesty after a levy.

### Flight {#flight}

The 2020 law shut the exits in its own text, with residence fixed in 2019 and the 180-day look-back.[1] The 2024 REIBP went the other way and let people who had left before the end of 2023 rejoin as residents.[3] A rich resident who stays and pays nothing adds nothing to the budget. The page on [whether the rich will leave](/arguments/they-will-leave) takes up the evidence.

### Revenue {#revenue-lesson}

A one-off levy raised AR$247.5 billion in a year.[6] The standing tax raised AR$2.0 trillion in 2025. The share paid by companies for their owners grew from AR$455 billion in 2024 to AR$1.2 trillion while the personal line fell 19%.[7] The page on [whether a wealth tax raises enough](/arguments/not-enough) answers the other side, and the one on [growth](/arguments/hurts-growth) covers the cost to the economy.
