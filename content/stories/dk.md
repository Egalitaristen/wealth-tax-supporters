Denmark taxed net wealth for 94 years and ended the tax by choice.[5] On 15 December 1995 the Folketing voted 113 to 16 to cut the rate to 0.7% for 1996 and to set it to zero from 1997.[1][4] The government said the latest tax reform now taxed capital gains, so wealth no longer needed a residual tax.[1] Mogens Lykketoft, who negotiated the deal for the government, says the repeal was the price of Conservative votes on the budget.[10] About 35,000 families paid the tax in its last year, and it raised about 760 million kr.[1] In 2026 three parties proposed a new one. On 8 November 2026 no bill or motion for it sits in the Folketing.[16]

## What it charged and who paid {#tax}

The state tax act of 1903 created an annual wealth tax beside the income tax. It began at 0.06% of taxable wealth with no general allowance, and by 1922 the scale reached 1.6% on wealth above 1 million kr.[5] From 1980 the rate was 2.2% above 1 million kr. The cuts started in 1989 and took the rate to 1% by 1991.[5] The base was a household's net wealth outside pensions: deposits, bonds, shares, housing, large durables and business assets, less debt. The allowance sat above the 97th percentile of households.[6]

Take a single person with 10 million kr. Under the 1995 rule, 1% above 1,635,500 kr., the bill was about 84,000 kr. Under the law passed in December, 0.7% above 2.4 million kr., it was about 53,000 kr.[1]

The tax fell on a thin top. For 1996 the tax minister, Carsten Koch, gave the Skatteudvalg a table of the 35,000 families by wealth. The 11,600 single people with 1.6 to 2.4 million kr. paid 3,050 kr. on average. The 3,700 families above 10 million kr. paid 101,700 kr. on average, about 380 million kr. in all, half of the yield. The 10,500 families above 4.8 million kr. paid about three quarters.[2] The government's own table by occupation shows 7,500 self-employed families and 9,400 major shareholders, who got 450 million of the 760 million kr. of relief, and 10,600 pensioners and 7,500 others.[1]

## Hollowed out before it went {#hollowed}

The wealth tax was worn down in the years before anyone repealed it. From 1980 business assets counted at a discount that rose from 20% to 40% by 1986, and forests counted at a fifth of their value.[5] A ceiling on the combined income and wealth tax let relief reach the wealth tax itself.[5] From 1989 major shareholders took 60% off the part of the tax that came from their shares.[5] Unlisted shares were valued by a formula, the skattekurs, and Tax Minister Jeppe Bruus said in 2022 that in most cases the real value was above the taxed value: "i langt de fleste tilfælde var aktiernes reelle værdi større end den, der udgjorde grundlaget for formueskatten."[8]

Lykketoft names the cause. The tax had been "udhulet af vidtgående rabatregler" and held down by a ceiling, until in the end only "meget samfundsbevidste rigmænd og formueejere med dårlige skatterådgivere" paid much.[10] By 1995 the Danish tax had lost much of its base to the reliefs.

## The deal of December 1995 {#repeal}

The government parties, Socialdemokratiet, Det Radikale Venstre and Centrum-Demokraterne, agreed with the Conservatives that the tax would be phased down in 1996 and abolished from 1997, as part of the budget deal for 1996.[1] The bill says "der ikke er det samme behov for formueskatten nu som tidligere, hvor formueskatten fungerede som en slags residualskat". It adds that future saving would come mainly from taxed money.[1]

The Social Democrats did not claim the repeal. Their spokesperson, Jacob Buksti, said that it was "en blomst, der ikke er vokset i det socialdemokratiske rosenbed" and that the party could live with it as part of the bundle.[3] Lykketoft, writing in 2021, agrees: "Det var bestemt ikke mit ønske, men en pris til de Konservative for et i øvrigt solidt S-præget finanslovforlig."[10]

Venstre, the Conservatives, Centrum-Demokraterne, Fremskridtspartiet and Dansk Folkeparti cheered. Peter Brixtofte of Venstre called the repeal a first step toward removing other "misundelsesskatter", and Flemming Hansen of the Conservatives said he had spoken for repeal many times and now saw it succeed.[3] The Radikale spokesperson, Elisabeth Arnold, said her party would have kept the tax and had "meget lange udsigter" of ever bringing it back.[3] The bill passed 113 to 16. SF, Enhedslisten and one Social Democrat voted against.[4] Kristen Touborg of SF called it "dette asociale lovforslag."[3]

The bill gives no finding that the tax had failed. Its reason is the new treatment of gains, and it puts the yield at 800 million kr. gross for 1996 and about 760 million net.[1]

## The tax returns as a proposal {#proposals}

In January 2022 three members from Frie Grønne put B 70 to the Folketing: a marginal scale from 1% on net wealth between 3.5 and 10 million kr. up to 20% above 1 billion kr., with a base that includes unlisted shares and pensions.[7] A fortune of 100 million kr. would owe about 3.4 million kr.[7] The [calculator](/calculator?w=100000000&b=3500000:1,10000000:2,50000000:5,100000000:10,1000000000:20&c=kr) holds the scale. Bruus rejected it for the government. The Folketing voted it down on 7 June 2022, ten for (Enhedslisten, Frie Grønne, Alternativet) and 86 against.[9] SF said it shared the intention and found the rates too high. It had proposed 0.5% above 5 million kr.[8]

On 26 February 2026 Mette Frederiksen proposed 0.5% of wealth above 25 million kr. per person, with a deduction for home equity up to 10 million kr., on 22,000 people and 6 to 7 billion kr. a year for smaller school classes.[11] She said: "Der er sket en koncentration af formuer, der skævvrider Danmark."[11] SF proposed 0.5% above 10 million kr. and Enhedslisten 1% above 35 million.[12]

## Where the Folketing stands on 8 November 2026 {#status}

The vote in March 2026 cost the parties that backed a wealth tax six seats, from 80 to 74.[13] The new government of Socialdemokratiet, SF, Moderaterne and Radikale Venstre published its platform on 2 June 2026. It does not mention a wealth tax, and Moderaterne and Radikale Venstre had opposed the Social Democratic proposal.[14] Finance Minister Peter Hummelgaard said on 9 July that the idea lives on in the government's plans to look at inheritance and housing sales.[15]

On 8 November 2026 I read every title on the Folketingstidende lists of motions and bills for the sessions of 2025-26 and 2026-27. None mentions a wealth tax.[16]

## What the economists find {#economists}

Katrine Jakobsen, Kristian Jakobsen, Henrik Kleven and Gabriel Zucman used Danish tax records, and their paper calls the Danish tax the largest of its kind. At an assumed return of 4.4%, its 2.2% rate took half of the return.[6] They find clear effects on wealth accumulation in the short and medium run, larger among the very wealthy. They report very little bunching at the threshold, which they read as modest evasion, and add that bunching cannot rule out larger avoidance.[6] They could not study the abolition itself: after 1997 Statistics Denmark stopped recording purely self-reported wealth.[6] Repeal broke the data series too.

A study of the Danish repeal of 1997 and the Swedish one of 2007, reported by TjekDet in March 2026, finds that each percentage point of tax moves about 2% of the taxed wealthy abroad. Jonas Kolsrud says the effect on investment is "stort set ingenting."[17] Katrine Jakobsen says the design and the valuation of unlisted firms decide the outcome.[17] Both points concern design, and the Danish record shows where design went wrong: the reliefs and the valuation of unlisted shares.

## Who supports it {#who-supports}

Support in 2026 sits with the parties that proposed it: [Socialdemokratiet](/dk/A0002), [SF](/dk/A0003) and [Enhedslisten](/dk/A0001). [Frie Grønne](/dk/A0004) wrote B 70. [Mette Frederiksen](/dk/A0008) announced the Social Democratic tax and [Pia Olsen Dyhr](/dk/A0009) of SF called it "rystende" that Denmark has no wealth tax.[14] [Mogens Lykketoft](/dk/A0043), who negotiated the repeal, argues for a new tax with no rebates or ceilings, at 1% above 7.5 million kr.[10]

## What Denmark shows {#lessons}

### The repeal was a bargain {#bargain}

Denmark did not conclude that wealth taxes fail. A minority government traded the tax for budget votes, and its negotiator says it was not his wish.[3][10] Anyone who cites Denmark as proof that a wealth tax cannot work has to explain why the record says otherwise.

### The base decides {#base}

The old tax raised 760 million kr. from 35,000 families on a base cut by reliefs, a ceiling, a major-shareholder discount and low values on unlisted shares.[1][5][8] B 70 writes the opposite rule into the text: unlisted shares and co-operative housing counted, pensions corrected for deferred tax.[7]

### The majority is missing {#majority}

The parties that backed the tax in some form hold 74 seats.[13] The government's answer is a higher inheritance tax and a commission on housing sales.[14][15]

### The neighbours {#neighbours}

[Norway](/no) taxes wealth today, and [Switzerland](/ch) and [Spain](/es) do too.
