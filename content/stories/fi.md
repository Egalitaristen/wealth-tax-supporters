Finland did not drop its wealth tax because it failed. The government that deleted it wrote down why: the base had holes, and rich owners planned around them.[1][2] A tax that is easy to dodge needs closing, not burying. In 2003 just under 40,000 people paid it, and half of the money came from only 2,700 of them.[1] In 2005 Finland buried it, and the richest tenth now owns more than half of the country's net wealth.[7]

## What the tax was {#tax}

The tax fell on what a person owned at the end of the year, minus debts. The rate was 0.9% from 1989 and 0.8% from 2005.[1] The Left Alliance's later bill copies the last scale: nothing below EUR 185,000, then EUR 80 plus 0.8% of the part above.[4]

In 2003 just under 40,000 people paid, just under EUR 120 million in all. Half of it came from only 2,700 payers.[1] The government expected EUR 70 million for 2005.[2] The money went to the state.[1]

## The holes {#holes}

The government's own bill lists them. Bank deposits and bonds were outside the base. So were savings and pension insurance. Listed shares counted at 70% of market value. Businesses, farms and forests were deliberately valued low.[1] The bill says owners moved taxable investments into untaxed ones around New Year, and that tax planning was normal.[1]

These are drafting choices. The committee said the same: the tax fell on people by chance, depending on what they owned and how well they planned.[2] That is an argument for a wider base.

## How it was scrapped {#repeal}

The immediate trigger was a change at the start of 2005. Dividends were taxed more heavily, which raised the tax on share ownership. The government argued that shares should be taxed on their returns instead.[1][2] It added that property was already under the property tax, and that Finland was one of few countries still using the tax.[1] The committee said repeal had been agreed in the 2004 incomes-policy deal and would support enterprise, growth and jobs.[2]

The Left Alliance members of the committee, Iivo Polvi and Kari Uotila, moved to reject the whole package. Their statement said repeal freed a few tens of thousands of the richest from a tax ordinary earners never paid, and cost EUR 130 million.[2] Parliament passed the laws anyway on 13 December 2005. Tax year 2005 was the last.[1][3]

## What repeal cost {#cost}

The state gave up the tax. The bill's own figure for the lost revenue is EUR 70 million a year.[1] The promised payoff was a hope: growth and jobs would make up the money.[2]

The same reform that raised the tax on shares also cut the capital income tax rate for everything outside the wealth tax's reach, as the committee itself noted.[2] So the government answered a problem in one corner of the base by dropping the tax on the whole base. Today capital income pays 30% up to EUR 30,000 and 34% above, and a listed-company dividend is only 85% taxable.[8] Wealth that grows without being paid out is taxed by nobody. That is the gap a wealth tax fills.

The government also said the tax was rare abroad.[1] A rare tax is not a bad one. Austria, Denmark and the Netherlands had dropped theirs, and Germany's court had ordered unequal treatment of asset types fixed.[1] Each case was about holes in the base. A wealth tax with one rule for all assets answers all of them, and a rich resident who stays and pays nothing helps no one.

## Wealth since 2005 {#after}

Statistics Finland's survey of 2023 found the richest tenth of households owning 52% of net wealth, 9.3 points more than in 2004. The richest 5% own 37%. The poorest half owns 4%, and the poorest tenth is in debt.[7] The typical household has EUR 96,000, 21% less in real terms than in 2019.[7] Finland scrapped the one tax aimed at the top end, and the top end has pulled further ahead since.

## Proposals since {#proposals}

The Left Alliance tabled the old tax again in 2006.[4] The Finns Party followed. In 2012 thirty-eight of its MPs, including Timo Soini and Jussi Halla-aho, signed a bill for 0.8% above EUR 300,000, worth about EUR 135 million in 2013.[5] A person with EUR 2 million would have paid EUR 13,600.

In 2022 the Left Alliance moved to prepare a millionaire tax: 0.5% above EUR 1 million, the home excluded, over EUR 500 million a year by the Parliament information service's count. It asks for a base without the old holes: deposits, bonds and savings insurance in, no favour for any ownership form.[6]

## Who supports it {#who-supports}

The [Left Alliance](/fi/A0055) leads. The [Green League](/fi/A0064) adopted a permanent wealth tax in its 2026 programme. The unions [SAK](/fi/A0051) and [STTK](/fi/A0063) back a wealth tax, and economists such as Matti Tuomala have recommended one.

## Where parliament stands {#today}

As of 9 November 2026 no bill, motion or budget motion filed in Parliament in 2026 proposes a wealth tax. The most recent is the 2022 motion.[6] The case is open for a new filing.

## What a Finnish wealth tax would look like {#design}

Tax all assets at market value: deposits, bonds, savings insurance and shares. Set the threshold at EUR 1 million so only the top few percent pay. Treat companies and sole traders the same. Add an exit tax so that leaving does not end the bill. That is the tax the 2022 motion describes, and it does not need the holes the 2005 government used as an excuse.[6]
