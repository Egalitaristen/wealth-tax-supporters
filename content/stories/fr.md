France replaced its wealth tax with a tax on real estate in 2018, and the state's own evaluators say the swap now costs more than €4 billion a year.[2] About 360,000 households paid the old tax, the ISF, in 2017 and it raised €4.2 billion.[2] Two thirds of them owed nothing in 2018, and the average bill of all former payers fell from €8,000 to €1,600.[2] The real-estate tax, the IFI, raised €1.83 billion in 2022.[2]

{{chart: collected}}

Why does it matter that the new tax only reaches real estate? The largest fortunes are shares in companies, not buildings. A share that doubles, a company that keeps its profit: none of it counts as income until the owner sells, and the owner can borrow against it meanwhile. The [pillar page](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#unrealised) follows the money step by step. A tax on real estate alone leaves exactly that wealth out. No tax in France sets a minimum on the largest fortunes. As of 14 November 2026 no tax on net wealth and no floor tax on fortunes is in force.

## What the ISF was {#isf}

The tax on large fortunes was created by the finance law of 30 December 1981 and abolished by a law of 11 July 1986. The finance law for 1989 brought it back as the solidarity tax on wealth, the ISF, to pay for the minimum income.[5] Its last scale, set in 2013, charged net taxable wealth in six bands:[1]

- Up to €800,000: nothing.
- €800,000 to €1.3 million: 0.5%.
- €1.3 million to €2.57 million: 0.7%.
- €2.57 million to €5 million: 1%.
- €5 million to €10 million: 1.25%.
- Above €10 million: 1.5%.

Households owed the tax once they passed €1.3 million, and a discount smoothed the step just above it.[1] Take €6 million of taxable wealth. The bands owe €2,500, €8,890, €24,300 and €12,500, about €48,200 or 0.8% of the fortune. The [calculator](/calculator?w=6000000&b=800000:0.5,1300000:0.7,2570000:1,5000000:1.25,10000000:1.5&c=%E2%82%AC) holds this scale. If that €6 million is shares in a company, the same household owes nothing under the IFI.[3][4]

The rate on paper was 1.5%. The richest paid far less, and the reason is a cap. ISF, income tax and social charges together could not take more than 75% of the previous year's declared income.[2] Wealthy households learned to declare little income. Of those with €100 million to €200 million of taxable wealth, 84% used the cap, and their declared income was 0.2% of their wealth. The richest 0.001% paid 0.1% of their wealth in 2017, against 1.5% on the books.[2] The cap applied to fewer than 12,000 households. Its real cost was about €2 billion a year, twice the tax administration's own estimate, because capped households declared even less income.[2]

{{chart: rate}}

The old tax failed at the top, and that is what a floor tax is built to fix. The [pillar page](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#minimum) explains how.

## What the 2018 swap cost {#ifi}

Why did the government drop the ISF? One aim, the evaluation committee notes, was to move investment from property into company shares.[2] From 1 January 2018 the IFI replaced the ISF. It taxes net real estate above €1.3 million on the same scale, and the cap still applies.[3][4] Two thirds of the households that paid the ISF in 2017 were out of the IFI in 2018. The biggest cut in rate went to households around the 99th percentile, about 0.5% of their wealth.[2]

The IFI raised €1.29 billion in 2018, against €4.2 billion for the ISF the year before, and €1.83 billion from 164,000 households in 2022.[2] The committee estimates the loss at €3.1 billion in 2018, because the IFI took in only 29% of what the ISF would have. If the ISF had grown as the IFI did, the loss in 2022 would be €4.5 billion. The committee calls the cost probably more than €4 billion.[2] The gap widens every year, because the fortunes left out of the base keep growing.

{{chart: lost}}

Did the cut send money into the economy? The committee finds no lasting shift out of property. The share of former ISF households selling more than €1 million of real estate rose to 14% in 2018 from 6% to 7% in earlier years, then fell back, and in 2019 to 2021 sales stayed below purchases.[2] On firms, the committee found it hard to show any effect of the ISF on dividends or investment when it compared companies whose owners gained most from the repeal with those whose owners gained little.[2] What the swap did was shrink the tax. The [page on property taxes](/arguments/we-already-tax-property) and the page on [Europe repealing the tax](/arguments/europe-repealed-it#france) set France beside the other repeals.

## The exit argument {#exit}

Opponents say the rich leave. Under the ISF, 1,150 payers left France in 2014 and 760 in 2017, out of about 360,000 payers. That is 0.2%.[1][2] After the swap, 260 payers a year left between 2018 and 2021, against 380 who returned, out of roughly 150,000 payers. That is again about 0.2%.[2] Between 2011 and 2016, an average of 950 ISF payers left a year and 370 came back.[2] The rate of leaving stayed where it was, and the tax follows the households that stay.

The sums are small too. Households that came back in 2021 paid about €5.5 million of IFI in 2022, 0.3% of the total, and those who left in 2021 had paid €3.8 million of it in 2019. The committee calls the budget stake probably secondary.[2] It also reports a study suggesting that firms whose reference shareholder leaves France do worse afterwards.[2] Behind that finding are a few hundred households a year, and the answer to a few hundred households is a rule that applies to them on the way out. The October 2026 Socialist amendment has one. Anyone who lived in France for six of the ten years before leaving owes, at the moment of leaving, ten times the annual floor rate, payable over up to ten years.[14] The pages on [whether the rich will leave](/arguments/they-will-leave) and on [Europe repealing the tax](/arguments/europe-repealed-it) take up the wider claim.

## The holdings tax of 2026 {#holdings}

The government did try something in the 2026 budget. Article 7 of the finance law of 19 February 2026 sets a tax on the assets of holding companies that are not used in an operating business, due for financial years closed from 31 December 2026.[17][19] The Conseil constitutionnel reviewed the law on 19 February 2026 and did not strike the article down.[17] The tax falls on companies, not on households. It asks nothing of a household whose wealth sits outside a holding, and it sets no minimum on what a fortune pays. The floor tax counts the whole net wealth of the household.

## The taxe Zucman {#zucman}

Bill no. 768 of 7 January 2025, by [Eva Sas](/fr/A0200), [Clémentine Autain](/fr/A0049) and the Écologiste et social group, creates a minimum tax for households with net wealth above €100 million. Its statement of reasons cites the economist [Gabriel Zucman](/fr/A0229) for a yield of €15 to 25 billion a year.[6] The October 2026 amendment puts the number of households at about 1,800.[14] For scale, the committee puts the yearly loss from the 2018 swap at more than €4 billion.[2]

The tax charges only the difference. It takes 2% of net wealth and subtracts the income tax, social contributions, IFI and property taxes already paid.[14] A fortune of €500 million that pays €5 million in those taxes owes €10 million at the floor, minus €5 million: €5 million. Households that already pay 2% owe nothing. The amendment counts professional assets, so a holding company or a pile of shares does not take a fortune out of reach.[14]

What happened to it:

- February 2025: the Assemblée adopted the bill, 116 to 39 with 31 abstentions.[7]
- June 2025: the Sénat rejected it, 129 to 188.[8]
- Autumn 2025: the Assemblée rejected the same tax as an amendment to the 2026 budget on 31 October, 172 to 228, and the Sénat rejected it on 28 November.[9][10]
- October 2026: the Assemblée rejected three versions of it as amendments to the 2027 budget. Each got 35 votes in favour, against 70, 68 and 70.[11][12][13][20]

No court has ruled on the tax, because no text has been enacted. The [page on constitutional challenges](/arguments/unconstitutional) puts France beside the other countries whose courts have ruled on wealth taxes. Seven Nobel laureates in economics are named in the October 2026 amendment as backing the tax, and the [page on the laureates](/arguments/nobel-laureates) carries their case.[14]

## Where it stands on 14 November 2026 {#now}

The 2027 budget bill was filed on 1 October 2026.[16] The Socialist, Insoumis and Écologiste groups each filed a floor-tax amendment, and each was voted down on 16 October.[11][12][13] On 4 November the Assemblée rejected the first part of the bill, 0 for and 458 against with 2 abstentions, and under article 119 of its rules that rejects the whole text at first reading.[15] The Sénat's page, updated on 10 November, shows no Senate stage yet.[16] The tax is not in the bill.

The public is ahead of the chamber. The highest poll, by Ifop for the Parti socialiste on 10 and 11 September 2025, found 86% in favour, 58% completely.[21] Elabe for BFMTV found 74% in favour that month, with the question stating that professional assets are included.[22] On 4 and 5 November 2025 it asked whether the deputies were right to reject the tax. 59% said they were wrong and 40% that they were right.[23] More [polls](/resources?country=fr&kind=poll) are on the Resources page.

## Who supports it {#who-supports}

The tax began as an Ecologist bill and has the whole parliamentary left behind it. [Éric Coquerel](/fr/A0085) tabled the Insoumis amendment in October 2026,[11] and [Olivier Faure](/fr/A0104) signed the Socialist amendment.[14] Outside parliament, the [CGT](/fr/A0245) and [Sophie Binet](/fr/A0223), the [CFDT](/fr/A0247) and [Marylise Léon](/fr/A0224), [Oxfam France](/fr/A0239) and [Attac France](/fr/A0240) back it. Among economists, Zucman is joined by [Jean Pisani-Ferry](/fr/A0228) and [Olivier Blanchard](/fr/A0237). The pillar page lists [who backs the tax around the world](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#who).

## What France shows {#lessons}

### Shrinking the base costs more every year {#base}

The ISF was undone by changing what it taxed. The committee measures the result at €4.5 billion of lost revenue in 2022 on its assumption of equal growth.[2] A tax confined to real estate leaves out the wealth that grows fastest. A new tax has to count all of it, shares and holdings included, as the floor tax does.[14]

### Departures stay at 0.2% {#departures}

The tax administration's data show about 0.2% of payers leaving before the reform and about 0.2% after it.[1][2] The exit argument rests on a few hundred households a year. An exit charge, as in the October 2026 amendment, answers them.[14] The [Spanish](/es) and [Swiss](/ch) stories show wealth taxes that are in force today.

### The top pays least {#top}

The cap on the old ISF cut the effective rate to 0.1% for the richest 0.001%.[2] A floor of 2% on net wealth above €100 million fixes that where it arises, and it needs no cap, because it subtracts the taxes already paid instead.[14] The [pillar page](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#revenue) sets out what such a tax would raise.
