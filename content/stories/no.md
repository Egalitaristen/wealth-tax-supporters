Norway raised its wealth tax in 2022 and the papers said the rich were fleeing. Three years later the tax brought in 72% more: NOK 18.5 billion in 2021 and NOK 31.8 billion in 2024.[4] More people paid it, too, 719,007 against 667,022.[4] A study of Norway's tax records counts at most 100 people who left because of the rise, and finds that their leaving cost the tax about 2% of its revenue.[6]

Why tax wealth at all, when Norway already taxes income? Because at the top the wealth is shares in private companies, and the profit stays inside the company, often inside a holding company the owner controls. Nothing is paid out, so there is no income to tax. Records from twenty years of Norwegian and Swedish owners show where that ends up: all taxes together take about 50% of the income of the upper middle class and only 15% to 20% of the income of the richest.[5] The [pillar page](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#unrealised) follows the money step by step.

{{chart: effective-rates}}

Norway's answer is a yearly tax on what a person owns. In 2026 a single person pays 1.0% on net wealth above NOK 1.9 million and 1.1% on the part above NOK 21.5 million, and a couple has double the threshold.[1][2] In 2024 the average payer owed about NOK 44,000.[4] The sections below show how the tax works, what it raised, why the leavers did not matter, and who is trying to cut it now.

## What the tax is {#what-it-is}

The tax is charged once a year on what people own minus what they owe, whether or not the assets earned anything. A wealth tax is the only tax that reaches a fortune while it grows. Income tax sees the salary and the dividend, and a wealth tax sees the shares.

Norway has taxed wealth and income directly at state level since 1892, ten years after an 1882 act brought in direct municipal taxes.[13] One bill carries both parts, the municipality's and the state's.[1]

## How it works {#how-it-works}

### The base {#base}

Taxable wealth is what you own minus what you owe. The threshold is NOK 1.9 million for a single person. A couple taxed together gets NOK 3.8 million, and for them the higher rate starts at NOK 43 million.[1][2]

### Municipal and state parts {#municipal-and-state}

For 2026 the state charges 0.65% above the threshold and 0.75% on the part above NOK 21.5 million. The municipality adds at most 0.35%. Together that is 1.0% and 1.1%.[1][2]

### Valuation discounts {#discounts}

Not every asset counts at full value. A primary home counts at 25% of its market value, except the part above NOK 14 million, which counts at 70%. A second home counts in full. Shares and business property count at 80%, other operating assets at 70%. The discounts apply only to assets the taxpayer owns directly.[2]

These discounts are where the tax leaks, and they are a political choice. The share discount was 45% in 2021 and the Støre government cut it to 25% in 2022 and 20% in 2023.[3] Research on the owners of private firms in Norway finds that shares in private businesses, the main form of wealth at the top, enter the wealth tax base at only about a tenth of their market value.[5] A tax that counted every asset at market value would raise more from the largest fortunes and could run on a lower rate. See [what the tax has to count](/arguments/you-cannot-value-it) and [the design in short](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#difference).

### Filing and paying {#filing}

Owners of businesses do not need cash on hand. A private person can defer the part of the tax that falls on business assets for up to three years when it comes to NOK 30,000 or more, at an interest rate of the central bank's policy rate plus 5 percentage points.[17] The page on [owners with no cash](/arguments/no-cash) takes the objection in full.

## What it raises {#revenue}

The surprise came in 2022. The government and its two budget partners raised the state rates, added a higher bracket and cut the share discount to 25%.[3] The tax brought in 43% more in a single year, NOK 26.3 billion against 18.5 billion, and the number of payers fell 3% to 647,591.[4] The tax drew more from the top, and the rolls did not empty.

{{chart: receipts}}

In 2024 the tax raised NOK 31.8 billion from 719,007 people, 72% more than in 2021, and the group of payers had grown by 8%.[4]

The threshold has done most of the work of shrinking the group. It rose from NOK 120,000 in 2000 to NOK 1.2 million in 2015 and NOK 1.9 million in 2026, and the number of payers fell from 1,355,901 in 2001 to 513,532 in 2016.[3][1][4] Raising the threshold is how Norway keeps the tax on the top. That is the answer to the fear that thresholds creep down: in Norway they went up, and the page on [thresholds and spreading](/arguments/it-will-spread) shows why.

## How it grew and shrank {#history}

The tax moves with the government. A new system in 2010 set the primary-home discount at 75%. Under the Solberg government the rate fell to 0.85% in 2015 and the threshold rose to NOK 1.2 million that year. The discount on shares grew to 45% by 2021.[3] Then the Støre government reversed the direction: in 2022 it raised the rates and cut the discount to 25%, and in 2023 it cut the discount to 20% and abolished the discount on second homes.[3] That reversal came with the 72% rise above.

## The 2022 rise and the people who left {#departures}

When the tax went up, some of Norway's richest moved abroad, many to Switzerland. Headlines made it sound like a flood. The Finance Ministry counted: 48 people worth more than NOK 100 million moved abroad in 2022 and 34 in 2023, with NOK 45.7 billion of taxable wealth between them.[7] Norway's taxable net wealth in 2022 was NOK 3,223 billion, so the whole exodus held 1.4% of it.[4]

The study of the full reform goes further. It counts at most 100 people who left because of it, mostly from the top 0.1% of wealth and mostly in 2022 and 2023. Without any departures the receipts would have risen about 75% from 2021 to 2024. With them they rose 71.5%. The difference is 2% of the revenue. The firms of the people who moved stayed active, and Norwegian-owned firms paid out no more in dividends and invested no less after the reforms.[6] The page on [hurts growth](/arguments/hurts-growth) and the page on [they will leave](/arguments/they-will-leave) lay out the evidence.

The Swiss move shows the same thing from the owners' side. In May 2026 NRK counted 163 business owners who had moved to Switzerland since 2020. Their companies are still Norwegian. The dividends the owners drew from them rose from NOK 2.3 billion in 2019 to NOK 6.5 billion in 2024, and the owners paid about NOK 310 million of other Norwegian taxes in 2024. Together they had paid NOK 343 million of wealth tax before leaving, 1.1% of what the tax raised in 2024. Six of the 163 had sold their stakes completely.[8][4]

The leak that mattered was a different one, and it is closed. Until the end of 2022 a person could spend five years abroad and come back with the tax on earlier unrealised gains wiped out. The Finance Ministry counted NOK 142 billion of untaxed gains taken out of the country by people who emigrated in 2022 and 2023, NOK 134 billion of it in 2022, the year before the Storting acted.[10] The people who left had tax values about 70% below their real wealth, a larger gap than for the rich who stayed.[11] That is the valuation rules at work, not the rate. Since 29 November 2022 the tax on gains no longer lapses after five years, and since 20 March 2024 an emigrant has 12 years to pay it, in instalments or deferred with interest. The Finance Ministry expects the rule to bring in about NOK 100 million in 2026 and about NOK 1.2 billion a year after 12 years.[9]

What does a rich resident who stays and pays nothing give the country? Nothing. A tax with a hole loses money, and a tax with the hole closed collects.

The tax did not make households save less. Each extra krone of wealth tax made a household save 3.76 kroner more a year, mostly by earning more.[12]

## The political fight {#politics}

On 8 September 2025 Labour won 53 seats, the Progress Party 47 and Høyre 24. The Socialist Left, the Centre Party and Rødt took 9 each, the Greens 8, the Christian Democrats 7 and Venstre 3. Labour governs alone. The five parties that back the tax, Labour, the Socialist Left, Centre, Rødt and the Greens, hold 88 of 169 seats.[14] The Storting's decision for 2026 kept the rates.[1]

On 15 February 2026 Høyre's national congress voted 158 to 131 to abolish the tax entirely, on a proposal from its Buskerud branch, with the party's new leader Ine Eriksen Søreide voting for it. LO's chief economist Roger Bjørnstad said it would make Norway a tax haven.[15]

On 24 June 2026 the government's Tax Commission proposed one wealth tax rate between 0.25% and 0.75% in place of today's two, and no valuation discounts. Its package would cut the revenue by up to NOK 25.2 billion, depending on the rate. Rødt's member left the commission in protest.[16] The commission's own report keeps a tax on wealth. Ending the discounts is the right half of it, since a tax on the full value of what an owner has is the design this site argues for. The rate cut gives the money away: the tax raised NOK 31.8 billion in 2024.[4]
