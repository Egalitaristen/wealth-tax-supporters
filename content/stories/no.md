In Norway you can look up your neighbour's income, wealth and tax bill. Anyone aged 16 or over can log in and search the public tax lists, which show net income, net wealth and tax for each person. Every search is logged, and you can see who looked you up.[7] A single person pays 1.0% on net wealth above kr 1.9 million and 1.1% on the part above kr 21.5 million.[1][2] In 2024, about 719,000 people paid kr 32 billion.[4]

## What the tax is {#what-it-is}

The tax is charged once a year on what people own, whether or not the assets earned anything. Norway has taxed wealth and income directly at state level since 1892, ten years after an 1882 act brought in direct municipal taxes.[14] One bill carries two parts, one for the municipality and one for the state.[1]

## How it works {#how-it-works}

### The base {#base}

Taxable wealth is what you own minus the debts you owe. A couple taxed together gets double the threshold.[1][2]

### Valuation discounts {#discounts}

Not everything counts at full value. For 2026 a primary home counts at 25% of market value, except the part above kr 14 million, which counts at 70%. A second home counts in full. Shares and business property count at 80%, other operating assets at 70%.[2] Discounts apply only to assets you own directly.[2] These discounts are where the tax leaks, and they are a choice, not a law of nature. The Solberg government raised the share discount from nothing in 2016 to 45% in 2021, and the Støre government cut it back to 25% in 2022.[3]

### Municipal and state parts {#municipal-and-state}

For 2026 the state charges 0.65%, rising to 0.75% above kr 21.5 million, and the municipality charges 0.35%. Together that is 1.0% and 1.1%.[1][2] In 2024 the municipalities collected kr 21.5 billion of the total and the state kr 10.3 billion.[4]

### Filing and the public tax list {#filing}

Wealth goes on the annual tax return, and the discounts are applied automatically there.[2] The result is public through the tax lists.[7]

## What it raises {#revenue}

The surprise came in 2022. The government raised the state rates, added a 1.1% bracket and cut the share discount. Revenue rose 43% in a single year, to kr 26.2 billion, while the number of payers fell 3% to 647,591. The number paying more than kr 50,000 grew from 57,197 to 79,199.[5] The tax drew more from the people at the top, and nobody important vanished from the rolls.

By 2024 some 719,000 people paid kr 32 billion, an average of about kr 44,000 each.[4] In 2021 it was 667,022 people.[4] The threshold has done most of the work of shrinking the group. It rose from kr 120,000 in 2000 to kr 1.9 million in 2026, and the number of payers fell from about 1.36 million in 2001 to about 514,000 in 2016.[1][3][4]

## How it grew and shrank {#history}

The tax moves with the government. The red-green government of 2006 to 2013 tightened it in steps, and a new system in 2010 set the primary-home discount at 75%.[3] Under the Solberg government the rate fell to 0.85% in 2015 and the threshold rose to kr 1.2 million that year.[3] The Støre government then added the 1.1% bracket above kr 20 million, which now starts at kr 21.5 million.[1][3]

## The 2022 rise and the people who left {#departures}

When the tax went up in 2022, some of Norway's richest moved abroad, many to Switzerland. Headlines made it sound like a flood. The Finance Ministry counted the numbers.

It looked at 2,864 people who held more than kr 100 million in at least one year from 2011 to 2021, or who were on the business magazine *Kapital*'s list of the 400 richest. 130 of them emigrated between 2011 and mid-November 2022, 23 of them in 2022. The wealth tax they had paid in the year before leaving came to about kr 300 million, roughly 1% of what the tax raises in a year. Over 2012 to 2021, 102 people with similar wealth moved in, and they paid about kr 335 million.[8] The people leaving and the people arriving almost cancelled out.

The real leak was a different one. Until the end of 2022, a person could spend five years abroad and come back with the tax on earlier unrealised gains wiped out. The Finance Ministry counted kr 142 billion of untaxed gains taken abroad in 2022 and 2023, kr 134 billion of it in 2022, the year before the loophole closed. The wealth tax those emigrants no longer pay comes to about kr 770 million a year, according to NHH researcher Ole-Andreas Elvik Næss.[11] The leavers also had unusually generous tax values. Their assets were assessed on average about 70% below their real wealth, a bigger gap than for the rich who stayed.[10] The researcher's reading is that the untaxed gains drew them abroad. That loophole is now closed.

What does a rich resident who stays and pays nothing give the country? Nothing, and the abolition debate leaves that out. A tax with a hole in it loses money. A tax with the hole closed collects.

The other standard worry is that wealth taxes make people save less. Economist Marius Ring studied Norwegian households and found the reverse: for each krone of wealth tax, households saved about 3.8 kroner more, mostly by earning more.[13]

## The political fight {#politics}

On 8 September 2025 Labour won 53 seats, the Progress Party 47 and Høyre 24. SV, Sp and Rødt took 9 each and MDG 8. The five parties that back the tax hold 88 of 169 seats.[15] The decision for 2026 kept the rates.[1]

On 15 February 2026 Høyre's national assembly voted 158 to 131 to abolish the tax entirely. LO's chief economist, Roger Bjørnstad, said that would make Norway a tax haven.[16] On 24 June the tax commission proposed one rate between 0.25% and 0.75%, no valuation discounts and a higher general threshold. Its package would cut the wealth tax by up to kr 25.2 billion. The Rødt member resigned from the commission in protest.[18] Even the commission kept a tax on wealth.
