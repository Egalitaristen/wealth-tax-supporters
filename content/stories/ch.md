Income tax sees only what is paid out to you. A company that keeps its profit and grows makes its owner richer without paying them a franc, and the gain is not income until the shares are sold. Switzerland has an old answer: every canton also taxes what you own, once a year. In 2024 that wealth tax raised CHF 9,458 million for the cantons and municipalities, 10.2% of all the tax they collected.[2]

The Federal Supreme Court has spelled out why that is fair. In 2009 a couple in Geneva owned half of a company, and the tax office valued their wealth at CHF 40.3 million. Their cantonal and municipal tax for the year, income and wealth together, came to CHF 444,865, more than twice their taxable income of CHF 205,589. They said a bill bigger than your income is confiscation. On 5 January 2017 the court said no. The company had kept its profits, about CHF 26 million over the years, instead of paying them out, so the couple's shares had grown in value and nobody had taxed the growth. The bill stayed well below it.[5] [The pillar page](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#unrealised) shows the same pattern across the world's billionaires.

The exemption in 2014 ran from only CHF 25,000 in Obwalden to CHF 200,000 in Ticino, and 5,150,529 taxpayers were liable in 2016.[3] Switzerland shows that a tax on the holding can be run at scale, year after year. Its weak points are the ones the design leaves open, valuation discounts and cantons bidding against each other, and both have fixes.

## Once the main cantonal tax {#history}

Until the First World War the wealth tax carried the main weight of the cantons' direct taxes. Income taxes took the lead afterwards, and the wealth tax stayed as a companion to them.[2] The Confederation levied a direct federal tax on wealth until 1959, and ended it then because stacking it on the cantonal taxes would have been too heavy. In the Second World War it raised a one-off levy on wealth, the "defence sacrifice".[2]

Parliament passed the harmonisation law on 14 December 1990, and it took effect on 1 January 1993. It requires every canton to tax the whole net wealth of its residents. The cantons keep the tariffs, the rates and the allowances.[2] Today there is no federal wealth tax. There are 26 cantonal ones, with the municipalities adding their own on top.[2]

## How it works today {#how-it-works}

Every adult resident declares everything they own, subtracts their debts, and pays on what is left. Cash, bank accounts including crypto, shares, property, gold, cars, boats, art and collections all count. Household goods and personal items are taxed in no canton, and pension capital in the second pillar and pillar 3a is exempt until it is paid out. Married couples are assessed together.[2]

{{chart: thresholds}}

The bar is low. The OECD puts Zurich's threshold for a single person at €67,550 in 2017, against €1,300,000 in France.[3] The tax therefore reaches people with ordinary savings, and it reaches nearly all declared wealth. In Lucerne 42% of filers and in Bern 34% had wealth above the exemption, and the wealth under it was only 2.5% of the total in Lucerne and 5.4% in Bern.[4]

Assets are valued at market value. Listed shares go by the price list the Federal Tax Administration publishes each year, and unlisted shares by the price of a recent sale between independent parties or by a formula the cantonal tax offices agreed on.[2] How that works is on the page [you cannot value it](/arguments/you-cannot-value-it).

## What it raises, and who pays {#revenue}

In 2024 the wealth tax raised CHF 5,808 million for the cantons and CHF 3,650 million for the municipalities, CHF 9,458 million in all. That is 10.2% of the CHF 92,972 million they collected in tax, and 5.5% of the CHF 173,316 million collected by the Confederation, cantons and municipalities together.[2] In 2016 the OECD counted 3.7% of all Swiss tax revenue from it, and 1.0% of GDP.[3]

{{chart: revenue-share}}

The OECD ties Switzerland's much larger take to its low thresholds, its wide base and its many wealthy residents.[3] In 2022 the richest 1% of taxpayers held 45.8% of all taxable net wealth, and the Gini coefficient, the standard measure of inequality, was 0.823, where 1 would mean one person owns everything.[1] The statistics leave out pension capital, household goods, undeclared wealth and the foreigners taxed on their living costs, and the tax values of homes and unlisted shares sit below market value.[1] The real concentration is higher. In Geneva the top 1% held 67.3%, up from 62.6% a year earlier, a rise the tax administration ties to the exceptional profits of the commodity traders based there.[1] For the same pattern worldwide, see [where the wealth is](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#concentration), and for what such a tax raises, [the revenue](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#revenue).

## A bill above one year's income is no confiscation {#court}

The Geneva couple's case is the Swiss answer to the cry that owners "have no cash". The court did not say a tax above one year's income is fine because the owners are rich. It said a tax on the holding is a different kind of tax from an income tax and cannot be measured against one year's return. Only when the tax, wealth tax included, exceeds the owner's income and returns for years on end is the fortune being eaten away, and that is the point where taxation turns into confiscation.[5] Where a company keeps its profits, the shares' value rises faster than the tax, and the question is settled.[5]

Switzerland adds a safety valve of its own. In the OECD's 2018 survey, seven of the 26 cantons cap the total burden, as a share of income or of wealth.[3] The page on [owners with no cash](/arguments/no-cash) covers deferral and loans, and the page on [family firms](/arguments/family-firms) covers who a threshold leaves out.

## Cantons undercut each other {#competition}

Each canton sets its own rate, so the cantons bid against each other. In the 2000s the top rates fell, most sharply in central Switzerland, while the high-tax western cantons held theirs.[4]

Lucerne went furthest. It cut its wealth tax in half in 2009. Bern put a bigger cut and a smaller one to a vote, and the smaller won by 50.9% to 49.1%. Lucerne's top rate had been 0.58%, and its cut ran 0.18 points deeper than Bern's. Set against Bern, declared wealth in Lucerne rose 33.7% in the six years that followed, and still its tax revenue in 2015 was 8% below its 2008 level, while Bern's was 4% above.[4]

{{chart: lucerne-bern}}

Across the cantons, every 10% cut in the top rate lost between 2.7% and 3.6% of wealth tax revenue.[4] That is the weak spot of a tax run by 26 governments and the reason for a common floor: if no canton can undercut the others, nobody gains by running. The same logic works between countries, and [the page on leavers](/arguments/they-will-leave#minimum) sets out how a minimum tax follows it.

## Rich foreigners and the lump sum {#lump-sum}

Foreigners who have not lived in Switzerland for ten years and do not work there can pay tax on their living costs in place of their income and wealth. In 2012 there were 5,634 of them.[6] In 2014, 5,382 paid CHF 740 million between them, 1.2% of income and wealth tax revenue.[4] Their wealth sits outside the wealth statistics.[1]

A federal initiative to end the regime lost on 30 November 2014 with 40.8% yes. Five of the six cantons with the highest yes shares had already scrapped it themselves. Only Schaffhausen voted yes. Valais, which has many lump-sum payers, voted 79.3% no, and the opponents ran about twenty times as many newspaper advertisements as the backers.[6]

## What voters and parliament have said {#votes}

Votes on bigger taxes on wealth have been lost. A 20% national inheritance tax on estates above CHF 2 million lost on 14 June 2015 with 71.7% no and in every canton.[7] In Geneva the ten-year solidarity contribution on fortunes above CHF 3 million lost on 18 June 2023: 47,369 voters said yes and 58,170 no, so 44.9% yes, on a turnout of 40.1%.[9] On 30 November 2025 the Young Socialists' inheritance tax initiative got 21.7% yes.[8] The register records four motions for a federal wealth tax or levy that failed in the National Council since 2021. The latest, [motion 23.3361](/resources/ch-motion-23-3361), asked for a constitutional change to allow a federal tax on personal wealth. It lost on 13 March 2024 with 60 yes and 132 no.[10]

None of these was a vote on the yearly wealth tax that every canton already levies. The survey after the 2025 vote found that the arguments that did the damage were the threat to family firms and the flight of the wealthy, and that voters judged the proposal too extreme, including supporters of the Social Democrats, of whom only 53% voted yes.[8] Those two arguments have answers: the page on [family firms](/arguments/family-firms) shows how a threshold keeps a firm's owners out of reach, and [the page on leavers](/arguments/they-will-leave) shows who left when other countries raised their tax. The page on [whether it will spread](/arguments/it-will-spread) takes up the fear of a threshold that creeps down.

## Who backs it now {#supporters}

The federal proposals are young, and none is a bill. In February 2026 the Green Liberals put forward a federal wealth tax of 0.33% above CHF 5 million, which they say would raise about CHF 2.7 billion a year to pay for the 13th AHV pension and the army. National Councillor [Patrick Hässig](/ch/A0113) presented it.[13] [Alfred Gantner](/ch/A0084), co-founder of the investment firm Partners Group, said in December 2025 that a tax could start at 1% above CHF 200 million, run to 1.2% at half a billion and 1.5% at CHF 1 billion.[14] [Alliance Sud](/ch/A0181), the development charities' alliance, calculates that a rate of 2.7% on the richest 0.1% of taxpayers would bring in more than CHF 10 billion a year, and says a federal tax needs bank data exchanged inside Switzerland to be enforced.[15] On 23 October 2026 the economist [Gabriel Zucman](/fr/A0229) said he would like a rate of 2% on Swiss fortunes, for example as a federal wealth tax.[16]

In the spring of 2026 a Tamedia survey found 68% in favour of a federal tax to finance the AHV and the army, and 25% against.[16] The question and the sponsors are on the [poll page](/resources/ch-poll-leewas-2026). On 14 September 2026 it was reported that the Council of States security committee had rejected a financing concept with a wealth tax on especially large fortunes, by 8 votes to 2 with one abstention, and on 21 September 2026 the full Council turned down paying for the army fund with a wealth tax. The bill now goes to the National Council.[12][11] You can try the Green Liberal scale in the [calculator](/calculator?w=20m&b=5000000:0.33&c=CHF).

## What the Swiss record shows {#lessons}

### Valuation {#valuation}

Switzerland values assets every year for nearly everyone. The Federal Tax Administration reports that the tax values of homes and unlisted company shares usually sit below market value and differ from canton to canton.[1] The dossier shows where. Aargau cuts the value of unlisted shares by 50%, Neuchâtel by 60%, and Valais sets qualifying holdings at 50%. Nidwalden lowers the rate on stakes of 10% or more.[2] A discount is a rate cut in another form, and the answer is the one the Swiss already use for listed shares: count the asset at its market value and write no exemption in. [You cannot value it](/arguments/you-cannot-value-it) takes the objection in full.

### Flight {#flight}

When Lucerne cut its rate, the wealth its residents declared jumped. A study of where the extra wealth came from found that about a quarter came from people moving in from other cantons, a fifth from a rise in house prices, and about half from residents who stayed and declared more financial assets.[4] The tax office gets no bank data about residents except in criminal cases, so financial wealth is whatever people write on the form.[4] The wealth was there before, and a lower rate made it easier to admit.

Switzerland is also where leavers go. It was the top destination of the French real estate tax payers who left in 2021, with 19% of them, and a count by the Norwegian broadcaster NRK found 163 Norwegian business owners who had moved there since 2020.[17][18] They moved to a country that levies a wealth tax in every canton. The page on [leavers](/arguments/they-will-leave) has the full count.

So the fixes are a floor that no canton can undercut and bank reporting that leaves little to hide. Neither is a reason to give up the tax: a rich resident who hides assets pays nothing, and the money they hide does not go away. The answer to leavers is on [the leavers page](/arguments/they-will-leave), and the pillar page shows why [a minimum](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#minimum) is built the way it is.

### Revenue {#revenue-lesson}

Cutting the rate does not pay for itself, as the cantons found.[4] Every canton that bids the rate down gives away part of the 10.2% of cantonal and municipal tax that wealth brings in.[2]
