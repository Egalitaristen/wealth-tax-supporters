Austria taxed net wealth at 1% a year until the end of 1993. Parliament then repealed the tax inside a large tax package, and the money was found by raising a tax on payrolls.[1][3] Nothing was done about the weak spots of the old tax, such as land valued at decades-old assessments. They were left in place and the tax was deleted. Today Austria collects very little on wealth, and the SPÖ, whose committee members helped pass the repeal, asks for the tax back.[4][10]

## What the tax was {#tax}

The Vermögensteuergesetz of 1954 charged a flat 1% a year on taxable net wealth.[1] Each taxpayer had an allowance of S 150,000 (Austrian schillings), the same for a spouse and for every minor child in the household. Spouses and children were assessed together. Companies seated in Austria paid too, with a minimum base of S 1 million for a stock company and S 500,000 for a limited company.[1]

A couple with two minor children had S 600,000 free. Take such a family with taxable wealth of S 5 million. It paid 1% of S 4.4 million, which is S 44,000, or about €3,200 at the fixed rate of ATS 13.7603 to the euro.[1][6]

In its last years the government counted the tax at S 8.3 billion a year, about €600 million.[3][6] A smaller related levy, the Erbschaftssteueräquivalent, brought S 2.1 billion on top.[3]

## How it was abolished {#repeal}

The government sent the Steuerreformgesetz 1993 to the Nationalrat on 22 September 1993. Its own summary calls it the most sweeping change in the tax system in decades. "Traditional" taxes on trade and on wealth would go, and the government expected this to strengthen the equity capital of Austrian firms.[3][4] A motion from 1991 by Hermann Böhacker and colleagues gave the same reason: a tax on business assets was said to hold back equity, and other industrial countries taxed it less.[4]

The finance committee took the bill up on 4 November 1993. Members of the SPÖ and the ÖVP moved the amendment that carried it by majority. The law was published on 30 November as BGBl. 818/1993 and took effect on 1 January 1994.[2][3][4]

The reason given in the bill was company equity. Flight of rich residents does not appear in the committee report.[3][4] A tax with an allowance per person and an assessment of the household can be adjusted for companies without being deleted for everyone.

## What the repeal cost {#cost}

The package cost the state about S 17 billion a year.[3] The government paid for part of it with labour. The payroll tax went from 2% to 3% and became a municipal tax worth an extra S 8.3 billion.[3][5] The Greens' dissent in the committee report, written by Andreas Wabl, counted the new burden on work at about S 8.3 billion and noted that private households were relieved of about S 2 billion of the wealth tax and its companion levy.[4] Work paid more, and the owners of large fortunes paid less.

The Greens named a second damage. Austria already raised only 1.1% of GDP from taxes on wealth, against 1.9% in the European Community and 2.1% across the OECD. With the tax gone, they wrote, private wealth would in effect no longer be taxed. They added that the last general revaluation of land was from 1 January 1973, so assessed values stood at a fifth to a tenth of real value.[4] Here lay the real flaw of the old tax, and a new valuation would have fixed it.

The same law offered an amnesty. Shares were brought into the final withholding tax, and the Vermögensteuer that owners had avoided before 1994 was forgiven.[3]

A study from 1996 found that company taxes fell sharply from 1994. Even after counting the higher payroll tax, the Finance Ministry's estimate for 1996 only reached the 1992 level although profits had risen. Banks were paying almost half as much as before.[5] The same study describes the Privatstiftungsgesetz of 1993, which let owners place wealth in a private foundation that paid an entry tax of 2.5% and could then receive certain capital income free of tax. The study called this a tax-free zone created at home.[5] It expected Austria to fall to the bottom of the OECD in wealth taxation after the repeal.[5]

## What happened after {#after}

By 2022 the slide had gone far. In property taxes made up 1% of Austria's tax revenue, €2.6 billion, which put Austria 34th of 38 OECD countries, while total taxes stood at 42.7% of GDP against an OECD average of 33.9% in 2023.[9]

Wealth meanwhile stays concentrated. In 2021 the median household had net wealth of about €128,000 and the mean was about €293,000. Net wealth above €1 million occurs only in the top 5% of households.[8] The Nationalbank says its survey misses the very top. The report cites the European Central Bank, which puts the share of the richest 1% at 40% in 2022, against about 28% in the euro area.[8]

The inheritance and gift tax went the same way. In March 2007 the Constitutional Court struck down the inheritance tax, and in June the gift tax, because land was valued at three times assessments that were decades old. It set the end of the rule at 31 July 2008. For the gift tax the government itself asked for that same deadline, to check which accompanying rules it needed.[7] Parliament had 17 months to correct the valuation. It did not, and no inheritance tax has been collected since.[16] Momentum Institut counts about €20 billion inherited a year in Austria, rising to €40 billion within 25 years.[11] The lesson is the one from 1993: when the base is out of date, update the base.

## A modern wealth tax {#today}

The SPÖ model taxes the net wealth of households and private foundations. It charges 0.5% on wealth from €1 million to €10 million, 1% from €10 million to €50 million and 2% above €50 million. An owner-occupied home is free up to €1.5 million. The SPÖ says at most 2% of the population would pay and puts the yield, together with a new inheritance tax, at €5 to €6 billion a year.[10]

Take a household with €12 million of net wealth outside its home. Under the bands printed by the SPÖ it pays 0.5% on €9 million, which is €45,000, plus 1% on €2 million, which is €20,000. That is €65,000 a year, or 0.54% of its wealth.[10] The Momentum Institut puts the yield of a net wealth tax from €1 million at up to €5 billion a year.[11] Only the top 5% of households have more than €1 million, so the tax stays on the top.[8]

A study ordered by a business foundation and a bank starts from the same €5 billion. It then reports a net long-run effect of €2 billion after investment falls, and claims that 40,000 jobs would be lost.[12] That is a model, and it takes the €5 billion as given. It is also more than three times the S 8.3 billion the old tax raised, in euro terms.[3][6][12] The old tax ran for nearly forty years, and the Armutskonferenz paper points out that most large wealth is real estate, which cannot leave.[13]

A new tax should avoid the old holes: value land and company shares at market value, include private foundations, keep the allowance at the household level and tax wealth held abroad. The SPÖ model already names foundations.[10] An exit tax on those who leave with a fortune completes it.

## Where things stand in November 2026 {#position}

The government of ÖVP, SPÖ and NEOS has no wealth tax in its programme. Finance Minister Markus Marterbauer of the SPÖ said on 13 March 2025 that such taxes were not up for debate for five years. He called taxes on large inheritances and fortunes economically sound and hoped to see them in the next coalition programme.[15]

On 25 February 2026 the Nationalrat rejected an urgent motion from the Greens for an inheritance tax on large estates. SPÖ speakers called wealth taxes right. State Secretary Alexander Pröll rejected both wealth and inheritance taxes, the FPÖ is against every new tax, and the ÖVP and the NEOS warned that the taxes would hinder business transfers.[14] The Greens ask for an inheritance tax and no annual wealth tax, so the register does not list them.[16]

On 9 November 2026 the news and motion pages of parlament.gv.at show no bill for an annual net wealth tax in the current Nationalrat. In a 2022 poll for the Momentum Institut 65% of Austrians aged 16 and over backed a tax on large wealth and 19% were against.[17]

## Who supports it {#who-supports}

The SPÖ has the most developed model. Party leader [Andreas Babler](/at/A0002) presents the tax as a way to cut taxes on work, and Finance Minister [Markus Marterbauer](/at/A0018) argued for a progressive wealth tax before taking office. The [Arbeiterkammer](/at/A0007), the [ÖGB](/at/A0005) and the union [GPA](/at/A0009) back a tax from €1 million. The [Momentum Institut](/at/A0012) supplies the yield figures, [Attac](/at/A0011) and the [KPÖ](/at/A0016) also call for it, and heiress [Marlene Engelhorn](/at/A0014) campaigns for it. Die Armutskonferenz, an [anti-poverty network](/at/A0038), has argued for a wealth tax on the richest tenth since 2010.[13] Economist [Jakob Kapeller](/at/A0027) designed the 1-2-3-4 scale of 1% from €1 million up to 4% above €1 billion.

## What Austria shows {#lessons}

### Repeal moved the burden {#burden}

The state gave up S 8.3 billion a year from wealth and raised the payroll tax by about the same amount.[3] 
### The flaws were fixable {#fixable}

Old land values and soft rules hurt the 1954 tax in 1993, and old land values ended the inheritance tax in 2008.[4][7] A new tax starts from market values.
