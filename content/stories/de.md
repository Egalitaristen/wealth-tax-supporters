Germany has a wealth tax. It is in the statute book, and no tax office has billed anyone under it since 1997.[6] People often say the Federal Constitutional Court struck it down in 1995. It did not. The Court found a narrow flaw: land was valued at 1964 prices and everything else at today's prices, and one rate was applied to both. It gave the legislature until the end of 1996 to fix that.[1] The governing parties drafted a repeal instead. Parliament struck the repeal from the bill, and the deadline passed with nothing fixed.[3][4]

## What the Court decided {#ruling}

A tax court in Rhineland-Palatinate asked whether charging one rate on every asset fits the equal treatment clause of the Basic Law.[1] The Court said no, but it did not cancel the tax. It declared the rule incompatible with the Constitution and left the fix to parliament. Land was taxed on values fixed in 1964, raised by 40%, while shares and cash were taxed at current values.[1] The DIW, a Berlin economics institute, puts those land values at only 10% to 20% of market prices by the early 1990s.[7] The judges were unanimous on the result.[1]

The order says plainly that wealth may be taxed. The Court wrote that the stock of a person's property can be a basis for a tax, and named the wealth tax as the example.[1] It left open whether such a tax could also serve redistribution under other rules. On the remedy it said parliament had several options. It set 31 December 1996 for a new rule, kept the old law running until then, and allowed up to five years of transition if a general revaluation was needed.[1]

Critics still quote one passage: the combined burden of the wealth tax and income taxes should stay near half of the expected return on the asset.[1] That passage is weak. Judge Böckenförde agreed with the result and called the reasoning unnecessary and without footing in the Constitution.[1] In 2006 the Court itself wrote that a half-split ceiling follows neither from the operative part nor from the reasons that carry it.[2] The Court says the ceiling opponents cite is not part of what its order binds anyone to.

## What the tax was {#tax}

The tax was charged yearly on net wealth, and the Länder kept the yield.[1][16] The rate for individuals was 0.5% from 1978 to 1994. In 1995 it doubled to 1.0% and the allowance rose to DM 120,000, about €61,000.[7] A single person with €2 million of net wealth and no other allowances owed 1% of €1.94 million, about €19,000 a year.[7]

The Court put the yield at about DM 6 billion around 1993, near €3 billion, and said most of it came from business assets.[1] The 1995 assessment fixed €3.8 billion of tax, 67% of it from individuals. 904,000 individuals paid, 3.1% of households.[7] The yield had slid from 0.45% of GDP in the early 1960s to 0.2% by the early 1990s.[7]

## Why it lapsed {#lapse}

The cure was plain: revalue the land. On 11 June 1996 the CDU/CSU and FDP filed a different answer. Their annual tax bill repealed the wealth tax law from 1 January 1997 and gave up on a general revaluation of land. Its stated reasons were that the tax cost more than twice as much to collect as income tax and weighed on business capital.[3] The same day the Greens filed a bill that kept the tax, raised the personal allowance to DM 350,000 and ordered a new valuation of land.[16]

The repeal did not survive. The budget committee's report of 5 November 1996 lists its removal, with the result that the tax formally stays in force but cannot be levied.[4] The Bundesrat asked for full compensation of the Länder for the revenue lost from 1997, and the Länder received higher real estate transfer and inheritance taxes.[5][7] Nobody wrote the valuation law. In 2003 the Finance Committee recorded that the legislature had made no use of the chance and that the tax had been inapplicable since 1 January 1997. It recommended rejecting a CDU/CSU bill to repeal the law.[6] The Länder cannot legislate their own tax while the federal law stands.[7]

## Where the bills stand on 8 November 2026 {#bills}

The Left's motion of February 2026 asks for a bill taxing net wealth above €1 million, with an extra €5 million allowance for business assets. The rate rises from 1% to 5% at €50 million and jumps to 12% above €1 billion.[8] The DIW, commissioned by the Left, puts the yield before any change in behaviour at €147 billion a year, 3.2% of GDP. The motion itself says about €100 billion after people react.[7][8] In the debate Parsa Marvi said for the SPD that this coalition will not bring wealth taxation back, but his party's programme provides for it.[11] In October the Finance Committee recommended rejection, with CDU/CSU, AfD, SPD and Greens voting against the Left.[10]

The Greens' motion of September asks for a tax with a €500 million allowance, a rate of 2% rising to 3% at €1 billion and 4% from €10 billion, and income tax paid credited. They expect more than €20 billion a year, from a few hundred payers.[9][10] The 2025 coalition agreement of CDU, CSU and SPD never mentions a wealth tax.[12] In committee the CDU/CSU said valuation is why the tax has not been levied since 1997. The SPD called the concentration of large fortunes a real problem.[10] Valuation is a solved problem: since 2009 inheritance tax values land and companies close to market prices, and the DIW proposes the same rules for a wealth tax.[7]

## Who sits at the top {#top}

The Bundesbank's survey of 2021 puts 56% of net wealth with the richest 10% of households, and notes that the figure is an underestimate.[13] Its Gini coefficient for 2023 is 0.724, hardly changed since 2021.[14] The DIW's 2026 estimate gives the richest 10% of adults just over two thirds of €16.5 trillion. The top 1%, from €2.3 million, hold just over a third.[7] A €1 million allowance would leave 1.6 million people, 2.3% of adults, liable.[7]

## What a constitutional design looks like {#design}

The Court asked for equal valuation and little else. Since 2009 the valuation tool exists.[7] A design that follows the order has four parts.

- One valuation for all assets, so one rate scale fits all of them.[1][7]
- A high allowance. The 1995 tax caught 3.1% of households, the Left's design 1.3 million people, the Greens' a few hundred.[7][9]
- Tax on the owner, not the company. The old tax hit companies with no relief for their owners.[7]
- Rates under the return on wealth, with income tax credited. The Left's motion cites a typical return of about 6%, and the Greens credit income tax paid.[8][9]

The 12% bracket is the weak spot. The DIW warns that with heavy avoidance the net yield of the Left's design can turn negative at billionaire rates, and the Greens told the committee that 12% on top of a typical return invites the charge of a strangling tax.[7][10] The Greens' design stops at 4%, which answers both. The DIW's 2016 estimate for a design with high allowances was €10 billion to €20 billion a year, with collection costs of 4% to 8% of the yield.[15] That is a long way from the "twice as much to collect" of the 1996 repeal bill.

## Who supports it {#who-supports}

Support is wide and sits in the opposition. [Die Linke](/de/A0001) has tabled wealth tax motions in the Bundestag since 2010, and [Heidi Reichinnek](/de/A0022) spoke for the 2026 motion on 6 March.[8][11] The [Greens](/de/A0002) filed their own tax in September 2026 under [Katharina Dröge](/de/A0323).[9] The [SPD](/de/A0118) tells the committee it wants more from very large fortunes.[10] The unions [DGB](/de/A0124), [ver.di](/de/A0139) and [IG Metall](/de/A0141) back a wealth tax, as do [Attac](/de/A0120) and the [Netzwerk Steuergerechtigkeit](/de/A0132). The DIW's president [Marcel Fratzscher](/de/A0155) and [Gabriel Zucman](/de/A0166) are in the register too.

## What Germany shows {#lessons}

### The Court asked for a law {#law}

The order called for equal valuation and allowed up to five years of transition.[1] The legislature answered with a repeal bill, and when its repeal article was struck the law stayed in place and the tax stayed unlevied.[3][4] The task now is one vote on one design that follows the order, with market-based valuation and a high allowance.
