Iceland has abolished its wealth tax, brought it back and let it die a second time. The first tax went in 2004. The crash of 2008 brought a new one in 2009, aimed at the top of the wealth ladder. In its last year it raised ISK 10.3 billion.[8] Then the end date written into the law arrived, and the government that took office in 2013 chose not to renew it.[7][10]

## The first tax and its repeal {#first}

Iceland has long taxed net wealth, which means everything you own minus your debts. From 1988 to 2002 the rate was 1.2% to 1.45% on wealth above a threshold. That threshold sat at roughly the average household's net wealth, so it reached ordinary families.[14] In 1995 the tax had 49,558 payers. In 2000 it had 65,012, and it raised ISK 3.6 billion.[4] In 2002 the rate was halved to 0.6% and revenue fell by half.[14] The last version taxed wealth above ISK 4.98 million at 0.6%.[2]

In 2004 the finance minister, Geir H. Haarde, put a tax-cut package to Parliament. It cut the income tax rate by 4 points in steps up to 2007 and abolished the net wealth tax on individuals and companies. Together the cuts were worth about ISK 20 billion a year once fully in force. The wealth tax alone cost ISK 3.7 billion a year.[1] The last bills went out in 2005, on wealth at the end of 2004. For the next four years Iceland had no tax on wealth at all.[1][4]

The old tax had a real design fault: it reached tens of thousands of people and raised little. A higher threshold fixes that, and Iceland later did exactly that. Deleting the tax fixed nothing. The 2009 bill itself says that ownership of wealth had become more concentrated in the years before.[3] The state met the crash with no wealth tax in its books and had to build one from scratch under pressure.

## Back after the crash {#return}

When the banks collapsed in 2008 the state needed money. The finance minister, Steingrímur J. Sigfússon, brought the tax back in a bill in autumn 2009, under a new name, auðlegðarskattur, which means wealth tax. The bill's explanation gives the reason in numbers. According to the 2009 tax returns, 1,400 couples, 2.2% of all couples, owned net wealth above ISK 120 million. Together they owned about ISK 208 billion, roughly a quarter of the net wealth in the country. The bill adds that people who had built up large fortunes had enjoyed low taxes on capital income, while ordinary people carried heavier burdens.[3]

The first scale taxed net wealth at 1.25% above ISK 90 million for a single person and ISK 120 million for a couple, for three years.[3] It was enacted at the end of 2009.[9] A family needed a fortune of that size to pay anything. In the first year, 3,678 people paid.[4]

The tax also closed a loophole. Owners of companies can hold shares in the books at a low nominal value while the shares are worth far more. A second charge, the additional wealth tax, hit the gap between nominal and real value of those shares.[8] It was a direct answer to avoidance, and it brought in ISK 2.4 billion in 2012 and ISK 4.4 billion in 2014.[5][8]

## What it raised {#raised}

The tax raised ISK 3.6 billion in 2010 and ISK 10.3 billion in 2014, about ISK 37 billion over the five assessments.[4][5][8] In the first year 3,678 people paid. By 2013 there were 5,980 payers, about 3,100 families, and in 2014 the taxable base was ISK 2.2 trillion.[4][6][8] The state tightened the tax twice while it ran. In 2011 the thresholds were cut to ISK 75 million and ISK 100 million and the rate went to 1.5%. In 2012 a 2% band was added above ISK 150 million and ISK 200 million.[5]

Take a single person with ISK 250 million in net wealth under the last scale. Nothing is due on the first ISK 75 million. The next ISK 75 million pays 1.5%, which is ISK 1.125 million. The remaining ISK 100 million pays 2%, which is ISK 2 million. The bill is ISK 3.125 million, 1.25% of the fortune.[8]

## What it did to inequality {#effects}

The Icelandic Confederation of Labour, ASÍ, measured it. During 2009 to 2013 the wealth tax raised the tax burden on the very highest incomes, which kept the whole tax system rising with income all the way to the top. It cut wealth inequality, measured by the Gini index, by 0.5 to 0.6 points, much more than the old tax did. The old tax had lower thresholds, so many more people paid. The new one fell on the very richest. When the tax went, the tax burden on the top 1% of earners fell by 7.4 points.[14]

Inequality of wealth is far larger than inequality of income in Iceland. The richest tenth of families own 56% of household assets, and the top tenth by income takes 31% of income (2020).[14] A tax on the stock of wealth hits the place where the inequality sits.

Avoidance was the weak point. A government working group put the loss from offshore holdings at ISK 58 billion in unpaid capital income tax and wealth tax in 2009 to 2014.[14] That is a reason to chase offshore money. Dropping the tax leaves those holdings as untaxed as before and gives up the revenue from every other fortune as well.

## Why it lapsed {#lapse}

The law gave the tax an end date. Parliament extended it by a year in 2011, with changes that raised it.[5][14] Then in 2013 a new government took office. In October 2013 Bjarni Benediktsson, the finance minister, told Parliament the tax had been presented as temporary and had already been extended once.[10] In a written answer earlier that year he had given Parliament the price: letting it lapse would cost the state ISK 9.7 billion in direct revenue in 2015.[7]

On 19 December 2013 Steingrímur J. Sigfússon, by then in opposition, moved to extend it. His version answered the objection about pensioners who own a paid-off home and have little income. It added an allowance for the home you live in, ISK 30 million for a single person and ISK 40 million for a couple. Even so, he said, the tax would bring in ISK 8 billion to 9 billion in 2015, at a time when the government's policy was taking more than ISK 20 billion out of the state's revenue base.[12] The finance minister replied that the government's priority was a cut in the middle income tax bracket, and he said no.[11] The vote was 37 against, 7 for and 15 abstaining. Steingrímur and [Katrín Jakobsdóttir](/is/A0015), later prime minister, were among the seven who voted yes.[13]

The last bills went out in 2014, on wealth at the end of 2013.[8] Taxable wealth kept rising after the tax ended: the tax authority calculated that the 2013 rules would have raised ISK 6.4 billion in 2015 and ISK 7.5 billion in 2016, with the thresholds frozen.[8] In 2024 the finance minister told Parliament that the 2013 rules, with the thresholds raised in line with consumer prices, would have raised ISK 36.9 billion for 2023, on a base of ISK 10.1 trillion.[9]

## The case today {#today}

ASÍ's 2021 report says a well-designed tax on large fortunes would raise over ISK 20 billion a year.[14] The [Social Democratic Alliance](/is/A0005) took a tax into the 2021 election: 1.5% on net wealth above ISK 200 million, falling on the richest 1% only.[15][16] Its economist [Kristrún Frostadóttir](/is/A0006), now Prime Minister, gave the example of a person with ISK 1 billion, who would pay 1.5% of the ISK 800 million above the line, ISK 12 million a year.[16] [Jóhann Páll Jóhannsson](/is/A0007), who wrote the party's op-ed, is now a member of Parliament.[15] The [Socialist Party](/is/A0001) gave an example scale in 2024: 2% above ISK 200 million for couples, rising to 9% above ISK 10 billion.[17] The Left-Green Movement's 2023 congress adopted a moderate wealth tax on individuals' assets that leaves out housing.[18] The union federation [BSRB](/is/A0009) called for one in a resolution in November 2021.[19]

The register holds no Pirate Party position on a wealth tax, and no poll.

As of 9 November 2026 no item before Parliament has a wealth tax in its title. The last items with the old tax in the title were questions put to the finance minister in 2015 to 2016 and 2023 to 2024, asking what it would have raised.[8][9][20] The proposals above are party and union policy. None has become a bill.

## What Iceland shows {#lessons}

### The tax raised real money {#money}

Five assessments raised about ISK 37 billion. The minister's own tables say the 2013 rules, with thresholds moved up with prices, would have raised ISK 36.9 billion in 2023 alone.[9]

### A temporary tax is the weakest design {#temporary}

The 2009 tax was labelled temporary at birth. Every year the question was whether to renew it, and by 2013 the finance minister could stand in Parliament and point out that it had been presented as temporary.[10] A permanent tax with indexed thresholds, an additional charge on company equity and tight enforcement offshore would have stayed.

### Deleting a tax is a choice {#choice}

The 2004 repeal gave up ISK 3.7 billion a year.[1] Five years later the state needed money and wrote a new tax in a hurry.[3] The old tax needed a higher threshold, and the new one got it. Neither needed to be deleted.
