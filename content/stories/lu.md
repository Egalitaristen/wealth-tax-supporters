Luxembourg is the country that dropped the wealth tax for people and kept it for companies. The result is a test run. In 2024 the tax on companies raised EUR 1.10 billion.[3] In 2005 the government expected to give up about EUR 22 million a year by freeing households.[1] The companies pay, the banks among them stay, and the households have paid nothing on their wealth since 2006.[2]

## What the tax was {#tax}

The law was the 1934 German wealth tax law, brought to Luxembourg in 1941 and kept after the liberation.[1] Until 2005 it charged resident and non-resident individuals 0.5% of net wealth.[1] Half of one percent a year sounds small. On a fortune of EUR 20 million it is EUR 100,000 a year.

The state never leaned hard on it. Between 2000 and 2003 the whole tax, companies and individuals together, raised EUR 150 to 160 million a year. It fell to about EUR 134 million in 2004, and the 2006 budget pencilled in EUR 100 million. Private fortunes paid less than 20% of it, and over 56% of the receipts from 2000 to 2004 came from the financial sector.[1] The government put the cost of freeing individuals at about EUR 22 million a year.[1]

## How it was repealed {#repeal}

The repeal was a trade. The 2004 coalition agreement said that a 10% withholding tax on certain savings interest "calls for" scrapping the wealth tax on individuals. The prime minister, Jean-Claude Juncker, put it in plain words in August 2004: bank secrecy stays, a 10% levy on some interest arrives, and in return the wealth tax on private persons goes.[1]

The bill was filed on 19 October 2005 and became law on 23 December. Article 11 says that from assessment year 2006 the rules taxing individuals are repealed.[1][2] The finance minister of the day, Luc Frieden, later became prime minister.[11]

The committee report names the reasons. A 2003 committee on the financial centre wanted to bring "big fortunes" to Luxembourg and pointed to Belgium, where a friendlier regime was said to have drawn 7,000 Dutch families and 63,000 French taxpayers. The report listed the countries that had dropped the tax: Austria in 1994, Germany and Denmark in 1997, the Netherlands in 2001. It cited the cost of running the tax against its yield, and a 2004 ruling of the EU court against exit taxes.[1]

The professional chambers split. The Chamber of Private Employees doubted the "social merit" of the repeal while the authorities preached austerity. The Chamber of Civil Servants asked whether it was justified given the budget and told the government to take the bill back and think again. The Chamber of Commerce welcomed the package as a first step towards an onshore market for rich clients.[1]

None of this says the tax could not be collected, and the report names no loophole it wanted to close. It deleted the tax. It also admitted it could not put a number on the gains it hoped for from an inflow of capital.[1]

The swap was meant to break even. The new levy was to bring in about EUR 40 million a year against EUR 37 million lost, and the report says the state would take back with one hand what it gave up with the other.[1] That levy is owed on savings interest above a small-saver allowance of up to EUR 250.[1] It raised EUR 163 million in 2024.[3] Owners of large fortunes stopped paying a yearly charge on everything they own. Savers started paying a flat charge on the interest they earn.

## The tax that stayed {#companies}

Companies and other legal entities still pay net wealth tax. Individuals, including owners of sole proprietorships and partners in transparent firms, are out.[7] The scale is 0.5% up to EUR 500 million of net wealth, then EUR 2.5 million plus 0.05% of everything above.[6] A company with EUR 100 million pays EUR 500,000. A company with EUR 600 million pays EUR 2.5 million plus 0.05% of EUR 100 million, which is EUR 50,000, so EUR 2.55 million in all, an effective 0.425%. A company with EUR 5 billion pays EUR 4.75 million, an effective 0.095%.

That last line is a flaw, and the household tax should not copy it. A rate that falls to a tenth on the largest holdings charges a company worth EUR 5 billion less than a fifth of the rate it charges a company worth EUR 100 million.

Companies also owe a minimum amount. The Constitutional Court struck down part of the old flat minimum, and a government draft law then proposed three steps of EUR 535, EUR 1,605 and EUR 4,815 by balance sheet size.[17]

Now look at what the tax does. It raised EUR 385 million in 2015, EUR 771 million in 2019, EUR 1.10 billion in 2024 and EUR 985 million in 2025.[5] In 2024 that was 7.6% of the main taxes the tax office collected.[3] The finance minister says the jumps in 2023 and 2024 partly come from a few taxpayers assessed for several years at once.[5] The pattern still stands: in 2023 seven taxpayers each paid more than EUR 10 million, about EUR 150 million between them.[4] That is roughly seven times the EUR 22 million the state gave up when it freed every household.

And who pays? The financial sector paid EUR 846 million of the EUR 985 million collected in 2025, about 86%.[5] The banks and funds that opponents say would flee a wealth tax pay one every year, and they are still in Luxembourg.

## Who owns Luxembourg {#concentration}

The central bank's household survey of 3,699 households in 2023 puts median net wealth at EUR 676,000 and the average at EUR 1.16 million. The richest 1% of households own about 13% of all net wealth, the richest 5% own 33%, the richest 10% own 47% and the richest 20% own 63%. The poorer half of households own about 9%.[8]

The Chamber of Employees adds more. In 2021 the average net wealth of the richest 1% topped EUR 19 million. The richest 10% hold 71% of the wealth that produces income, and the poorer half hold less than 1% of it.[9] The chamber says the best-off households can arrange their wealth so that little of it shows up as taxable income while it keeps growing. At equal wealth, it says, households are taxed differently depending on whether the wealth shows up as income.[9] A tax on income cannot do the job of a tax on wealth.

## What has been proposed {#proposals}

Ahead of the 2023 election the LSAP, déi gréng and déi Lénk all favoured bringing the tax back for individuals, and the CSV, DP and ADR opposed it. The LSAP offered to study it, with the main home left out and an allowance of up to EUR 2.6 million. Déi Lénk wanted it back with household savings under EUR 200,000 and the main home exempt.[12] Déi gréng wrote a new tax on "important wealth" into their programme, with an allowance of EUR 1 million.[13] A study for the Greens in the European Parliament put a European tax at EUR 496 million a year for Luxembourg, 0.64% of GDP.[13]

On 13 November 2024 Sam Tanson of déi gréng moved a Chamber motion for a global minimum tax on the wealth of billionaires. It was refused, 40 votes to 20.[10] A month earlier the finance minister, Gilles Roth, had told the Chamber that the government does not intend to bring back a wealth tax on individuals, because income tax is progressive. He added that Luxembourg does not object to talks on international cooperation.[11] Progressive income tax does not answer the question. It reaches only the part of a fortune that shows up as income.

On 20 May 2026 déi gréng moved another motion, on the "breet Mëtt", the broad middle. It asked for extra top income brackets and a better balance between taxes on work and on capital. It was refused, 40 votes to 18 with two abstentions.[16]

On 11 June 2026 the Chamber of Employees gave its opinion on the government's reform of personal income tax. The reform, it said, leaves untouched the favoured treatment of capital income, the absence of tax on large fortunes and the lack of any tax on direct inheritances. It called a progressive wealth tax on very high fortunes "indispensable", with an exempt amount and with small savings and the main home left out. It cited research that a tax only on net wealth above EUR 2 million, which covers 8.9% of residents, could raise several percentage points of GDP.[9] On 28 October 2026 the Chamber passed the reform in its first vote, 37 to 11 with 12 abstentions.[15]

I checked the Chamber's pages on 9 November 2026. The last motion on a wealth tax that they show is 4406.[10]

## Who supports it {#who-supports}

Parties in the Chamber, the employees' chamber and a former Green MEP have taken a position. [déi Lénk](/lu/A0001) and [déi gréng](/lu/A0002) back a wealth tax on individuals, [Sam Tanson](/lu/A0004) moved the billionaire-tax motion, and the [Chamber of Employees](/lu/A0006) made the case in its 2026 opinion.[9][10] The LSAP has promised only a study, so it is not counted in the register yet.

## What a new Luxembourg tax would look like {#design}

The EU Tax Observatory sets out five lessons from Europe's old wealth taxes: tax every asset with no carve-outs, set a high threshold such as EUR 100 million, work as a minimum tax that tops up what a fortune already pays, add exit rules, and use today's automatic information exchange between countries to find assets abroad.[14] Apply them here and the design is clear.

The floors on the table run from EUR 200,000 of savings (déi Lénk) to EUR 1 million (déi gréng), EUR 2 million (the Chamber of Employees) and EUR 2.6 million (the LSAP).[9][12][13] A floor of EUR 2 million reaches 8.9% of residents.[9] The Observatory's floor of EUR 100 million reaches only the very top.[14] Whichever is chosen, count all assets at market value and leave out small savings and the main home, as the Chamber of Employees asks. Charge a fortune of EUR 5 billion at least the rate charged on one of EUR 100 million, not the 0.095% that the company scale charges. Pair it with an exit tax. The 2005 report treated an EU court ruling on exit taxes as a reason to give the tax up. Today France, Germany, Norway and Spain run exit taxes.[14] And a rich resident who stays and pays nothing adds nothing to the budget.

## What Luxembourg shows {#lessons}

### The tax can be collected {#collected}

Luxembourg collected EUR 1.10 billion from a wealth tax on companies in 2024, 80% of it from the financial sector.[3][5] The tax works. Households were let off.

### The repeal was a choice {#choice}

It was made in a package, with the bill filed on 19 October and the law dated 23 December 2005.[1][2] The civil servants' chamber told the government to take the bill back, and the Council of State would have preferred a fresh start. The report names no loophole that anyone tried to close first.[1]
