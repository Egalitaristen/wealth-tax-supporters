The United States has never had a federal tax on net worth, and the reason is a single clause. Article I of the Constitution says "direct taxes" must be shared among the states by population.[1] Nobody knows whether a wealth tax counts as direct, because the Supreme Court has never ruled on one. In 2024 it went out of its way to say that question was still open.[3] Congress has bills on the table, and California put the first statewide wealth tax on its 3 November 2026 ballot. The result is pending.[20]

## What the Constitution says {#constitution}

Article I, section 2 says "Representatives and direct Taxes shall be apportioned among the several States" by population. Section 9 repeats it: "No Capitation, or other direct, Tax shall be laid, unless in Proportion to the Census or enumeration."[1] Apportionment means a state with 5% of the people pays 5% of the tax, whatever its wealth. A state with less property per person would face a higher rate. The Court noted in 2024 that Congress appears not to have passed an apportioned tax since the Civil War.[3]

In 1895 the Court ruled in *Pollock v. Farmers' Loan and Trust* that a tax on the rents or income of real estate is a direct tax.[2] The country answered with the Sixteenth Amendment in 1913, which lets Congress tax incomes "without apportionment."[3] Property taxes count as direct. Income taxes do not.[3]

*Moore v. United States* (20 June 2024) upheld a 2017 tax on American shareholders of foreign companies. The Court called its decision "narrow" and listed what it was leaving alone: taxes on "holdings, wealth, or net worth." It also noted that the government itself had suggested an unapportioned tax on wealth "might be considered a tax on property, not income."[3] Five Justices joined the opinion, two more agreed with the result, and two dissented.[3] So no court has struck down a wealth tax. The question stays open until one reaches the Court.

## The federal bills {#bills}

Senator Elizabeth Warren introduced the Ultra-Millionaire Tax Act on 1 March 2021 with eight Senate cosponsors, and Representatives [Pramila Jayapal](/us/A0009) and [Brendan Boyle](/us/A0010) filed it in the House with 19.[4][5] The scale has not changed since: 2% of net worth between US$50 million and US$1 billion, and 3% above, rising to 6% if Congress enacts a universal health insurance program.[4] Jayapal reintroduced it in 2024 with 33 cosponsors and on 25 March 2026 with 39, and Warren's 2026 Senate bill has ten.[6][7][8] The [2026 bill](/resources/us-ultra-millionaire-tax-act-of-2026) taxes wealth on the last day of each year.[7]

A [fortune of US$200 million](/calculator?w=200000000&b=50000000:2,1000000000:3&c=US$) owes US$3.0 million a year under that scale, 1.5% of the fortune. A fortune of US$2 billion owes US$49 million, 2.5%.[7]

Representative Summer Lee's [Oligarch Act of 2025](/us/A0304) goes further: 2%, 4%, 6% and 8% as net worth passes 1, 10, 100 and 1,000 times a threshold set at 1,000 times median household wealth, with a US$50 million floor.[9] Senator [Bernie Sanders](/us/A0002) filed the [Make Billionaires Pay Their Fair Share Act](/resources/us-make-billionaires-pay-their-fair-share-act) on 2 March 2026, with Senator [Chris Van Hollen](/us/A0333). Representative [Ro Khanna](/us/A0115) carries it in the House.[10][11][12] It charges 5% a year of the whole net worth of anyone above US$1 billion, so a fortune of US$2 billion owes US$100 million a year.[10]

## What it would raise {#revenue}

At a Senate Finance subcommittee hearing on 27 April 2021, Warren put the Ultra-Millionaire Tax at US$3 trillion and said it would not raise taxes on 99.9% of Americans.[13] The economists Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman had found that about 100,000 families would owe it and that it would raise US$3.0 trillion over 2023 to 2032, about 1% of GDP a year. They had already assumed that taxpayers cut their bill by 15% through avoidance and evasion.[14]

The Penn Wharton Budget Model, a university group, scored US$2.1 trillion over 2022 to 2031 on the government's standard scoring rules. With the IRS enforcement money in the bill it reached US$2.4 trillion, and US$2.7 trillion over 2023 to 2032, the window of Warren's US$3 trillion.[15] The Tax Foundation's Scott Hodge gave the hearing his group's figure for Warren's proposal with a 6% top rate: nearly US$2.2 trillion in ten years.[13] Both models also charge the tax with a drag on the economy, 0.8% of GDP in the long run for the Tax Foundation and 1.2% by 2050 for Penn Wharton. After that charge, both still find it raises trillions.[13][15]

Estimates differ because they rest on different guesses about avoidance. Kyle Pomerleau of the American Enterprise Institute listed figures from US$366 billion to US$5.3 trillion over a decade.[13] Every one of them is a large sum. Senator Ron Wyden's 2021 plan to tax the gains of billionaires in publicly traded assets scored US$507 billion at Penn Wharton.[16]

## California's Proposition 40 {#california}

Proposition 40 is a one-time tax of 5% on the net worth of people with more than US$1 billion who lived in California on 1 January 2026, due in 2027. Real estate, pensions and retirement accounts are excluded, and 90% of the money goes to health care.[17] Its text taxes net worth in full at US$1.1 billion and phases the rate down by 0.1 point for every US$2 million below that. Payers can spread it over five years at a 7.5% yearly charge.[18] The Legislative Analyst expected "tens of billions of dollars" over several years.[17]

Suzanne Jimenez filed it as Initiative 25-0024 and amended it on 24 November 2025.[18] It needed 874,641 valid signatures. On 17 June 2026 the Secretary of State declared it eligible, after a sample projected more than the 962,106 needed.[19] The healthcare workers' union [SEIU-UHW](/us/A0023) funded the campaign, and the economist [Joseph Stiglitz](/us/A0525) signed the Nobel laureates' letter for it.

Proposition 40 was on the 3 November 2026 ballot, and the result is pending. Before the vote, a Berkeley IGS poll in August had 48% yes to 41% no among likely voters, and the Public Policy Institute of California's survey of 16 to 22 October had 46% yes.[22][24]

Two rival measures sat on the same ballot. Proposition 42 would ban new state taxes on personal property, including business interests, intellectual property and financial assets, for any new tax taking effect on or after 1 January 2026.[21] Proposition 41 would bar new state taxes that exclude their revenue from the spending limit.[28] Under the voter guide, if either gets more yes votes than a conflicting measure, the conflicting measure is void. The Legislative Analyst says Proposition 40 could be stopped from becoming law even with a majority of yes votes.[21][28]

## Other states {#states}

Washington's [House Bill 1319](/resources/us-house-bill-1319), by Representatives [Chipalo Street](/us/A0374), [Timm Ormsby](/us/A0375) and [Nicole Macri](/us/A0376), proposed 1% a year on financial assets above US$100 million, a base of about 3,400 residents.[25] Minnesota's HF 4616 (2026), by [Aisha Gomez](/us/A0402) and 16 other authors, proposes 1% of wealth above US$10 million. It was referred to the Taxes committee on 25 March 2026 and has had no further action.[26][27]

## What Americans say {#polls}

Most Americans, including most Republicans, back a tax on the super-rich when they see what it raises. The University of Maryland's Program for Public Consultation tested the Warren scale, 2% above US$50 million and 3% above US$1 billion, with its revenue shown. Nationally (2,509 adults, February 2026), 88% of Democrats and 72% of Republicans favored it, about 8 in 10 overall. In 11 battleground states and 28 House districts (19,949 adults, 21 July to 17 August 2026), 70% to 87% did.[23]

## Who supports it {#who-supports}

[Elizabeth Warren](/us/A0001), Jayapal, Boyle and Sanders lead in Congress, and 39 House members signed on to the 2026 bill.[7] The economists [Emmanuel Saez](/us/A0675) and [Gabriel Zucman](/us/A0679) wrote the revenue estimates, and the law professor [David Gamage](/us/A0676) testified for the tax in 2021.[13][14] The register holds 886 people. ## Four ways around the Constitution {#case}

The direct-tax clauses bind Congress. They do not reach a state legislature. And because the Court has left the federal question open, there are four routes.

### Write a tax that is not direct {#design}

The Court's own list of undecided questions includes wealth taxes.[3] Gamage told the Senate in 2021 that Congress "clearly has the power to levy an annual wealth tax," and that any doubt concerns only how the tax is designed.[13]

### Apportion it {#apportion}

Congress passed five apportioned Direct Tax Acts in the late 1700s and early 1800s, and Gamage knows of no one who has disputed that they were constitutional. He proposes a uniform wealth tax with an apportioned fallback written in, so that the tax stands under either reading.[13]

### Amend the Constitution {#amend}

Congress and the states answered *Pollock* with the Sixteenth Amendment, ratified in 1913.[3] They can do it again.

### Start in the states {#states-route}

Proposition 40 shows the state route in use, and the Washington and Minnesota bills are in committee.[17][25][27] Propositions 41 and 42 show what a state campaign has to plan for: a conflicting measure is void if it gets more yes votes.[21][28]
