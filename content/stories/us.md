The United States has never had a federal tax on net worth. The obstacle is one clause: Article I says "direct taxes" must be shared among the states by population.[1] The Supreme Court has never decided whether a tax on wealth is direct, and in 2024 it wrote that wealth and net worth taxes raise questions it was not answering.[3] Congress has bills on the table, and California put the first statewide wealth tax on its 3 November 2026 ballot.[20]

## What the Constitution says {#constitution}

Article I, section 2 says "Representatives and direct Taxes shall be apportioned among the several States" by population. Section 9 repeats it: "No Capitation, or other direct, Tax shall be laid, unless in Proportion to the Census or enumeration."[1] Apportionment means a state with 5% of the people pays 5% of the tax, whatever its wealth. A state with less property per person would face a higher rate. The Court noted in 2024 that Congress appears not to have enacted an apportioned tax since the Civil War.[3]

In 1895 *Pollock v. Farmers' Loan and Trust* held that a tax on the rents or income of real estate is a direct tax.[2] The country answered with the Sixteenth Amendment in 1913, which lets Congress tax incomes "without apportionment."[3] The Court still calls property taxes direct and income taxes indirect.[3]

*Moore v. United States* (20 June 2024) upheld the 2017 Mandatory Repatriation Tax, which taxes American shareholders of foreign companies on those companies' undistributed income. Justice Kavanaugh wrote for five Justices; Justices Barrett and Alito agreed with the result and Justices Thomas and Gorsuch dissented.[3] The Court called its holding "narrow" and listed what it left alone: its analysis "does not address" taxes on "holdings, wealth, or net worth." It also recorded that the government itself suggested an unapportioned tax on wealth "might be considered a tax on property, not income."[3] So the Court has not struck down a wealth tax. The question stays open until a wealth tax reaches the Court.

## The federal bills {#bills}

Warren introduced the Ultra-Millionaire Tax Act on 1 March 2021 with eight Senate cosponsors, and Representatives [Pramila Jayapal](/us/A0009) and [Brendan Boyle](/us/A0010) filed it in the House with 19.[4][5] The scale has not changed since: 2% of net worth between US$50 million and US$1 billion, and 3% above, rising to 6% if Congress enacts a universal health insurance program.[4] Jayapal reintroduced it in 2024 with 33 cosponsors and on 25 March 2026 with 39, and Warren's 2026 Senate bill has ten.[6][7][8] The [2026 bill](/resources/us-ultra-millionaire-tax-act-of-2026) taxes wealth on the last day of each year.[7]

A [fortune of US$200 million](/calculator?w=200000000&b=50000000:2,1000000000:3&c=US$) owes US$3.0 million a year under that scale, 1.5% of the fortune. A fortune of US$2 billion owes US$49 million, 2.5%.[7]

Representative Summer Lee's [Oligarch Act of 2025](/us/A0304) goes further: 2%, 4%, 6% and 8% as net worth passes 1, 10, 100 and 1,000 times a threshold set at 1,000 times median household wealth, with a US$50 million floor.[9] Senator [Bernie Sanders](/us/A0002) filed the [Make Billionaires Pay Their Fair Share Act](/resources/us-make-billionaires-pay-their-fair-share-act) on 2 March 2026, with Senator [Chris Van Hollen](/us/A0333). Representative [Ro Khanna](/us/A0115) carries it in the House.[10][11][12] It charges 5% a year of the whole net worth of anyone above US$1 billion, so a fortune of US$2 billion owes US$100 million a year.[10]

## What it would raise {#revenue}

At the Senate Finance subcommittee hearing of 27 April 2021, which Warren chaired, she put the Ultra-Millionaire Tax at US$3 trillion and said it would not raise taxes on 99.9% of Americans.[13] Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman, writing to her in February 2021, found about 100,000 families liable and US$3.0 trillion over 2023 to 2032, about 1% of GDP a year. They assumed taxpayers cut their bill by 15% through avoidance and evasion.[14]

The Penn Wharton Budget Model scored US$2.1 trillion over 2022 to 2031 on government scoring conventions. With the IRS enforcement money in the bill it reached US$2.4 trillion, and US$2.7 trillion over 2023 to 2032, the window of Warren's US$3 trillion.[15] The Tax Foundation's Scott Hodge gave the hearing its model's figure for Warren's proposal with a 6% top rate: nearly US$2.2 trillion in ten years.[13] Both models find the tax raises trillions. They also report a cost to output: 0.8% of GDP in the long run for the Tax Foundation and 1.2% by 2050 for Penn Wharton.[13][15] Kyle Pomerleau of the American Enterprise Institute listed estimates from US$366 billion to US$5.3 trillion over a decade, a spread he put down to "wide variation in several assumptions."[13]

Senator Ron Wyden's 2021 Billionaires Income Tax, which taxes gains in publicly traded assets of filers above US$1 billion, scored US$507 billion at Penn Wharton.[16]

## California's Proposition 40 {#california}

Proposition 40 is a one-time tax of 5% on the net worth of people with more than US$1 billion who lived in California on 1 January 2026, due in 2027. Real estate, pensions and retirement accounts are excluded, and 90% of the money goes to health care.[17] Its text taxes net worth in full at US$1.1 billion and phases the rate down by 0.1 point for every US$2 million below that. Payers can spread it over five years at a 7.5% yearly charge.[18] The Legislative Analyst expected "tens of billions of dollars" over several years.[17]

Suzanne Jimenez filed it as Initiative 25-0024 and amended it on 24 November 2025.[18] It needed 874,641 valid signatures. On 17 June 2026 the Secretary of State declared it eligible, after a sample projected more than the 962,106 needed.[19] The healthcare workers' union [SEIU-UHW](/us/A0023) funded the campaign, and the economist [Joseph Stiglitz](/us/A0525) signed the Nobel laureates' letter for it.

It was on the 3 November 2026 ballot as Proposition 40.[20] The count is not final and the result is pending as of 8 November. Polls before the vote: 48% yes to 41% no among likely voters in August (Berkeley IGS), 52% yes in September, and 46% yes in the Public Policy Institute of California's survey of 16 to 22 October.[22][24]

Two competing initiatives sat on the same ballot. Proposition 42 "prohibits new state taxes on personal property, including business interests, intellectual property, and financial assets" and applies to new taxes that take effect on or after 1 January 2026.[21] Proposition 41 bars new state taxes that exclude their revenue from the spending limit.[28] The guide says of each: "States that other voter-approved measures appearing on the same ballot are void if they conflict with this measure and this measure receives more affirmative votes."[21][28] The Legislative Analyst adds that Proposition 40 "could be stopped from becoming law even if it gets yes votes from a majority of voters."[21]

## Other states {#states}

Washington's [House Bill 1319](/resources/us-house-bill-1319), by Representatives [Chipalo Street](/us/A0374), [Timm Ormsby](/us/A0375) and [Nicole Macri](/us/A0376), proposed 1% a year on financial assets above US$100 million, a base of about 3,400 residents.[25] Minnesota's HF 4616 (2026), by [Aisha Gomez](/us/A0402) and 16 other authors, proposes 1% of wealth above US$10 million. It was referred to the Taxes committee on 25 March 2026 and has had no further action.[26][27]

## What Americans say {#polls}

The University of Maryland's Program for Public Consultation tested the Warren scale, 2% above US$50 million and 3% above US$1 billion, with its revenue shown. Nationally (2,509 adults, February 2026), 88% of Democrats and 72% of Republicans favored it, about 8 in 10 overall. In 11 battleground states and 28 House districts (19,949 adults, 21 July to 17 August 2026), 70% to 87% did.[23]

## Who supports it {#who-supports}

[Elizabeth Warren](/us/A0001), Jayapal, Boyle and Sanders lead in Congress, and 39 House members signed on to the 2026 bill.[7] The economists [Emmanuel Saez](/us/A0675) and [Gabriel Zucman](/us/A0679) wrote the revenue estimates, and the law professor [David Gamage](/us/A0676) testified for the tax in 2021.[13][14] The register holds 886 people. ## The case {#case}

The direct-tax clauses limit Congress and do not reach a state legislature. The Court has left the federal question open, so four routes remain.

### Write a tax that is not direct {#design}

The Court's list of undecided questions includes wealth taxes.[3] Gamage told the Senate in 2021 that Congress "clearly has the power to levy an annual wealth tax," and that the doubt concerns only the design.[13]

### Apportion it {#apportion}

Gamage notes that Congress passed five apportioned Direct Tax Acts in the late 1700s and early 1800s, and to his knowledge no one has disputed that they were constitutional. He proposes a uniform wealth tax with an apportioned fallback written in.[13]

### Amend the Constitution {#amend}

Congress and the states answered *Pollock* with the Sixteenth Amendment, ratified in 1913.[3] The same route is open again.

### Start in the states {#states-route}

Proposition 40 shows the state route in use, and the Washington and Minnesota bills are in committee.[17][25][27] Proposition 41 and Proposition 42 show what a state campaign has to plan for: a conflicting measure is void if it gets more yes votes.[21][28]
