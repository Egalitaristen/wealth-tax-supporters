The objection pictures a saver: someone who earned a wage, paid tax on it, put what was left into assets, and now faces a second tax on the same money. For the very rich that picture is wrong. Of the $4.25 trillion held by US billionaires in 2021, about $2.7 trillion, 64%, was gains on shares they had not sold.[1] Nobody paid tax on those gains and nobody counted them as income. There is no first tax for a second one to double.

The rest of the objection fails too. Every tax we have is paid out of money that was taxed before. The OECD calls double taxation "far from unique" to wealth taxes and points out that consumption taxes are paid out of post-tax income.[4] And the minimum tax on billionaires is built so that nothing is taxed twice: anyone who already pays 2% of their wealth in income tax owes nothing extra.[6]

## Where the fortune comes from {#untaxed}

Money can only be taxed twice if it was taxed once. Look at what the largest fortunes are made of. In the United States, capital gains make up 50% of the wealth of households with more than $50 million and 54% of those with more than $100 million. About 90% of those gains are in shares of businesses. The economists who counted them write that such gains can wait decades untaxed, and often forever.[2]

The reason is how companies work. A company that keeps its profit instead of paying it out makes its shares worth more. The owner has been paid nothing, so there is no income to tax, and capital gains tax is only charged when the shares are sold. The listed companies of the 400 richest Americans paid out 19% of their pre-tax income as dividends in 2018 to 2020.[3] The other 81% stayed in the company, and the owner's share of it turned up as a higher share price.

{{chart: payout}}

Their income comes mostly from this: 34% from the profits of listed companies and 49% from the profits of private businesses, and only 16% from labour income and "pure" capital gains.[3] These fortunes are not saved-up pay. They are the owners' share of businesses, and the income tax sees about 40% of it, against about 70% for the whole population.[3]

The OECD draws the conclusion. Where wealth comes mainly from asset revaluation and capital gains are not adequately taxed, a wealth tax is not double taxation. For the very wealthy, it adds, part of the wealth comes from capital income taxed at rates that can be low, so double taxation is likely to be much more limited.[4] The pillar page shows [how that growth escapes tax](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#unrealised) and [how the owners live on loans](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#bank).

## Every tax is paid from money taxed before {#every}

Treat the objection as a rule and nothing survives it. A cleaner pays income tax on her wages, then VAT or sales tax when she spends what is left, then a yearly property tax on the flat she bought with taxed money. That property tax is no oddity: a recurrent tax on immovable property is charged in every OECD country and is the most common form of tax on property.[4] Nobody argues that her flat is double taxed and so should be exempt.

The OECD took up the objection in a section of its own. Multiple levels of taxation are everywhere, it says, and the objection is a popular one against wealth taxes in particular.[4] So the question to ask is not whether money was taxed before. It is whether the whole stack of taxes falls fairly. In the United States, it does not:

{{chart: all-taxes}}

The 400 richest Americans paid 24% of their income in all taxes in 2018 to 2020, counting corporate and business property taxes, against 30% for everyone and 45% for the top earners of wages.[3] The stack is lightest at the very top. The objection asks us to protect the people it is lightest for.

## A charge on the holding, like a property tax {#holding}

A wealth tax is a yearly charge on what you hold, whatever it earned. The Institute for Fiscal Studies, whose economists are sceptical of wealth taxes, shows the arithmetic: a tax of 20% on a normal 5% return takes £1 from £100 of savings, and so does a tax of 1% on the stock. Both raise the same whatever the savings actually earned.[5] It is built the same way as the property tax on a house, which is charged on the house and does not depend on the rent it could fetch.

The return is the part no other tax reaches. A billionaire's wealth has earned about 7.5% a year after inflation over four decades. They pay about 0.3% of their wealth in individual taxes a year, about 4% of their income.[6] A charge of a point or two on the holding takes a slice of that untaxed growth. It does not take a second slice of any wage.

## The minimum tax credits what is already paid {#minimum}

The simplest version of the tax answers this objection in its design. Under it every billionaire would pay at least 2% of their wealth a year in tax. Someone who already pays that owes nothing extra, and someone who pays less is topped up to 2%.[6] The tax is a floor under what is already paid. By construction it cannot take the same dollar twice.

What it changes is the return. A billionaire's 7.5% a year falls to 7.2% after the 0.3% they pay today, and to 5.5% with the minimum tax. They still get richer.[6]

What about the corporate tax their companies already pay? It is already inside the 24% above: corporate tax is 37% of the total tax payments of the 400 richest Americans.[3] Add up every tax, corporate and business property taxes included, and they paid 1.3% of their wealth in 2018 to 2020, which is below 2%. Their individual income taxes alone came to 0.6% of wealth.[3]

A straight wealth tax of 2% would come on top of any income tax paid. The minimum tax is the version that only offsets the failure of the income tax, when it fails.[6] Either way it is aimed at people who pay little: a minimum at $1 billion reaches about 3,000 taxpayers on Earth.[6] The pillar page sets out [the minimum tax](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#minimum).

## And the saver? {#saver}

The objection is at its strongest for someone who saved from wages. That is why the line is drawn at $1 billion. The OECD notes that a wealth tax levied only on the very wealthy might not generate much double taxation in practice.[4] A teacher with a paid-off house and a pension is not the target. Where an owner has the wealth but not the cash, the page on [owners with no cash](/arguments/no-cash) shows how the tax is paid.

## What follows {#design}

The objection fails in three places. The wealth it protects was mostly never taxed once. The test it sets would abolish every tax we have. And the minimum tax is designed to credit what a billionaire already pays. What is left is a plain question: should the people with the fastest growing fortunes pay about 0.3% of their wealth a year, or at least 2%?
