A cleaner on the minimum wage pays income tax on her wage, payroll tax on the same wage, and sales tax or VAT when she spends what is left. If she owns a flat she pays property tax on it every year, with money that was taxed twice already. Nobody calls the property tax unfair because the flat was bought with taxed income. The objection to a wealth tax says that the richest, uniquely, should not pay a tax on money that was taxed before.

## What "double" would have to mean {#meaning}

If double taxation were a reason to refuse a tax, no tax in the system would survive. Income tax takes a share of the wage, VAT takes a share of what the wage buys, and property tax takes a share of the house bought with it. Each tax falls on money that an earlier tax already reached. A tax system is a stack of them, and the question is whether the stack falls on the people who can carry it.

The richest Americans carry less of it than the population as a whole. In 2018 to 2020 the 400 richest paid total taxes equal to 23.8% of their economic income, against 30.2% for the whole population.[1]

## The money was not all taxed {#untaxed}

The premise of the objection is that wealth is saved after-tax income. For the largest fortunes it is mostly something else.

Saez, Yagan and Zucman estimate that for households with more than US$50 million, capital gains are 50% of wealth, and that about 90% of those gains are in business shares.[2] In 2010 about 44% of the value of estates above US$20 million was unrealised capital gains, in US Treasury estate data as the same authors report it.[2] An unrealised gain has not been taxed once, let alone twice.

The gain stays untaxed for as long as the owner holds the asset. If a wealthy investor never sells stock that has risen in value, the White House Council of Economic Advisers explains, the gains are wiped out for income tax purposes when the assets pass to the heirs under the stepped-up basis.[3] Wages, by contrast, are taxed as they are paid. The 400 richest Americans earn much of their income inside companies they own. Taxable income was about 40% of their economic income in 2018 to 2020, against about 70% for the whole population.[1]

## An annual levy on holding {#holding}

A wealth tax is a recurrent tax on net wealth, charged whatever the assets earned that year.[4] The OECD describes it as equivalent to a proportional tax on a presumed return, and notes that income taxes are typically levied on realisation while a wealth tax is levied on an accrual basis.[4] The Mirrlees Review gives the arithmetic: a 20% tax on a 5% return is the same as a 1% tax on the stock.[5]

The tax hits the return as it accrues. For the largest fortunes the return accrues as unrealised gain, which no other tax touches until sale, and which the stepped-up basis wipes out at death.[3] A wealth tax charges the owner each year for what the owner holds, the way a property tax charges the owner of a flat.

## The fair part of the objection {#fair}

One part of the complaint is true. A wealth tax is charged irrespective of the actual return, so an owner whose assets fell in value still owes it.[4] The same holds for property tax, which is due in a year when the house lost value. The rate is the answer: at 1% to 2% of the stock, set against a normal return of 5%, the bill is a fifth to two fifths of a typical year's return.[5] A year of loss is the exception that a deferral rule can absorb.

The OECD adds a view that a fair page has to report: that there are "limited arguments for having a net wealth tax in addition to broad-based personal capital income taxes and well-designed inheritance and gift taxes". It adds that there are "stronger arguments" for one where those taxes are absent.[4] The figures above show that at the top the income tax base is narrow and gains go untaxed. The OECD's second case applies.

## What follows from it {#design}

The taxes we already have fail the test the objection sets: wages, purchases and homes are taxed on money that was taxed before. A wealth tax reaches the part of a fortune that no other tax has reached. The 400 richest Americans pay a lower total rate than the average American, on a base that is largely untaxed gain.[1][2] The design follows: value assets at market value, do not exempt the largest holdings, and let owners defer when cash is short.
