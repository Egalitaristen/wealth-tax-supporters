The four taxes in this objection have different names for a reason. Each is charged on something else, at a different moment, and none of them is a tax on what a person owns.

## What each one reaches {#bases}

A property tax is a recurrent tax on immovable property.[4] It reaches the home, the land and the office. In the data on US households, owner-occupied homes are 7.0% of the assets of the top 0.1%, while corporate shares and mutual funds are 57% and business equity 17%.[1] The same households hold 3.9% of all owner-occupied real estate and 28.5% of all household business equity.[1] The Federal Reserve's real estate line is owner-occupied housing only, and rented-out property sits in business equity, so the 7% is a floor for real estate and not a total. A property tax reaches the homes among those assets and none of the shares.

A capital gains tax is charged when an asset is sold. Dividends and gains face a top rate of 20% in the United States, 23.8% with the investment surtax.[6] A gain on an asset that is never sold is never charged, and at death it is wiped out for income tax by the stepped-up basis.[6] Saez, Yagan and Zucman find that for households with more than US$50 million, capital gains are 50% of wealth, about 90% of the gains are in business shares and residences are only 2% of the unrealised gains of the rich.[2] A tax on gains waits for a sale. The largest holdings are not sold.

An estate or inheritance tax is charged once per death, on what is in the estate. In the Balkir, Saez, Yagan and Zucman study the top-400 decedents paid 0.8% of their wealth in estate tax when married and 7% when single, and their gross estates were about 39% of the estimate of their wealth.[3]

A net wealth tax is charged on the whole stock, net of debts, every year. The OECD separates it from taxes on capital income and taxes on wealth transfers, and notes that, compared with recurrent property taxes, it covers a broad range of property with debts deductible.[4]

## Why a property tax cannot stand in {#substitute}

France ran the experiment. Until 2017 it levied the ISF, a net wealth tax. In 2018 it replaced it with the IFI, a tax on real estate above €1.3 million. The ISF raised €4.2 billion in 2017. The IFI raised €1.29 billion in 2018, and the evaluation committee put the loss against the ISF at €3.1 billion that year.[5] Two thirds of the households that paid the ISF owed nothing under the new tax, and across all former ISF payers the average bill fell from €8,000 in 2017 to €1,600 in 2018.[5]

A real estate tax reaches real estate. The households that had paid the ISF held their wealth elsewhere, and the replacement did not reach it.

## Why they work together {#together}

The OECD does not say the taxes compete. It writes that there is "a strong case for an accompanying inheritance tax on efficiency, equity and administrative grounds."[4] The Mirrlees Review reaches a different conclusion on the annual tax: it argued against it in Britain.[7] Both agree on what the taxes are: separate instruments aimed at separate bases.

The OECD's view on the combination is that there are "limited arguments for having a net wealth tax in addition to broad-based personal capital income taxes and well-designed inheritance and gift taxes", and "stronger arguments" where those are not in place.[4] A gains tax charged only on sale, with the stepped-up basis at death, is not a broad-based personal capital income tax at the top.[2][6]

## What follows {#design}

A wealth tax fills the gap the others leave. It is charged on the shares and private companies where the largest holdings sit, every year, whether or not anything is sold or inherited. It sits alongside a property tax on homes, a gains tax on sales and an estate tax at death. A country that has all four loses nothing by adding the fourth.
