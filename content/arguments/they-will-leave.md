A billionaire's fortune is not a pile of money that fits in a suitcase. It is shares in companies: factories, shops, software, jobs. In Sweden, 37% of the top 0.1% of the wealth ladder own a company, and the firms owned by the top 2% employ 9.2% of everyone in the country.[9] A person can move to Switzerland. The company cannot. Its workers, its customers and the law it lives under stay where they are, and so does the profit the company makes.

That is why the numbers are small. Norway raised its wealth tax in 2022 and the headlines said the rich were fleeing. A study of the full reform counts at most 100 people who left because of it, almost all in 2022 and 2023, and finds that their leaving cost the tax about 2% of its revenue.[4] Over the same three years the receipts of the tax rose from NOK 18.5 billion to NOK 31.8 billion, an increase of 72%.[1] Without the leavers the rise would have been 75%. The tax grew by nearly the same amount either way. The few who move take little with them, and the law can make them take less still: a tax that follows the person abroad for years leaves nothing to gain from the trip. A rich person who stays and pays nothing was never an asset, so the one who leaves with nothing owed is no loss.

## A fortune is tied to a place {#fortune}

More than 90% of the world's billionaires live in the country they are citizens of.[11] The reason is the one above. Most of their wealth is an ownership stake in a company, and a company is tied to its workers, its customers and its courts.

Norway shows what that means after an owner moves. In May 2026 the broadcaster NRK counted 163 business owners who had moved to Switzerland since 2020. Their companies are still Norwegian. The combined equity of those companies roughly doubled from 2019 to 2024, and the dividends the owners received from them rose from NOK 2.3 billion to NOK 6.5 billion. The movers paid about NOK 310 million of other Norwegian taxes in 2024, because the profit is made in Norway and taxed there. Only six of them had sold their stakes completely.[5]

The same fact limits the people it could tempt. A founder who moves abroad and keeps running a company at home pays for it. One study of Norway finds that the firms of owners who leave make 12.6% less revenue than those whose owners stay.[7] Few people accept that to avoid a yearly tax.

## What happened when countries counted {#counts}

**Norway.** The Finance Ministry counted the people worth more than NOK 100 million who moved abroad: 48 in 2022 and 34 in 2023. Together they held NOK 45.7 billion of taxable wealth.[2] Norway's taxable net wealth in 2022 was NOK 3,223 billion, so the whole 'exodus' held 1.4% of it.[1] The wider study of the reform finds at most 100 people left because of it, mostly in the top 0.1% of wealth and mostly in 2022 and 2023. Their owners' firms stayed active, and the study found no sign that firms owned by wealth tax payers paid out more, invested less or went bust more often after the reforms, and no fall in the number of startup founders moving to Norway.[4]

The loudest figure on the other side comes from a think tank that campaigns against taxes on ownership. It counted 17 people a year with more than NOK 50 million moving out under the previous government and 77 a year in 2022 and 2023, and it says itself that it has not tested whether the tax was the cause.[6] Take the number as it stands. Seventy-seven people are a rounding error in a tax that 719,007 people paid in 2024.[1] Meanwhile the number of payers rose by 8%, from 667,022 to 719,007, and the money collected by 72%.[1]

**Sweden and Denmark.** Researchers followed every wealthy taxpayer in the two countries. In Denmark, about 0.4% of the top 0.1% of the wealth ladder moved abroad each year, and the net movement was close to zero. In Sweden, more rich people moved in than out in the years before the tax was repealed.[9] When Sweden repealed its tax, the chance that a wealthy person left in a given year fell by 0.05 percentage points, a 30% fall.[9] That is the whole of the 'flight' Sweden bought by giving up the tax. The same study finds that a rise of one percentage point in the top rate shrinks the number of wealthy taxpayers by about 2%.[9] Raising a rate from 1% to 2% doubles the rate, and the base shrinks by a fiftieth.

**France.** The old wealth tax lost 1,150 payers to other countries in 2014 and 760 in 2017, when there were about 360,000 payers. That is 0.2% a year.[8] After France replaced it in 2018 with a tax on real estate alone, about 260 payers a year left against about 130,000 payers. The rate was 0.2% again.[8] France took away the tax and the rate of leaving did not move.

**Britain.** When the country tightened the tax rules for rich residents with a foreign domicile in 2017, 0.2% of those who had lived there a long time and 2% of the British-born among them left. The researchers' estimate is that ending the special regime would send fewer than 100 people abroad and raise £3.2 billion a year, 84% more than it brings in now.[13]

## Few leave, and the revenue stays {#revenue}

A count of people says little. What matters to the budget is how much revenue they take with them.

{{chart: norway-receipts}}

Take Norway. Had no one left, the receipts would have risen 75% between 2021 and 2024. With the people who did leave, they rose 71.5%.[4] The difference, 1.715 against 1.75, is 2% of the revenue. The 163 business owners who moved to Switzerland had paid NOK 343 million of wealth tax in total before they went, which is 1.1% of the NOK 31.8 billion collected in 2024.[5][1] Of the 163, 41 owed no wealth tax at all in 2019 because they were under the threshold.[5]

{{chart: what-left}}

France shows the same thing from the other side. The households that left in 2021 had paid €3.8 million of the real estate tax in 2020, 0.2% of what it raised. The average bill of a household that left was €14,000.[8] Replacing the wealth tax with that tax cost the state about €3.1 billion in 2018, the first year, when the wealth tax had raised €4.2 billion in 2017. By 2022 the state's own evaluation committee put the loss at €4.5 billion: it estimates the old tax would have raised €6.3 billion that year, against the €1.83 billion the real estate tax raised.[8] France gave up billions to stop a leak of millions.

{{chart: france-leavers}}

Net of the people who return, the leak was smaller still. Under the wealth tax, France lost about 950 payers a year to other countries from 2011 to 2016 and gained back 370. Under its replacement, about 260 left each year from 2018 to 2021 and about 380 came back.[8] The committee's own judgement of the budget stake in all this: probably secondary.[8]

## A rich person who leaves and pays nothing is no loss {#nothing}

The objection treats a billionaire living in the country as the prize. What a country gets from the very rich is tax, jobs and investment. A rich resident who pays no tax on their wealth, as the world's billionaires pay about 0.3% of it a year, gives none of the first.[11] On the other two, the Norwegian study finds that the firms of wealth tax payers did not invest less or pay out more after the reforms.[4] So the jobs stay with the companies, and the revenue is what the owners who stay now pay. See [why the tax is needed at all](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#bank).

## The tax follows the person {#follows}

Leaving a country does not need to end what you owe it. Norway shows how far it can go.

- Until late 2022, the exit tax on gains made at home lapsed five years after a person left. Since the 2023 budget it no longer lapses.
- Since March 2024, a person who leaves has 12 years to pay the bill, in instalments or deferred with interest, and the right to have it waived on return is limited to those 12 years.
- The Finance Ministry expects the exit tax to bring in about NOK 100 million in 2026, rising to about NOK 1.2 billion a year after 12 years.[3]

A company charge does the same job for owners. The Princeton study that found the largest figures on Norway's side, a share of affected households leaving of more than 2% in the reform year and a long-run loss of output of 1.3%, tests a charge on the market value of a firm when its owner leaves. It finds that the charge curbs tax flight, most of all among the most productive entrepreneurs, and raises output.[7] The problem it measures has a design answer, and the answer is in the study.

Seven Nobel prize winners in economics proposed it for France in July 2025: anyone who leaves stays liable for the minimum tax for five years in the bill the National Assembly passed, and ten years, they wrote, would cut the risk of leaving further.[12] They did not want France to wait for other countries either.[12]

The widest version is a tax that follows long-time residents who move to a country that does not collect. A person who has lived in a country for decades and become rich there would owe a minimum tax for a number of years after moving, with the tax they pay abroad deducted, so nobody pays twice. The countries that take part act as collector of last resort, and the bank data to enforce it already crosses borders under the existing automatic exchange between tax offices.[11]

## One country does not have to do it alone {#minimum}

A minimum tax on billionaires, agreed by a group of countries, changes the arithmetic. If the countries where billionaires live all charge the same floor, there is no cheap place to run to. The report for the G20 puts it plainly: with a common standard, differences in effective tax rates between countries would be small, and a critical mass of countries is enough to stop a race to the bottom.[11] Participating countries can also tax the assets that billionaires who live elsewhere still hold inside their borders: property, bank accounts and the share of their companies' assets located there.[11]

Switzerland's cantons show how it works inside a country. Switzerland has the highest share of tax revenue from an annual wealth tax in the OECD. When a canton cuts its rate, about a quarter of the response is people moving in from other cantons.[10] A floor set for all the cantons leaves nobody a cheaper canton to move to. The same goes for countries. [The minimum tax](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#minimum) is built on this.

Switzerland is also where leavers go. It was the top destination of the French real estate tax payers who left in 2021, with 19% of them, and the 163 Norwegian business owners above went there.[8][5] It is a country that levies a wealth tax every year.[10]

## What the design has to do {#design}

- **Keep taxing people after they leave** for ten years or more, with a credit for what they pay abroad.
- **Charge the unrealised gains when someone leaves**, and on the market value of a company when its owner goes, so that moving is no way to wipe a gain out.
- **Count every asset at market value.** Every discount in the rules is a reason to rearrange what you own, and no reason to move.
- **Set the threshold high.** A minimum tax on billionaires reaches about 3,000 people in the world.[11] The fewer the people, the easier every one of them is to follow.
- **Count departures properly.** The Finance Ministry's own count of movers is not audited, which is a data fault any government can fix.[2]

Ten countries levy an annual tax on net wealth today, Norway and Switzerland among them.[14] The rich who do leave are the case the exit tax, the common floor and market valuation are built for.
