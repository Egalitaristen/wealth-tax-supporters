"Enough" depends on what the tax is measured against. A wealth tax is not meant to pay for the whole of government. It is meant to collect from the people who now pay almost nothing, and the amount there is large.

Billionaires pay about 0.3% of their wealth a year in individual taxes. A minimum tax of 2% would take that up to a floor, and it would raise $200 billion to $250 billion a year worldwide from about 3,000 people.[1] Against the world's $111.7 trillion of GDP in 2024, that is about 0.2%.[4] And the money is not a new burden on the rich. It is the gap between what they pay now and what everyone else already does.[1]

The tax also does a second job that no sum of money can do afterwards. It slows the speed at which the largest fortunes compound, from 7.1% a year to about 5.4%, so that a few thousand people do not take a larger and larger share of the economy each decade.[1] The rest of this page shows the numbers, what they pay for, and why the tax is judged by what it does and not by whether it alone covers a budget.

## What a tax on the largest fortunes raises {#revenue}

Where wealth taxes exist, they have raised between 0.2% of GDP in Spain and 1.0% in Switzerland, in the OECD's count for 2016.[2] Norway's raised NOK 31.8 billion in 2024 from 719,007 payers. That is 0.59% of GDP, and 2.9% of the current taxes on income and wealth that Norway's public sector received, oil included. Its receipts rose 72% in three years, from NOK 18.5 billion in 2021.[3]

A minimum tax at $1 billion lands in the same range, from far fewer people. The report that designed it counts about 2,800 billionaires in 2024, worth $14.2 trillion together. They pay about 0.3% of that wealth in individual taxes today, which is about $43 billion a year. A floor of 2% lifts it to about $284 billion, a net gain of $242 billion if it is well enforced and $193 billion if a fifth is lost to avoidance and evasion.[1] The report rounds these two cases to its headline of $200 billion to $250 billion.

{{chart: yield-gdp}}

Spread that over the taxpayers. Norway collects about NOK 44,000 on average from each of its 719,007 payers.[3] The minimum tax would collect roughly $80 million to $90 million from each billionaire, from about 3,000 returns, where Norway checks 719,007.[1][3] The OECD worried in 2018 that wealth taxes cost too much to run against the limited revenue they bring in.[2] That worry was about taxes that reach hundreds of thousands of payers and leave the biggest fortunes out. This one reaches the biggest fortunes and almost no one else.

## Why some taxes raised little, and why that is not the ceiling {#low}

The wealth taxes that raised little were taxes with holes. Sweden exempted the shares in privately held companies, which is where the biggest fortunes sat. Norway counts those shares for only about a tenth of their market value. A study of Norway finds that at full market value, wealth tax payments would be an order of magnitude larger than they are.[9] Billionaires in Norway pay an effective rate of no more than 0.3% of their wealth, against a top statutory rate of 0.85%.[1]

So 0.59% of GDP is what a leaky tax raises. Switzerland, whose thresholds are comparatively low and base broad, raised 1.0%.[2] The minimum tax is built to close the leak at the source: it is a floor on what a billionaire pays in all taxes together, through whatever instrument a country prefers, so no exemption can lower it.[1] Someone who already pays more than 2% of their wealth owes nothing extra. Only those who pay little are asked for more.[1]

## What the money pays for {#funds}

Two numbers show the scale. The yearly financing gap for school education in the 79 poorest countries is $97 billion on average from 2023 to 2030, 21% of what those countries need to spend.[5] The UN puts the cost of ending hunger by 2030 at $93 billion a year.[6] Together that is $190 billion a year, and the minimum tax's low-end estimate is $193 billion.

{{chart: what-it-funds}}

These are estimates from different bodies, and the tax would be spent where governments choose. They show what the order of magnitude buys. The sum is large enough to pay two of the world's biggest unfunded bills with money to spare, from fewer than 3,000 people.

The same works at a smaller scale. California has about 250 billionaires worth $2.3 trillion together. A one-time tax of 5% on their wealth would raise about $100 billion, as much as a 5% income tax on every California taxpayer. From 2019 to 2025 those billionaires paid California income tax equal to 1.6% of the $1.4 trillion their wealth grew. The letter pointed to the Medicaid cuts of 2025 as what the money would help cover.[7] In Europe alone, Nobel prize winners in economics put the yield of a 2% minimum tax at about $50 billion.[8]

## The number is a floor and responds to design {#design}

The estimate is for the first year, with the most cautious assumptions. Wealth is a stock, so in the first year billionaires cannot simply spend it away. The low end assumes that 20% of the revenue is lost to avoidance and evasion, more than the 15% usually used to score wealth tax proposals. It also uses the average billionaire's tax, and a note in the report says that counting each taxpayer separately would raise more, since the minimum tax is worked out person by person.[1]

The rate and the threshold are the dials. At 1% the tax on billionaires raises $80 billion to $100 billion. At 3% it raises $307 billion to $384 billion. A 2% floor extended to everyone worth more than $100 million adds $108 billion to $135 billion, for $302 billion to $377 billion in all.[1] A country that finds it wants more can ask for more, and the report finds the rate has a large and non-linear effect on revenue.[1]

## The second job: slowing the compounding {#compounding}

Since 1987 the wealth of the world's billionaires has grown 7.1% a year after inflation. Average wealth grew 3.2% a year and average income 1.3%.[1] At 7.1% a fortune doubles in about 10 years. Behind that rise is a return of about 7.5% a year, less the 0.3% of wealth the billionaires pay and the 0.1% they spend.[1]

With a well enforced 2% minimum tax the same fortune grows 5.4% a year, and doubles in about 13 years. That is our arithmetic from the report's figures, and the report arrives at the same place: a net return that falls from 7.2% to 5.5%, close to the 5% to 6% the average person earns on savings.[1]

{{chart: growth-slowed}}

This is why counting the revenue alone misses the point. In 1987 the richest 0.0001% of households, about 3,000 of them, held wealth equal to 3% of world GDP. In 2024 it was more than 13%.[1] A fortune that outgrows the economy that much stops being a prize and becomes a power. Nobel prize winners in economics wrote to California voters that billionaires gave about 1% of federal election donations in the 2000 cycle and 19% in 2024.[7] A tax that holds down the speed of that growth does something spending cannot do later: it keeps the decision with voters. The pillar page sets this out in [great fortunes buy influence](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#power).

## No tax is enough on its own, and we accept smaller ones {#smaller}

No single tax pays for everything. Inheritance and gift taxes are in force in 26 of the 35 OECD countries and raise 0.4% of all tax revenue on average.[2] The wealth taxes of 2016 raised from 0.5% of all taxes in France to 3.7% in Switzerland, with Norway at 1.1%.[2] The global minimum tax of 15% on multinational companies is expected to raise sums similar to this one, if anything slightly smaller.[1] Nobody says a tax is useless because it raises one slice of a budget.

The test of a tax is what it fixes, and what it costs per euro to collect. This one fixes a regressive hole. In France the working class, the middle class and most of the top 1% pay close to the national rate of 52% of their income in all taxes. Billionaires pay 27%, about half.[1] On average people pay the equivalent of 1.1% of their wealth in individual capital taxes, including property taxes on their homes, and billionaires pay a quarter of that.[1] The $200 billion to $250 billion a year is the amount that gap leaves uncollected: the report calls it the mechanical loss from under-taxing billionaires, against a standard where they pay no less than everyone else.[1]

## What follows {#follows}

A minimum tax on about 3,000 people raises about 0.2% of world GDP, and more than two of the world's largest unfunded bills combined. It does so by closing a gap in the tax system and not by taxing anything new. Its second effect, slowing fortunes that now grow five times faster than incomes, is one money cannot buy back.
