The objection measures a wealth tax against a government budget and finds it short. Nobody proposes to fund a government by taxing the richest 2%. A wealth tax has two jobs, and the objection counts only one of them.

## Enough to fund what {#revenue}

The projections are large against the programmes they are earmarked for. Saez and Zucman estimate that Warren's tax would raise about US$3.0 trillion over 2023 to 2032, about 1% of GDP a year, after assuming that taxpayers cut their bill by 15% through avoidance and evasion.[2] The Penn Wharton Budget Model scored US$2.1 trillion over 2022 to 2031 on government scoring conventions, US$2.4 trillion with the enforcement money in the bill.[3]

In Uruguay, De Rosa and Vilá estimate that a 1% tax on the top 1% would raise 0.75% to 1% of GDP, US$600 to 800 million a year, with the 17% they expect to be lost to evasion already counted.[4] A minimum tax of 2% on fortunes above US$100 million would raise about US$260 million, 0.4% of GDP.[5] The Danish Social Democrats' 2026 proposal was tied to smaller school classes, and the Uruguayan union commission's to a fund against child poverty.[12][13]

The taxes that exist raise similar sums. The OECD found that net wealth taxes brought in between 0.2% of GDP in Spain and 1.0% in Switzerland in 2016.[8] Norway's wealth tax was 0.59% of GDP in 2024, a ratio we calculate from Statistics Norway's tables and which includes oil in GDP.[7] Colombia collected COP 1.22 trillion from 34,229 filers in 2025.[6] Bolivia collected about Bs 960 million between 2021 and October 2025.[10]

## The unfavourable side {#unfavourable}

Some existing taxes raised little. Sweden's wealth tax came to 0.16% of GDP in 2006, its last full year, and never exceeded 0.4% in the postwar period.[9] Bolivia's yield fell from Bs 241 million in 2021 to Bs 169 million in 2024.[10] The German estimates vary a great deal with the design: DIW puts the Left's scale at €147 billion a year before behavioural responses, and the Left's own motion says about €100 billion after them.[11] These are the figures from the design with the highest rates. A tax that raises 0.2% of GDP is not enough for most programmes, and the size depends on the base: Switzerland raises more than Spain because its thresholds are lower and its base broader.[8]

## The second job: the compounding {#compounding}

The wealth of the top 0.1% of US households grew from US$6.8 trillion in 2006 to US$27.9 trillion in 2026, a factor of 4.1. Over the same 20 years nominal GDP grew by a factor of 2.4.[1] That is 7.3% a year against 4.4%, calculated from the Federal Reserve and FRED figures.[1] Over the last ten years the gap is wider: 9.9% a year against 5.7%.

A wealth tax subtracts from that growth. At a rate of 2% a year it takes the top 0.1% from 7.3% growth to about 5.3%, still above the economy. At about 3% it takes growth to about 4.3%, close to the economy's 4.4%, and at that point the group's share of national wealth stops rising if the tax applies to the whole group. The calculation ignores consumption, gifts and behaviour, and it is for one group in one country. It shows the order of magnitude: Warren's top rate is 3%.

This is why the revenue measure is incomplete. A tax that raises 1% of GDP and holds the top fortunes to the pace of the economy has done something that no amount of revenue buys back later.

## What reversing it takes {#reverse}

The same arithmetic runs further. A tax above the growth gap would shrink the group's share of wealth. Reversing the rise takes a rate above the gap and an enforcement system that finds the assets. A tax set at 1% does not do it, and a tax set at 3% to 4% on the largest fortunes would, as a calculation that ignores behaviour.
