Picture a bakery, a farm or a workshop that has been in one family for three generations. Whether a wealth tax touches it depends on the threshold. A minimum tax that starts at $1 billion reaches about 3,000 people on Earth.[1] A workshop worth $2 million is a five-hundredth of that line. The tax is also charged on the owner's holding and not on the firm, and at 1% of the holding a year it takes a fifth of what a normal 5% return brings in.[17]

The firms that do sit above the line are not the ones the objection pictures. Their owners earn about 7.5% a year on their wealth after inflation and pay tax equal to 0.3% of it.[1] Countries that tried to protect family firms by exempting them found that the exemption became the way out for the richest. In Spain the share of closely held stock that the top 0.01% of wealth holders claimed as exempt went from 15% in 1994 to 77% in 2002, while the total they held stayed the same.[7]

Where the tax was raised on business owners, their firms carried on. Norway nearly doubled its top tax rate on business wealth between 2021 and 2023, from 0.47% to 0.88%.[2] A study of the firms owned by people who pay finds no sign that they paid out more, invested less or went bankrupt more often.[2] So the design is a high threshold, no exemption, and time to pay for an owner who is rich in shares and short of cash.

## Who actually pays {#who-pays}

The threshold decides. In Norway it is NOK 1.9 million for a single person, and 719,007 of the 4,805,655 people with a wealth figure paid in 2024, 15.0% of them.[9][11] In the Swiss canton of Bern, where thresholds are low, 30% of all taxpayers owed wealth tax between 2001 and 2011.[6] In France it was slightly less than 1% of tax households in 2016.[6] In Spain the number of payers fell from 981,498 in 2007 to 188,680 in 2015 as the thresholds were raised.[6] Colombia's tax had 34,229 filers in 2025, and Bolivia had 263 registered payers at the end of October 2025.[12][13]

{{chart: who-pays}}

A low threshold reaches the middle class, and with it the people who own a farm or a shop that has risen in value and brings in little cash. Saez and Zucman name this as the weakness of the old European taxes: low thresholds that left moderately wealthy owners with a bill and little cash. The cleanest answer, they write, is a threshold high enough that mere millionaires do not owe anything. The Warren wealth tax they studied starts at $50 million, 50 times a typical European threshold, and less than 0.1% of US families would pay.[7] The minimum tax on billionaires goes further. It reaches about 3,000 people, and about half of what they own is shares of listed companies, with the rest mostly large private firms.[1]

Those are the two kinds of "family firm". One is a workshop or a farm worth a few million that employs the family and some neighbours. The other is a company worth billions that happens to be owned by a family. A threshold at $1 billion is 200 times a $5 million firm. It leaves the first alone and asks the second for a share of what it earns.

## What the tax costs a firm {#cost}

The tax is charged on the owner's shares. The company pays the same corporate tax with or without it, and its workers, its plant and its payroll are not part of the owner's bill. For why it does not slow investment, see [it hurts growth](/arguments/hurts-growth).

What the owner pays is small against what the holding earns. A tax of 1% on the value of a holding that earns a normal 5% a year takes one fifth of the return, whatever the holding earned that year.[17] For the owners of firms worth billions the return is higher. Billionaires have earned 7.5% a year on their wealth since the 1980s and pay tax equal to 0.3% of it, which is 4 cents of every dollar they earn. A minimum tax of 2% takes just over a quarter of that return and leaves them 5.5% a year.[1] Take a family firm worth $2 billion. At 7.5% it earns its owners about $150 million a year. A 2% tax is $40 million. They still get richer. See [why this income is never taxed today](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#unrealised).

The objection says the money has to come out of the firm. The Zucman report answers it. Liquidity problems are real for people with little income from their assets, it writes, but billionaires earn 7.5% against a 2% tax, and they have bank loans to draw on.[1] For an owner whose wealth is tied up in a firm, countries have written time to pay into the law. From income year 2026 Norway lets owners defer the part of the wealth tax that falls on business assets for up to three years, for an amount of NOK 30,000 or more, at the central bank's policy rate plus 5 percentage points.[10] The United States design in the Zucman report lets a billionaire pay the minimum tax in five equal yearly instalments.[1] More in [no cash](/arguments/no-cash).

## What happened to the firms of people who pay {#norway}

Norway ran the test between 2021 and 2024. It raised the top wealth tax rate from 0.85% to 1.1%, cut the discount on private firm shares so that each dollar of book equity counted for $0.80 in the tax base instead of $0.55, and raised the dividend tax from 32% to almost 38%. That nearly doubled the top rate on business wealth, from 0.47% to 0.88%.[2] Norway's rules for 2026 still give a 20% discount on shares and a 30% discount on operating assets.[9]

The study of what followed compares firms owned by Norwegians who pay the tax with firms owned by foreigners and others who do not. It finds no sign that the Norwegian-owned firms paid out more dividends or invested less. It can rule out a fall in the investment rate larger than 1.2 percentage points, about half what a standard model predicts. It finds no sign that the reform caused more bankruptcies.[2] An earlier study of the small and medium-sized firms of Norwegian owners from 2007 to 2017 goes further. It finds that a household's higher wealth tax went with faster employment growth in its closely held firms, not slower.[3]

One study of 4,381 European companies from 2000 to 2017 found that family firms paid dividends about 3.5% higher where the majority owner faced a sharp rise in wealth tax, and that investment was lower afterwards.[4] That is an owner paying a bill out of the firm's profits, which is where a tax on shares has to be paid from. The rise is 3.5%. In Norway's rise from 2021 to 2024 the payouts did not move.[2] The design answers what is left: a high threshold, so the bill falls on firms with deep pockets, and time to pay, so the firm does not have to pay it all out in one year.

Across Sweden and Denmark, a one percentage point rise in the top wealth tax rate lowers investment in the whole economy by 0.07%, value added by 0.10% and employment by 0.02%.[5] The effects come from owners who move away, and an exit tax answers that: see [they will leave](/arguments/they-will-leave#follows).

## The exemptions written for family firms {#exemptions}

If the worry is that the tax will kill firms, the standard answer is an exemption: leave business assets out of the base. Many countries wrote one. France, Spain and Sweden exempted business assets from their wealth taxes. Germany, Norway, Luxembourg and Ireland valued them at a discount or taxed them at a lower rate.[6] Of the 13 European wealth taxes counted in one survey, 10 had full or partial exemptions for business assets.[5]

Sweden exempted shares in unlisted firms from 1991 and fully exempted any private business in which the owner held more than 25%.[5] Spain exempted business assets and shares in unlisted closely held companies in 1994, and listed ones in 1998. The firm had to carry out real activity, the owner had to hold 5% or the family 20%, and one of them had to manage it and earn at least half of their income from it.[8] The wealth tax was repealed in Sweden in 2007.[5]

The Spanish case is the clearest. The study of the Spanish top 0.01% of wealth holders found that stock claimed as exempt grew from 15% of their closely held stock in 1994 to 77% in 2002. The total they held, taxed and exempt together, stayed stable. Nobody started more firms. The wealthy moved what they had into the exempt box.[7]

{{chart: spain-exempt}}

A later study of Catalonia in the years after Spain brought the tax back found the same pattern. Taxpayers facing a higher wealth tax did not save less. They rearranged what they owned toward the exempt business assets, and the loss in revenue over four years was 2.6 times what the tax raised in 2011.[8] The OECD, after surveying the countries, wrote that exempting or giving significant relief to business assets creates tax avoidance opportunities "particularly at the very top of the wealth distribution", where taxpayers shelter their assets inside a business, and that there is strong evidence the mechanism is used for that purpose.[6] The Zucman report adds that past wealth taxes failed to tax the ultra-rich because of exemptions, most importantly for owner-managers of large companies, and because of special valuation for closely held businesses.[1]

Who benefits from an exemption for family firms? Whoever can put their money into a family firm. In 1997 the French finance minister, Dominique Strauss-Kahn, remarked that his country's wealth tax bothered millionaires without bothering billionaires.[7] A bakery gets nothing from the exemption, since a high threshold already protects it. A holding company that owns a fortune gets everything, and the tax empties out. A threshold at the top protects every real family firm. An exemption protects the people who can arrange to look like one.

## When the owner dies {#death}

The firm gets a price tag at least once. When the owner of a business dies, the estate tax has to put a value on what the estate holds, at fair market value.[14] The United States already does this for every large estate, farms and firms included.

The exemption is $15 million per person in 2026, and the rate is 40% on what is above it.[14][15] In 2019, 2,129 estates were taxable, 0.07% of deaths.[15] Among farm estates the share is 0.3%: in the Department of Agriculture's forecast for 2024, 141 of 41,104 farm estates owed estate tax.[16]

{{chart: estates}}

Of the estates that do owe tax, 300 had more than half their assets in farms or businesses in 2023, the Tax Policy Center estimated. They made up 8% of the taxable estates and 14% of the estate tax revenue.[15] They are the farms and firms above the exemption, and the law gives them time. A business that makes up more than 35% of the estate can pay in up to 10 equal instalments, the first due as late as 5 years after the tax is assessed, which comes to 14 years in all.[15][18]

The threshold did most of the protecting. Under George W. Bush it rose from $1 million to $5 million, on the argument that the "death tax" was killing family businesses and farms. With the higher threshold, Saez and Zucman write, that argument is much harder to make. The 2017 tax law doubled it again to $11.2 million in 2018, and the estate tax survived.[7] The OECD notes that countries often give family businesses relief from inheritance tax, and that where liquidity is a concern, instalments help.[6] A wealth tax at a threshold far above the estate tax's, with time to pay, is the same design.

## What the design has to do {#design}

- **Set the threshold at the top.** $1 billion reaches about 3,000 people.[1] A low threshold is what put the shop and the farm in Europe's tax.
- **Write no exemption for business assets.** Count every firm at its market value, with no discount for private shares. See [you cannot value it](/arguments/you-cannot-value-it#hard) on how valuation works.
- **Give the illiquid time to pay.** Deferral up to three years, as in Norway, or five yearly instalments, as in the Zucman design.[10][1]
- **Charge the owner and not the firm.** The company's payroll and investment sit outside the bill.
- **Add an exit tax.** An owner who leaves stays liable: see [they will leave](/arguments/they-will-leave#follows).
