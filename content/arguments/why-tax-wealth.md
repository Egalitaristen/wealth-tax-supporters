Every country taxes what people earn, what they spend, the home they own, the assets they sell at a profit and what they leave to their children. Where researchers have measured what the very richest actually pay, the same pattern appears: the effective rate rises through the middle and upper middle of the distribution and then falls at the top. In France in 2016 it was 46% for the top 0.1% of tax households and 26% for the top 0.0002%.[1] In Norway and Sweden it falls from about 50% for the upper middle class to 15% to 20% for the top 0.0005%.[2] A Dutch study puts the top 0.0001% below 20%, and in the United States the 400 richest paid 24% of their economic income in 2018 to 2020, against 30% for the whole population.[3] These are four countries with different tax systems and the same shape. The cause sits in how the taxes meet the way the largest fortunes are held.

## How the largest fortunes are held {#held}

At the top, wealth is mostly shares in companies the owner controls. In Norway most of the wealth at the top of the distribution corresponds to shares in private businesses.[2] In Germany real estate, businesses and corporate shares dominate the top wealth strata.[6] In Sweden the top 2% of the wealth distribution control close to 10% of total employment and 15% of value added through private businesses.[7]

A company can keep its profit. Ring, Seim and Zucman find that personal holding companies in Norway and Sweden shield about half of dividend distributions from individual income tax.[2] In France the authors count the retained profits of companies a household controls as part of its income, and find that the corporate tax is the only tax reaching substantial rates for billionaires.[1] The rich do pay tax, through their companies. The personal taxes are where the gap opens.

## What each tax reaches {#taxes}

An income tax reaches income when it arrives in the owner's hands, and a company that keeps its profit sends none. A tax on spending reaches a fortune only as fast as its owner spends it. A property tax is a recurrent tax on immovable property.[4] A capital gains tax is charged on realisation, when the asset is sold, so an asset held for decades is not charged.[4] An inheritance tax is charged once, at death. The OECD adds that a net wealth tax is "distinct from other taxes on capital, including taxes on capital income and taxes on wealth transfers".[4]

None of them is charged on the holding. A wealth tax is: a recurrent tax on net wealth, covering a broad range of property with debts deductible, levied whatever the assets earned that year.[4] The OECD states the difference in one sentence: income taxes are typically levied on realisation, and a wealth tax in theory on an accrual basis. It can be more comprehensive than a capital income tax, covering "both income and non-income generating assets".[4]

## Where the wealth is {#concentration}

The World Inequality Database puts the top 1% share of net personal wealth in 2024 at 23.5% in Norway, 27.5% in Sweden, 27.7% in France, 27.9% in Germany, 31.5% in Switzerland, 34.8% in the United States, 39.2% in Colombia and 40.5% in Uruguay.[8] The national statistics differ from the modelled database. Statistics Norway counts 21.7% for the top 1% of households in 2024, and the Swiss tax administration counts 45.8% of taxable net wealth for the richest 1% in 2022.[9] The sources use different methods, and the spread from 22% to 46% is wide. Across 18 OECD countries the bottom 40% own 3% of household wealth and the top 10% hold half.[4]

## What a wealth tax does differently {#difference}

Every tax we use hangs on an event: wages are paid, goods are bought, an asset is sold, an owner dies. A fortune can avoid the event by holding shares for decades without selling them. A wealth tax hangs on holding the assets, and the holding continues every year.

The rate is small because the base is large. The Mirrlees Review, which argued against an annual wealth tax in Britain, gives the arithmetic: a 20% tax on a 5% return is the same as a 1% tax on the stock.[5] The rates in force are of that order. Norway charges 1.0% above NOK 1.9 million and 1.1% above about NOK 21.5 million, in its 2026 tables.[10] Colombia charges 0.5%, 1% and 1.5% on net wealth above 72,000 UVT.[11] The OECD found that net wealth taxes raised between 0.2% of GDP in Spain and 1.0% in Switzerland in 2016, and Norway's was 0.59% of GDP in 2024.[4][12] The OECD says Switzerland collected the most thanks to comparatively low thresholds and broader tax bases.[4]

## The OECD's objection {#objection}

The OECD reviewed the evidence in 2018 and wrote that there are "limited arguments for having a net wealth tax in addition to broad-based personal capital income taxes and well-designed inheritance and gift taxes." It added that there are "stronger arguments for having a net wealth tax in the absence of broad-based personal capital income taxes and taxes on wealth transfers."[4] The Mirrlees Review reached a similar view for Britain.[5]

The second condition fits what the studies above show. The countries measured do not tax capital broadly at the top: the effective rates fall to 26% in France and 15% to 20% in Norway and Sweden.[1][2] The old wealth taxes also fell short, and the OECD's own case is for a better design. Norway's tax absorbs barely 1% of income at the top, because shares in private businesses enter the base for only a tenth of their market value.[2] France capped its tax by reference to personal income, and the IPP note says it could not correct the fall in rates at the top.[1] The remedy is to value everything at market value and cap nothing against income. Repealing a tax does not fix its valuation rules.

## What people think {#polls}

The register records 36 polls from 26 countries on its Resources page.[13] In June 2024 an Ipsos survey in 22 countries found support, in the register's wording of the questions, for higher taxes on the wealth of the rich of 74% in India, 69% in Brazil, 68% in South Africa, 78% in Türkiye and 86% in Indonesia.[14] The questions differ between countries and the Resources page gives each wording. National polls add 70.6% in Spain for a tax on assets of €10 million or more, 75% in Britain for 1% above £10 million and 2% above £1 billion, and 86% in France, in a poll for the Parti socialiste, for 2% above €100 million.[15][16][17]
