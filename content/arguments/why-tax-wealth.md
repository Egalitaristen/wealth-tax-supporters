A wage earner is taxed on pay before it arrives, on what she spends and on the home she owns. The richest Americans are taxed on much less of what they have. In 2018 to 2020 the 400 richest paid total taxes equal to 24% of their economic income, against 30% for the whole population, and their taxes came to 1.3% of their wealth.[1] Taxes on income, spending, property, gains and inheritance each reach a slice of a fortune. A wealth tax is the one that reaches the fortune.

## What each tax reaches {#taxes}

Income tax is charged on income as it is paid out. The 400 richest Americans earn much of theirs inside companies they own, and those companies pay out less than 10% of their profits as dividends. Taxable income was about 40% of their economic income in 2018 to 2020, against about 70% for the whole population, and their personal income and payroll taxes took 11% of economic income, against 20.5% for everyone.[1] They do pay tax: about nine points of their 23.8% came from the corporate tax on those companies.[1] The gap is in the personal tax.

Spending taxes reach a fortune only as fast as its owner spends it.

Property tax is a recurrent tax on immovable property alone.[8] Owner-occupied homes are 7.0% of the assets of the top 0.1% of US households. Corporate shares and mutual funds are 57% and business equity is 17%.[3]

Capital gains tax is charged when an asset is sold. Dividends and gains face a top rate of 20%, or 23.8% with the investment surtax, against 37% (40.8%) on ordinary income.[6] An investor who never sells owes nothing on the gain, and at death the gain is wiped out for income tax through the stepped-up basis.[6] About 44% of the value of estates above US$20 million in 2010 was unrealised gains, in US Treasury estate data as reported by Saez, Yagan and Zucman.[5]

Estate tax is charged once. In the Balkir, Saez, Yagan and Zucman study the top-400 decedents paid 0.8% of their wealth in estate tax when married and 7% when single.[1]

A wealth tax is a recurrent tax on net wealth. It covers a broad range of property with debts deductible, and it is charged whatever the assets earned that year.[8] The OECD states the difference: an income tax is typically levied on realisation, a wealth tax on an accrual basis, and a wealth tax "can potentially be more comprehensive than a capital income tax, covering both income and non-income generating assets."[8]

## What the taxes add up to {#result}

For the 400 richest Americans the total effective tax rate was about 30% in 2010 to 2017 and 24% in 2018 to 2020.[1] In France the effective direct tax rate in 2016 was 46% for the top 0.1% of tax households and 26% for the top 0.0002%. The French authors find that income tax and the old wealth tax were a negligible part of the billionaires' income, and that the corporate tax was the main tax they paid.[2] In Norway and Sweden effective rates fall from about 50% for the upper middle class to 15% to 20% for the top 0.0005%, in a working paper by Ring, Seim and Zucman.[4]

Balkir, Saez, Yagan and Zucman note that the top-end effective rates they find in the United States are higher than in Europe.[1] On both sides of the Atlantic the pattern is the same: the effective rate falls once a household is rich enough to hold its wealth in companies.

## Where the wealth is {#concentration}

The top 0.1% of US households held 15.0% of net worth in 2026, up from 8.6% in 1989. The top 1% held 32.5% and the bottom half 2.3%.[3] Their net worth grew about 4.1 times between 2006 and 2026, to US$27.9 trillion, while nominal GDP grew 2.4 times. Their combined wealth equals about 86% of a year's GDP, against 49% in 2006. These are our calculations from the Federal Reserve and FRED files.[3][7]

In Switzerland the tax authority reports that the richest 1% of taxpayers held 45.8% of taxable net wealth in 2022.[10] In Norway Statistics Norway counts 21.7% for the top 1% of households and 10.3% for the top 0.1% in 2024.[11] The OECD found that in 18 of its member countries the bottom 40% own 3% of household wealth and the top 10% hold half.[8]

## What a wealth tax does differently {#difference}

Every tax we use hangs on an event: wages are paid, goods are bought, an asset is sold, an owner dies. A fortune can avoid the event: it can hold shares for decades without selling them. A wealth tax hangs on holding the assets, and the holding continues every year.

The rate is small because the base is large. The Mirrlees Review, which argued against an annual wealth tax in Britain, gives the arithmetic: a 20% tax on a 5% return is the same as a 1% tax on the stock.[9] Senator Elizabeth Warren's [Ultra-Millionaire Tax Act](/resources/us-ultra-millionaire-tax-act-of-2021) charges 2% above US$50 million and 3% above US$1 billion. The [calculator](/calculator?w=200000000&b=50000000:2,1000000000:3&c=US$) gives US$3.0 million a year on a fortune of US$200 million, 1.5%. Ordinary income at the top bracket pays 37%.[6]

Net wealth taxes raised between 0.2% of GDP in Spain and 1.0% in Switzerland in 2016. The OECD says Switzerland collected considerably more than the others thanks to comparatively low exemption thresholds and broader tax bases.[8]

## The OECD's objection {#objection}

The OECD reviewed the evidence in 2018 and wrote that there are "limited arguments for having a net wealth tax in addition to broad-based personal capital income taxes and well-designed inheritance and gift taxes." It added that there are "stronger arguments for having a net wealth tax in the absence of broad-based personal capital income taxes and taxes on wealth transfers."[8] The Mirrlees Review reached a similar conclusion for Britain.[9]

Both conditions in the OECD sentence matter. The data above show broad capital income taxes are not in place at the top: the 400 richest Americans pay personal income tax on 40% of their economic income, French billionaires pay little income tax or wealth tax, and the Nordic rates fall to 15% to 20% at the top.[1][2][4] The OECD's own case for a wealth tax fits where we are.

The report also records that the old wealth taxes fell short. Norway's tax absorbs barely 1% of income at the top, because private business shares enter the base at a tenth of their market value.[4] France capped its wealth tax by reference to personal income, and the IPP note says it could not correct the fall in rates at the top.[2] The remedy is to value everything at market value and cap nothing against income. Repealing a tax does not fix its valuation rules.

## Who says so {#who}

[Emmanuel Saez](/us/A0675) and [Gabriel Zucman](/us/A0679), who wrote the studies behind the American figures, argue for a wealth tax. [Elizabeth Warren](/us/A0001) introduced the federal bill. In October 2026 the University of Maryland's Program for Public Consultation found that Warren's design, with its revenue shown, is favoured by large majorities of Democrats and Republicans in every battleground state and district surveyed, and nationally.[12]
