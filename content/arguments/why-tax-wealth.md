Every tax we pay is set off by something happening. A wage is paid. A shop sells you a coat. A house is sold at a profit. Someone dies. The largest fortunes are built so that none of these things happens: the owner leaves the profit inside a company and holds the shares for decades.[2]

The numbers show it. In France in 2016 the richest 0.0002% of households paid 26% of their income in direct taxes. The richest 0.1% paid 46%.[1] In Norway and Sweden the rate falls from about 50% for the upper middle class to 15% to 20% at the very top.[2] The richest people pay less than the people just below them.

A wealth tax is the one tax that does not wait for an event. It is charged on what you own, every year, at a rate near 1%.[9] The case for it comes in six steps:

1. [The richest pay a lower rate](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#lower-rate) than the people just below them, because their wealth sits in companies.
2. [Their fortunes grow](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#growth) more than five times as fast as average income.
3. [A tax on the holding](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#difference) is small each year and reaches what no other tax reaches.
4. [It would raise](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#revenue) $200 billion to $250 billion a year from about 3,000 billionaires.[13]
5. [Great fortunes buy influence](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#power), and the share is rising.
6. [The objections have answers](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#objections), and [most people already agree](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#polls).

## The richest pay less than the people just below them {#lower-rate}

Researchers have matched tax records to company records to see what the richest households really pay. In five countries the answer has the same shape. The rate climbs through the middle and the upper middle class, then drops at the top.

{{chart: effective-rates}}

In France the drop is from 46% for the richest 0.1% to 26% for the richest 0.0002%, which is roughly the billionaires. For them, income tax and France's old wealth tax were a negligible part of their income. Corporate tax was the main tax they paid.[1]

In Norway and Sweden, personal holding companies shield about half of dividend payments from income tax. Over two decades only 15% to 20% of the profit in the richest owners' companies is paid out to them, and only money that is paid out counts as income.[2]

In the United States the 400 richest people paid 24% of their income in 2018 to 2020, against 30% for the whole population and 45% for the top earners of wages.[3] In the Netherlands a study puts the rate under 20% for the richest 0.0001%, roughly the country's billionaires.[3]

The Nordic figure is lower than the American one even though the overall level of tax is much higher in Scandinavia.[2] What the richest pay depends on how the tax system treats a company that keeps its profit.

## The fortune sits in a company {#held}

The biggest fortunes are not piles of cash or houses. They are shares in companies the owner controls. In Norway most of the wealth at the top is shares in private businesses.[2] In Sweden the richest 2% control close to 10% of all jobs and 15% of the value the economy creates, through businesses they own.[6]

A company that keeps its profit sends no income to its owner, so no income tax is due. An ordinary worker cannot do that, because wages are taxed as they arrive. At the top, owners save nearly all of what their companies earn, and the income tax becomes trivial next to their income.[2]

None of this is new. In 1933 the New York Times reported that J.P. Morgan, one of the largest American fortunes of the time, had paid no income tax in 1931 and 1932. In 1934 the United States answered with a special tax on personal holding companies.[2] The country has penalised holding companies ever since, and its richest still pay only 24%.[2][3] A tax aimed at the company is not enough. The fortune needs a tax of its own.

## What each tax reaches {#taxes}

Income tax is charged when money reaches your hands. A capital gains tax is charged when you sell, so an asset held for decades is never charged.[4] A property tax is charged each year on land and buildings, and a private company's shares are neither. An inheritance tax is charged once, at death.[4]

A net wealth tax is a different tax. It is charged every year on the total value of what you own, with debts taken off, whatever the assets earned that year. The OECD puts the difference in one line: income taxes are normally charged when gains are realised, a wealth tax as value builds up, and it covers assets that earn nothing as well as assets that do.[4]

In 2018 the OECD wrote that where taxes on capital income and on inheritance are broad and well designed, the arguments for a wealth tax on top of them are limited. The same report found that taxes on capital income alone will most likely not be enough to address wealth inequality, and that the arguments for a wealth tax are stronger where the overall burden on capital is low.[4] The figures above show a low burden at the top: 26% in France and 15% to 20% in Norway and Sweden.[1][2] The OECD's own test for a wealth tax is met.

More on this: [we already tax property](/arguments/we-already-tax-property) and [every tax is paid from money that was taxed before](/arguments/double-taxation).

## The fortunes grow faster than the economy {#growth}

Since 1987 the wealth of the world's billionaires has grown by 7.1% a year after inflation. Average income grew by 1.3% a year over the same period, and average wealth by 3.2%.[13]

{{chart: growth-gap}}

In 1987 the richest 0.0001% of households held wealth equal to 3% of world GDP. By 2024 it was more than 13%. That group is about 3,000 households, roughly the number of dollar billionaires.[13] The report that counts this puts the tax billionaires pay at 0.3% of their wealth a year. A fortune that earns 7.5% a year before tax keeps 7.2% after it.[13]

A fortune that grows more than five times as fast as the income of everyone else stops being a prize for the best idea or the hardest work and becomes a head start that no new company and no new family can match.

The same drift shows in the national statistics. Across 18 OECD countries the bottom 40% own 3% of household wealth. The top 10% hold half, and the top 1% almost a fifth.[4] Between 1990 and 2024 the share of the richest 1% rose in France, Germany, Sweden, Switzerland and the United States. In Norway it stayed at 23.5%.[7]

{{chart: top-one-percent}}

## A tax on the holding {#difference}

A wealth tax hangs on the holding, so a fortune cannot get out of it by waiting. It can be small because the base is large. A 20% tax on a 5% return raises the same as a 1% tax on the stock: £1 from £100, whatever the asset earned.[5]

Norway's rates for 2026, state and municipal tax together, are 1.0% on a single person's net wealth between NOK 1.9 million and NOK 21.5 million and 1.1% above that.[9] In 2024 the tax raised NOK 31.8 billion from 719,007 taxpayers.[8] In 2016 the net wealth taxes of the OECD countries that had them raised between 0.2% of GDP in Spain and 1.0% in Switzerland.[4]

Design decides what a wealth tax collects, and the design is where the tax has been weak. A study of Norway finds that shares in private businesses, the main form of wealth at the top, enter its wealth tax base at only about a tenth of their market value.[2] That is a big reason its tax takes so little from the very top. The answer is to count every asset at its market value and to write no exemptions in. Scrapping a tax does not fix its valuation rules.

### The minimum tax {#minimum}

The simplest design today is a floor. Anyone worth more than $1 billion pays at least 2% of their wealth a year, through whatever tax a country prefers: a wealth tax, or an income tax on a broad definition of income. Someone who already pays more than 2% of their wealth owes nothing extra. Only those who pay little are affected, and the billionaires' rate would become no lower than that of middle-class workers.[13]

The same report estimates what it would leave them. A billionaire's return of 7.2% after tax today would fall to 5.5%. They would still get richer.[13]

## What it would raise {#revenue}

A 2% minimum tax on billionaires would raise $200 billion to $250 billion a year worldwide, from about 3,000 taxpayers. Extending it to people worth more than $100 million would add $100 billion to $140 billion.[13] The seven Nobel prize winners who asked France for such a tax in 2025 put the figures at about $250 billion worldwide and $50 billion in Europe.[14]

The same logic works at the level of a state. California has about 250 billionaires, worth $2.3 trillion together. From 2019 to 2025 they paid California income tax equal to 1.6% of the $1.4 trillion their wealth grew. A one-time tax of 5% on their wealth would raise about $100 billion, as much as a 5% income tax on every Californian taxpayer.[15] Californian voters rejected it on 3 November 2026, 52.8% to 47.2%, as reported by the Associated Press on 9 November with the count not yet certified.[18] Nearly half of those who voted backed it.

A wealth tax also has a second job beside revenue, which is to slow how fast the largest fortunes outgrow the economy. The page on that: [not enough](/arguments/not-enough).

## Great fortunes buy influence {#power}

Six winners of the economics Nobel wrote to California voters in September 2026 that billionaires gave about 1% of federal election donations in the 2000 cycle and about 19% in 2024. They added that several of the biggest newspapers and social platforms in the United States are owned or controlled by billionaires.[15]

{{chart: donations}}

A tax on the largest fortunes slows how fast a few people can gather that kind of power, and leaves more of the decisions to voters.

## The objections, answered {#objections}

Every wealth tax meets the same objections. Each has a page of its own with the evidence. Here are the answers in short.

### They will leave {#leave}

Norway's Finance Ministry counted 48 people worth more than NOK 100 million who moved abroad in 2022 and 34 in 2023.[11] From 2021 to 2024 the receipts of the wealth tax rose 72%, from NOK 18.5 billion to NOK 31.8 billion.[8] A rich person who stays and pays nothing was never an asset. The page: [they will leave](/arguments/they-will-leave).

### You cannot value it {#value}

In 2024 719,007 people in Norway paid wealth tax.[8] A tax that is asked of that many people every year is one the authorities know how to value. A minimum tax on 3,000 billionaires has far fewer returns to check.[13] The page: [you cannot value it](/arguments/you-cannot-value-it).

### Owners have no cash {#cash}

Norway lets owners defer the part of their wealth tax that falls on business assets for up to three years, when it comes to NOK 30,000 or more.[10] A pensioner with a paid-off house borrows, sells or defers in the same way. The page: [no cash](/arguments/no-cash).

### Family firms {#firms}

The threshold decides who pays. A minimum tax at $1 billion reaches about 3,000 people on Earth.[13] The page: [family firms](/arguments/family-firms) and, on whether thresholds creep down, [it will spread](/arguments/it-will-spread).

### Europe repealed it {#repealed}

Twelve OECD countries had a wealth tax in 1990. Four did in 2017.[4] France replaced its wealth tax in 2018 with a tax on real estate alone. The state's own evaluators estimate that this cost €3.1 billion in revenue in 2018, against a wealth tax that had raised €4.2 billion in 2017.[12] The page: [Europe repealed it](/arguments/europe-repealed-it).

### It hurts growth, and it is unconstitutional {#growth-law}

The evidence on investment and the rulings of courts each have a page: [hurts growth](/arguments/hurts-growth) and [unconstitutional](/arguments/unconstitutional).

## What people think {#polls}

The register holds 71 polls from 43 countries on a tax on wealth.[16] In 41 of the 43, the highest result is above half of those asked.

{{chart: poll-ranking}}

In the United States 80% back an annual tax of 2% on wealth over $50 million and 3% over $1 billion, once told what it would raise, in a University of Maryland survey of 2,509 adults in October 2026.[16] In Britain 76% back a 2% tax on assets over £10 million, in a YouGov poll of 2,167 adults for Oxfam in June 2026.[16] In France 86% back a 2% tax on wealth above €100 million in an Ifop poll for the Socialist Party, and 74% in an Elabe poll for the BFMTV channel, both in September 2025.[16]

The wording and the sponsor differ from poll to poll, so the Resources page shows both for each of them.

## Where it exists {#where}

{{chart: status-map}}

The register counts 10 countries with an annual tax on net wealth in force and 13 more that had one.[17] Sweden, Denmark, Germany, the Netherlands, Austria and Finland are among those that no longer levy one, and the page on [Europe repealing it](/arguments/europe-repealed-it) shows what that cost.

## Who backs it {#who}

Eight winners of the economics Nobel have signed public letters asking for a tax on the largest fortunes: seven to France in July 2025 and six to California in September 2026.[14][15] The page: [Nobel laureates](/arguments/nobel-laureates).

[Gabriel Zucman](/fr/A0229) and [Emmanuel Saez](/us/A0675) wrote studies behind several of the figures on this page. The [directory](/directory) lists the politicians, economists, unions and campaigners on the record in each country, and the register holds 150 bills and laws.[19]
