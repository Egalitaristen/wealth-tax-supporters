Every country taxes what people earn, what they spend, the home they own, the assets they sell at a profit, and what they leave to their children. Where researchers have measured what the very richest actually pay, the same pattern turns up: the effective rate climbs through the middle and upper middle of society and then falls at the top. In France in 2016 it was 46% for the top 0.1% of households and 26% for the top 0.0002%.[1] In Norway and Sweden it drops from about 50% for the upper middle class to 15% to 20% for the top 0.0005%.[2] A study in the Netherlands puts the top 0.0001% below 20%, and in the United States the 400 richest paid 24% of their economic income in 2018 to 2020, against 30% for the whole population.[3] These are four countries with different tax systems and the same shape. The cause is how our taxes meet the way the biggest fortunes are held.

## How the biggest fortunes are held {#held}

At the top, wealth is mostly shares in companies the owner controls. In Norway most of the wealth at the top of the distribution is shares in private businesses.[2] In Germany, real estate, businesses and corporate shares dominate the top.[6] In Sweden the richest 2% control close to 10% of all jobs and 15% of the value created in the economy through private businesses.[7]

A company can keep its profit. In Norway and Sweden, personal holding companies shield about half of dividend payments from income tax.[2] In France, the retained profits of a household's companies count as its income, and the corporate tax is the only tax that reaches substantial rates for billionaires.[1] The rich do pay tax, through their companies. The gap opens in the personal taxes.

## What each tax reaches {#taxes}

An income tax reaches income when it lands in the owner's hands, and a company that keeps its profit sends none. A tax on spending reaches a fortune only as fast as its owner spends it. A property tax is a yearly tax on land and buildings.[4] A capital gains tax is charged when you sell, so an asset held for decades is never charged.[4] An inheritance tax is charged once, at death. The OECD adds that a net wealth tax is a different thing from taxes on capital income and taxes on wealth transfers.[4]

None of them is charged on the holding. A wealth tax is: a yearly tax on net wealth, covering a broad range of property with debts deducted, charged whatever the assets earned that year.[4] The OECD says the difference in one sentence: income taxes are normally charged when gains are realised, a wealth tax as value accrues, and it can cover both income-producing and non-income-producing assets.[4]

## Where the wealth is {#concentration}

The World Inequality Database puts the top 1%'s share of net personal wealth in 2024 at 23.5% in Norway, 27.5% in Sweden, 27.7% in France, 27.9% in Germany, 31.5% in Switzerland, 34.8% in the United States, 39.2% in Colombia and 40.5% in Uruguay.[8] National statistics give other numbers: Statistics Norway counts 21.7% for the top 1% of households in 2024, and the Swiss tax administration counts 45.8% of taxable net wealth for the richest 1% in 2022.[9] Whichever source you use, the range is huge, from 22% to 46%. Across 18 OECD countries the bottom 40% own 3% of household wealth and the top 10% hold half.[4]

## What a wealth tax does differently {#difference}

Every tax we use hangs on an event: wages are paid, goods are bought, an asset is sold, an owner dies. A fortune can avoid the event by holding shares for decades without selling them. A wealth tax hangs on the holding, and the holding goes on every year.

The rate is small because the base is large. A 20% tax on a 5% return is the same as a 1% tax on the stock, as one British review of tax policy worked out.[5] The rates in force are of that order. Norway charges 1.0% above NOK 1.9 million and 1.1% above about NOK 21.5 million, in its 2026 tables.[10] Colombia charges 0.5%, 1% and 1.5% on net wealth above 72,000 UVT.[11] Net wealth taxes raised between 0.2% of GDP in Spain and 1.0% in Switzerland in 2016, and Norway's raised 0.59% in 2024.[4][12] Switzerland collects the most because its thresholds are lower and its base broader.[4]

## The OECD's doubt, and the answer {#objection}

In 2018 the OECD wrote that there are limited arguments for a net wealth tax in addition to broad taxes on capital income and well-designed inheritance taxes. It added that there are stronger arguments for one where those taxes are missing.[4] Look at the countries measured above. They don't tax capital broadly at the top: effective rates fall to 26% in France and 15% to 20% in Norway and Sweden.[1][2] The OECD's own condition for a wealth tax is met.

The old taxes also failed where they were badly designed, and that is an argument for better design. Norway's absorbs barely 1% of income at the top, because shares in private businesses enter the base at only a tenth of their market value.[2] France capped its tax against income, which kept it from fixing the drop in rates at the top.[1] The answer is to value everything at market value and cap nothing against income. Scrapping a tax doesn't fix its valuation rules.

## What people think {#polls}

The site's Resources page lists 36 polls from 26 countries.[13] In an Ipsos survey of 22 countries in June 2024, support for higher taxes on the wealth of the rich was 74% in India, 69% in Brazil, 68% in South Africa, 78% in Türkiye and 86% in Indonesia.[14] The questions differ from country to country, and the Resources page gives each wording. National polls add 70.6% in Spain for a tax on assets of €10 million or more, 75% in Britain for 1% above £10 million and 2% above £1 billion, and 86% in France, in a poll for the Socialist Party, for 2% above €100 million.[15][16][17]
