The objection says that a tax that starts with billionaires will not stay there. It will slide down to the household with a paid-off house and a pension. Our answer has two parts. The record does not support the slide. And the part of the objection that is true, that a tax will reach more than billionaires, describes a tax working as it should.

## What thresholds did {#thresholds}

A threshold is a number in a law, and in the countries where we can follow it for years it went up.

Sweden's wealth tax had a threshold of SEK 800,000 in 1991. In the early 2000s it was raised to SEK 3 million for couples, and in 2006 only couples in the top 2% of the wealth distribution were liable. In 1999 every household above the 92nd percentile had been.[1] Denmark's last law raised the allowance to DKK 2.4 million from DKK 1,635,500 a year before the tax went.[6] In Spain the number of taxpayers fell from 981,498 in 2007 to 188,680 in 2015 as thresholds rose.[2]

Colombia's government bill of October 2026 raises its threshold from 72,000 to 95,500 UVT, and La República reports that some people who pay today would stop.[3] The direction of travel in these cases is upward, and each increase came from a government that was drawing the line.

## Where a threshold slides by itself {#erosion}

A threshold can move without a vote. Bolivia's is fixed in bolivianos: Bs 30 million. It was US$4.3 million when the law passed in 2020 and US$2.5 million in November 2026, because the boliviano fell from Bs 6.96 to Bs 11.81 to the dollar.[5] The tax slid down the wealth scale, with no change in the law. A threshold fixed in a currency that loses value does that. The remedy is to index it, which Uruguay does with its non-taxable minimum.[9]

## Where a tax reaches many {#many}

A tax that starts low reaches many. Norway's wealth tax was paid in 2024 by 701,482 residents, 15.4% of adults.[4] Of those, 313,434 paid under NOK 10,000, 178,023 paid NOK 10,000 to 24,999, and 2,838 paid NOK 1 million or more.[4] The wide reach is real, and so is the concentration of the payments at the top.

Swiss cantons with low thresholds did the same: in the canton of Bern, 30% of all taxpayers had a non-zero wealth tax liability in 2001 to 2011.[2] Swiss cantonal thresholds ran from CHF 25,000 to CHF 200,000 in 2014, and a threshold that low reaches the middle class.[2] A threshold at the top reaches few: under 0.1% of US families would pay Warren's tax, and 34,229 filers paid Colombia's in 2025.[7][3]

## Who can afford a tax {#afford}

We say plainly what the objection implies. It assumes there is a line below which a household cannot be asked to pay and that the line sits below the households in question. A household with US$5 million in shares can afford a tax of 1% of it, US$50,000 a year, from a return of US$250,000 at 5%. The 400 richest Americans paid total taxes equal to 1.3% of their wealth in 2018 to 2020.[8] The principle behind every tax in the system is that it is collected from those who can pay it for the public good: wages, purchases and homes all carry one.

The argument that multimillionaires cannot afford a tax does not hold, and the pages on liquidity and double taxation answer it. If a tax reaches multimillionaires as well as billionaires, it is because they have the means to pay.

## A threshold in the law {#design}

Thresholds are the cheapest part of the design to protect. Put them in the law, in the currency of the tax, indexed to prices or to median wealth, so that a change needs a vote. Say who the tax is for: Warren's design starts at US$50 million and the Oligarch Act at 1,000 times median household wealth. Then the claim that it will spread has a number to test it against.
