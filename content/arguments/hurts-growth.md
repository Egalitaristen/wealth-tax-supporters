A wealth tax is charged on the owner's shares. It is not charged on the company. The factory, the payroll and the new plant sit inside the company, and the company pays the same corporate tax with or without a wealth tax on its owner. The owner pays out of a fortune that, for billionaires, now grows almost untaxed.

Billionaires have earned 7.5% a year on their wealth after inflation over the past four decades. They pay tax equal to 0.3% of their wealth, so they keep 7.2%. A well enforced minimum tax of 2% would leave them 5.5%, which is what the average person in the world earns on their savings, 5% to 6% a year.[4] The fortune still compounds, a little slower.

{{chart: return-kept}}

Now look at where the tax has been tried. In Sweden and Denmark, a one point higher top rate lowers investment across the whole economy by 0.07%.[1] Norway nearly doubled the tax on business wealth between 2021 and 2023, and the firms of the people who pay it paid out no more and invested no less.[2] In six countries that scrapped their wealth tax, growth per person did not pick up in the ten years after.[9][10] The rest of this page shows each step.

## The arithmetic of a 1% tax {#return}

Take a stock that earns 5% to 7% a year in profit and rising value. A tax of 1% of its value each year takes one point from that return: from 5% to 4%, or from 7% to 6%. It is a tax on the holding. It takes a fifth to a seventh of the return, and it is charged whether the stock rose or fell.

Compare it with what the same owner pays today. Billionaires keep nearly all of their return, because the profits their companies hold back are never paid out to them and never become income.[4] In the United States the 400 richest people pay 24% of their economic income in all taxes, against 30% for the whole population.[8] A 1% tax on a 5% return works out to 20 cents on each dollar of return. For the largest fortunes, which pay close to nothing, it is a step toward what everyone else already pays. The pillar page shows [how the growth escapes tax](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#unrealised) and [what reaches the bank account](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#bank).

At the top, the same logic gives the minimum tax. A billionaire who pays less than 2% of their wealth a year in individual taxes pays the difference. Someone who already pays more owes nothing extra. The return drops from 7.2% to 5.5%.[4] A fortune that earns 5.5% a year still doubles in about thirteen years.

## What the tax does not touch {#untouched}

The tax is on the owner. It is not on the company's decisions. Whether the company builds a plant, hires 500 people or raises wages, the owner's tax bill is the same 1% of what the shares are worth. The company's profit is taxed as before, and its wages and payroll taxes are untouched.

One more thing works in the other direction. Because the tax is charged on the stock whatever it earned, it takes the same cut from an owner whose company earns 15% and an owner who sits on a holding earning 1%. For the first, it is a small part of the return. For the second, it is the whole return. A study of an economy with owners of unequal ability finds that a wealth tax moves capital toward the productive owners and away from the unproductive ones, so output rises.[5] That is the opposite of a drag on growth.

## What the studies find where it has been tried {#studies}

**Sweden and Denmark.** Researchers followed the wealthy through tax records and the firms they own. Raise the top wealth tax rate by one point and the number of wealthy taxpayers falls by about 2%, mostly because some move abroad. The effect on the whole economy, counting the firms those people owned, is 0.07% less investment, 0.10% less output and 0.02% less employment.[1]

When an owner does leave, their firm usually closes: employment drops by 33%, investment by 22% and output by 34%. Almost half of the closed firms, 45%, were taken over by other firms in Sweden, and the workers lost little.[1] The answer to leaving is not to drop the tax. It is an exit tax and a base with no exemptions. The page on the question: [they will leave](/arguments/they-will-leave).

**Norway.** Between 2021 and 2023 the top tax on business wealth went from 0.47% to 0.88% a year, and over the reforms to 2024 the dividend tax rose from 32% to almost 38%. Wealthy owners left: at most 100 people, mostly in 2022 and 2023, after which the rate of leaving fell back to what it was before. Startup founders and inventors did not leave. Firms owned by people who pay the tax paid out no more in dividends, invested no less and went bankrupt no more often than firms owned by foreigners and others outside the tax.[2] Wealth tax revenue from 2021 to 2024 rose by 71.5%, in spite of the departures.[2]

**How owners pay.** In Norway, each additional krone of wealth tax made a household save 3.76 kroner more a year, paid for by working and earning more.[3] The tax came out of consumption and extra work, not out of investment. In Catalonia, taxpayers who faced a higher wealth tax saved no less. They moved wealth into the business assets the tax exempted, which is the loophole to close and not a reason to scrap the tax.[6]

**The largest fortunes grow more slowly.** The Danish evidence shows the tax slows how fast the very wealthy add to their fortunes.[7] That is the aim. A fortune that grows at 7.5% a year, against 5% to 6% for everyone else, is not an investment the economy lacks.

## Countries that tax wealth, and countries that stopped {#countries}

Switzerland and Norway tax wealth, and they are among the richest countries in the world. In 2024 GDP per person at constant 2015 prices was $93,018 in Switzerland and $83,487 in Norway, against $66,857 in the United States and $40,701 across the OECD.[9] Uruguay, which taxes wealth, grew faster than the OECD between 1995 and 2024: 2.31% a year, against 1.44%. Colombia, which also taxes it, grew 1.72%.[9][10] None of this shows that the tax causes growth. It shows that a country with a wealth tax is not shut out of it.

The register counts 10 countries with a wealth tax and 13 that used to have one.[10] Take the six repeals that are ten years or more old and ask whether growth took off afterwards. Sweden repealed in 2007, Finland in 2006, the Netherlands in 2001, Germany in 1997 (the tax has not been levied since), Denmark in 1997 and Austria in 1994.[10]

{{chart: repeal-growth}}

The chart measures each country against the OECD average, so most of the effect of the 2008 crisis, which hit everyone, drops out. In none of the six did growth per person speed up relative to the OECD after repeal. Sweden went from 1.01 points a year ahead to 0.23 behind, Finland from 1.54 ahead to 0.86 behind, the Netherlands from 0.62 ahead to 0.07 behind and Germany from 0.05 ahead to 0.34 behind. Denmark stayed 0.25 to 0.27 points behind and Austria 0.03 to 0.05 ahead.[9] What this shows: no country that scrapped its wealth tax pulled ahead of its peers afterwards. What it does not show: that repeal caused the slowdown.

The share of output that went to investment points the same way: it rose in Denmark, Finland and Sweden and fell in Austria, Germany and the Netherlands. There is no pattern.[9] The promise behind repeal, that an economy freed from the tax would race ahead, did not come true in any of the six. More on what repeal cost: [Europe repealed it](/arguments/europe-repealed-it).

## The head start slows growth {#head-start}

The growth the objection cares about comes from people with ideas getting the means to try them. A fortune that compounds far faster than the economy does the reverse. Since 1987 the wealth of the world's billionaires has grown 7.1% a year after inflation, against 1.3% for average income.[4] The pillar page shows [how fast it grows](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#growth).

The OECD finds that rising inequality drags growth down. An increase of 3 Gini points, the average rise in the OECD over two decades, would cost 0.35 percentage points of growth a year for 25 years, a total loss of 8.5% of GDP. The loss comes from the bottom 40% of earners, whose families under-invest in education.[11] IMF staff find that lower net inequality goes with faster and longer lasting growth, and that redistribution is generally benign for growth.[13]

## What the money buys {#spending}

A wealth tax pays for something. The IMF finds that public investment of 1% of GDP raises output by about 0.4% in the same year and by 1.5% four years later in advanced economies.[12] The most thorough comparison of government programmes finds the best returns in spending on children: four expansions of health insurance for children in the United States repaid $1.78 to the government for each $1 spent.[14] In September 2026 six winners of the economics Nobel wrote to Californian voters that what lets a state thrive is adequate public spending on health, education and infrastructure, the engines of growth, and not letting a handful of billionaires live tax free.[15]

A minimum tax at $1 billion would raise $200 billion to $250 billion a year worldwide.[4] It would come from about 3,000 fortunes that grow at 7.5% a year, and it would pay for investment with a record of returning more than it costs.

## Who pays: not the founders {#who-pays}

The picture behind the objection is a founder with a young company and a small firm owner. A minimum tax at $1 billion reaches about 3,000 taxpayers on Earth.[4] A start-up that is worth $50 million is not touched, and neither is a family firm worth $200 million. The Norwegian record agrees: the people who left after the 2021 to 2024 changes were in the top 0.1% of the wealth distribution, and inflows of startup founders did not drop.[2] The page on firms: [family firms](/arguments/family-firms).

## What follows {#design}

A wealth tax lowers an owner's return by a point or two, lowers none of the company's choices, and falls on fortunes that now grow almost untaxed. Where it was tried, the studies find small effects on investment and none on the firms' dividends or investment. Where it was scrapped, growth did not take off. The money, spent on children, health and public investment, has strong evidence of returning more than it costs. The design that gets this right sets a high threshold, counts every asset at its market value and adds an exit tax.
