Twelve OECD countries had a yearly tax on net wealth in 1990. In 2017 four did.[1]

{{chart: oecd-count}}

That is the fact people reach for to say wealth taxes failed. Read the papers behind the repeals and the failure turns out to belong to the design. The taxes had exemptions for the owners of companies, valued property at prices from decades earlier, capped the bill at a share of income, or began so low that homes and bank accounts were caught. The richest slipped through and the middle class paid. Governments then scrapped the tax and kept the people it had missed.

France measured what that costs. Its wealth tax raised €4.2 billion in 2017. The state's own evaluators estimate that replacing it with a tax on real estate lost €3.1 billion in 2018 and, on their calculation, €4.5 billion in 2022.[10] Where governments kept the tax and fixed its rates, the money followed: Norway's receipts rose 72% in three years.[16] Since 2019, Venezuela, Bolivia and Tunisia have each passed a new one.[22][19][21]

## The holes the repealed taxes shared {#holes}

The OECD went through the experience of the countries that had a wealth tax. Across them, the tax bases were narrow because of many exemptions and reliefs, and avoidance and evasion were widespread.[1] The 2024 report for the G20 says the same in plain terms: past wealth taxes failed to tax the very rich because of exemptions, above all for the owners of large companies, because of caps on the tax owed as a share of income, because closely held businesses were valued low, and because enforcement was poor.[17] Each of those is a rule that can be changed.

An exemption for business owners. Spain exempted the shares of owner-managers from 1994. Researchers found that the exemption wore the tax base away, because wealthy owners rearranged their businesses to qualify.[1] Sweden's government wrote in 2007 that the uneven valuation rules and the many exemptions created loopholes and encouraged tax planning, above all among those with large capital assets.[2]

A cap tied to income. France's wealth tax charged 1.5% at the top. The richest 0.001% of taxpayers paid 0.1% of their wealth in 2017, fifteen times less. The reason was a cap that limited wealth tax, income tax and social charges together to 75% of the income declared the year before. Of the taxpayers with €100 million to €200 million of taxable wealth, 84% were capped, and the incomes they declared came to 0.2% of their wealth. The cap also gave them a reason to declare less, so it cost about €2 billion a year, twice what the tax administration had estimated.[10]

{{chart: france-cap}}

Values from decades ago. Germany's constitutional court ruled in 1995 that land was valued at prices set in 1964 while everything else was valued at current prices, and that taxing both at one rate broke the equal treatment the constitution demands.[6] In Austria the Greens wrote in the committee report of 1993 that land had last been revalued in 1973, so that assessed values stood at a fifth to a tenth of real values.[12]

A threshold that caught the middle class. In the last year each tax ran, a single person's wealth tax began at €61,355 in Germany, €90,756 in the Netherlands and €166,214 in Sweden.[1] Austria's tax had no threshold, only allowances.[1] Sweden's own tax committee wrote in 2007 that its tax had become, to a large extent, a tax on the better-off middle class's homes, funds and bank deposits. The government added that many small house owners had come to pay because assessed values were rising fast.[3][2] A tax that misses the largest fortunes through its holes and takes from house owners is unpopular and easy to repeal. The OECD notes that countries which kept their taxes raised the thresholds to keep from burdening the middle and upper middle class.[1]

A hole is closed by a rule: no exemption without strict criteria, no cap tied to income, market values, a threshold at the top.[1] Scrapping the tax does none of that. It leaves the largest fortunes as they were and takes the bill away from everyone else too.

## Sweden: the government listed the holes, then dropped the tax {#sweden}

Sweden's 2007 bill gave two reasons. The valuation rules were uneven and full of exemptions, and capital was said to be moving abroad: the tax agency estimated that SEK 500 billion might be held outside the country.[2] Sweden's trade union confederation, LO, answered that it would be better to abolish the exemptions than to abolish the tax.[2]

The tax brought in SEK 5 billion to 6 billion a year, the tax committee wrote. Scrapping it was estimated to cost the budget SEK 6.89 billion, and the government raised SEK 1.3 billion by limiting deductions for private pension saving.[3] Sweden had an exemption for business assets, the OECD reports.[1] Owners it protected owed nothing before and nothing after. On the claim that the rich leave, [they will leave](/arguments/they-will-leave#counts) counts who left Sweden, Denmark, France and Norway, and what it cost.

## Denmark: eroded by discounts, then traded away {#denmark}

Denmark's 1995 bill explained that the 1990s tax reform now taxed gains on shares and property, and that the wealth tax had been a residual tax that caught gains nobody else taxed.[4] A tax on gains is charged when the asset is sold, so a fortune that is never sold never pays it, as the OECD points out.[1] The bill abolished the tax from 1997. It was then worth about DKK 760 million a year from about 35,000 families, and DKK 450 million of that went to the self-employed and major shareholders.[4]

Mogens Lykketoft, the Social Democrat who negotiated the repeal as finance minister, wrote in 2021 that it was never his wish. It was the price of getting the Conservatives to back a budget. The tax had been worn down by discount rules for certain assets and held down by a ceiling on income and wealth tax together, so the wealthy with large deductions and untaxed gains felt almost nothing, and most had good ways out.[5] He proposed a tax with no discounts and no ceiling: 1% above DKK 7.5 million, which he judged would raise at least about twenty times more, in real terms, than the tax scrapped 25 years earlier.[5]

## Germany: the court asked for a fix {#germany}

Germany is the clearest case, because many people think the constitutional court killed the tax. It did not. It found that land was valued at 1964 prices and told parliament to fix the rules by the end of 1996.[6] The government coalition drafted a repeal. The budget committee of the Bundestag struck it out. Its report says the law formally stays on the books and cannot be collected, because of the court's decision.[7]

The states, who received the money, asked the mediation committee to change the law along the lines of their own bill on a new regulation of the wealth tax and the inheritance tax, and to compensate them in full if the wealth tax ended on 1 January 1997.[8] Nothing was levied after 1997.[23] The valuation problem has an answer already in use: a 2026 study of the Left Party's proposal values real estate and companies near market value, by the rules Germany applies to inheritance tax.[9]

## France: the best-measured repeal {#france}

France's wealth tax raised €4.2 billion in 2017. In 2018 it was replaced by a tax on real estate alone. Two thirds of the households that paid the old tax in 2017 were not liable for the new one in 2018, and across all former payers the average bill fell from €8,000 to €1,600.[10]

{{chart: france-cost}}

The new tax raised €1.29 billion in 2018 and €1.83 billion in 2022. In the committee's calculation the new tax raised 29% of what the wealth tax would have raised in 2018, a loss of €3.1 billion, and, if the old tax had grown as fast as the new one, €4.5 billion in 2022.[10] The cap that gave the richest their 0.1% is still there. When the committee reported in 2023, it still helped about 2,000 households, including about half of those declaring more than €20 million of taxable real estate.[10]

Did the repeal keep people from leaving? Under the wealth tax, from 2011 to 2016, about 950 taxpayers a year left France and 370 came back. Under its replacement, from 2018 to 2021, about 260 a year left and 380 came back. The committee called the budget stake in these movements probably secondary. It also could find no effect of the old tax on the activity of mid-sized family firms.[10] France gave up billions a year, and the departures the repeal was meant to stop were a few hundred a year.

## Austria and the Netherlands: swapped, not repealed {#swaps}

Austria abolished its wealth tax from 1 January 1994 inside a large tax package. The wealth tax was worth about ATS 8.3 billion a year, and the package raised the payroll tax by about the same, ATS 8.3 billion.[11] In euros, each is about €600 million. The Greens noted in the committee report that the payroll tax went from 2% to 3%, and that private households, too, were relieved of the wealth tax.[12] Austria's taxes on wealth as a whole had raised 1.1% of GDP, against 1.9% in the European Community and 2.1% across the OECD, and the Greens' point was that after repeal private wealth would in effect no longer be taxed.[12] The reason in the committee papers was company equity: a tax on business assets was said to hold back firms' equity, and other industrial countries taxed it less.[12] Work paid for the change.

The Dutch government's 2025 memo says the problem of the system before 2001 was that gains on assets went untaxed, and that this made it easy to build constructions around the tax.[13] In 2001 the Netherlands replaced its wealth tax with box 3, a tax on an assumed return from savings and investments. It did not repeal the taxing of wealth. In 2026 box 3 charges 36% on an assumed 6.00% return on investments, which is 2.16% of the holding above €59,357 a year.[14] Company shares of 5% or more sit in another box, taxed only when profit is paid out. The top 0.01% of incomes pay out 14% of their company profit.[15] The Dutch kept a wealth tax for savers and left out the largest fortunes. That is the opposite of fixing the holes.

## Did growth follow the repeals? {#growth}

The promise behind repeal was that economies freed from the tax would race ahead. The six repeals that are ten years old or more are a test of it. Measure each country's growth in GDP per person against the OECD average, which also takes the 2008 crisis out of the comparison, for the ten years before and the ten after.[25]

{{chart: repeal-growth}}

In none of the six did growth speed up relative to the OECD after repeal. Sweden went from 1.01 points a year ahead to 0.23 behind, Finland from 1.54 ahead to 0.86 behind, the Netherlands from 0.62 ahead to 0.07 behind and Germany from 0.05 ahead to 0.34 behind. Denmark stayed 0.27 and 0.25 behind and Austria 0.05 and 0.03 ahead.[25][23] What this shows is that no country that scrapped its wealth tax pulled ahead of its peers afterwards. The page on [growth](/arguments/hurts-growth#countries) has the rest of the evidence, including what happened to investment where the tax was kept.

## Who kept it, and what it raises {#stayed}

Norway, Switzerland, Spain, Colombia, Uruguay, Argentina and Bolivia all levy a wealth tax today.[23] In 2016 the four OECD countries that levied one raised between 0.18% and 1.03% of GDP.[1] The OECD explains the differences with design: the exemptions, thresholds, tax schedule and treatment of debts, and how much owners avoid.[1]

{{chart: revenue-gdp}}

Those small shares are what the holes produce. France's richest 0.001% paid 0.1%, and the G20 report puts the tax the world's billionaires pay at about 0.3% of their wealth, including in Norway, where the top statutory rate was 0.85%.[10][17] Where a tax is kept and its rates are raised, it collects. Norway's receipts rose from NOK 18.5 billion in 2021 to NOK 31.8 billion in 2024, up 72%.[16]

Some countries went back. Spain gave every taxpayer a 100% credit in 2008 and reinstated its tax in 2011, which the OECD says was meant to be temporary and has been kept. Iceland reinstated its tax from 2010.[1] Spain added a second tax in 2022: the solidarity tax, charged on net wealth above €3 million at 1.7% to 3.5% on top of the regional wealth tax. The law says its purpose is to give a fair share from the largest fortunes and to keep the burden from differing sharply between regions that tax wealth and regions that have dropped the tax.[20] Colombia created a wealth tax in its 2022 law, charged from 72,000 tax units at 0.5% to 1.5%, with the 1.5% rate written for 2023 to 2026.[18] Bolivia's law of December 2020 charges 1.4% to 2.4% on net fortunes above 30 million bolivianos from 2020.[19]

## What is new {#new}

Tunisia's parliament passed an annual wealth tax in its finance law for 2026, law no. 17 of 12 December 2025, published in the Official Gazette: 0.5% on net wealth of 3 million dinars and 1% above 5 million.[21] Venezuela's law of 2019 charges 0.25% to 1.5% on the net wealth of the largest taxpayers, those above 150 million tax units.[22] The register counts 10 countries with a wealth tax in force and 13 that used to have one, as of 13 November 2026.[23]

The design that answers every hole at once is the minimum tax. The 2024 report for the G20 proposes that billionaires pay at least 2% of their wealth in tax each year. It would raise $200 billion to $250 billion a year worldwide from about 3,000 taxpayers.[17] It has no exemptions for owners of companies, no cap tied to income, and it starts at a threshold no middle-class household will ever reach.

In France the bill for such a floor, a minimum of 2% on households worth more than €100 million, was adopted by the National Assembly at first reading on 20 February 2025 and rejected by the Senate on 12 June 2025, and the Assembly's record shows no outcome after that as of 12 November 2026.[24] It is a bill, not a law. It shows that the country that scrapped its tax in 2018 has had a vote for a new one in one chamber.

## Fix it, don't repeal it {#fix}

Every fault the repeal documents named has a known repair.

- Exemptions. Give business owners no blanket exemption. The OECD says that where one exists it needs clear criteria, because the exemption is used for avoidance.[1]
- Caps tied to income. Take them out. The OECD lists them as an avoidance route, and they cost France about €2 billion a year.[1][10]
- Old values. Value every asset near market value, as Germany does for inheritance tax and as the DIW study applies to a wealth tax.[9]
- Low thresholds. Aim at the top. A minimum at $1 billion reaches about 3,000 taxpayers in the world, and the French bill starts at €100 million.[17][24]
- Leaving. Charge an exit tax that follows the person, as [they will leave](/arguments/they-will-leave#follows) sets out.

A repeal does the opposite. It gives up the revenue from the people who had no loophole, and the people who did keep the advantage they had before.

## Who says so {#who}

The tax's holes were named by the people who then repealed it. Sweden's government wrote down the loopholes in 2007, and the union confederation LO told parliament to close them instead.[2] Denmark's finance minister, who negotiated the repeal, wrote 25 years later that the tax had been eroded and that a new one, with no discounts and no ceiling, would be worth many times more.[5] France's state evaluators measured the loss.[10] The [Swedish](/se), [Danish](/dk), [German](/de), [French](/fr), [Austrian](/at) and [Dutch](/nl) stories have the full record and the people who now argue for a new tax.
