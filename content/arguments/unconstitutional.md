The US Constitution does not say Congress may not tax wealth. It says how one kind of tax must be shared out. A "direct" tax has to be divided among the states by population, so a state with 5% of the people pays 5% of the total, whatever its wealth.[1][2] Every other tax, the duties, imposts and excises, only has to carry the same rate in every state.[1] Congress can choose either road. It has taken both, and a wealth tax can be written for either.

That is why "unconstitutional" is a question about drafting. A wealth tax laid as a charge on the privilege of holding a billion dollars, or as a direct tax with the sharing formula written in, does what the text asks. Two law professors who reviewed the long line of cases on the question conclude that wealth taxes drafted this way are constitutional.[5] The Supreme Court has never ruled on a tax on net wealth. In 2024 it said in so many words that it was not deciding one.[2]

Outside the United States the question has been put to courts in Germany, France, Spain, Switzerland and Colombia. In none of the rulings below did a court hold that taxing wealth is forbidden. Each time the tax stood, and where a court found a flaw it was a flaw of design: unequal valuation, or a rule that reached assets the owner could not use.

## What the clauses say {#clause}

The rule sits in two places. Article I, section 2 says "Representatives and direct Taxes shall be apportioned among the several States." Section 9 repeats it: no "other direct, Tax shall be laid, unless in Proportion to the Census."[1] Section 8, which gives Congress its power to tax, adds that "all Duties, Imposts and Excises shall be uniform throughout the United States."[1]

So there are two roads. On the first, a tax is shared among the states by population. On the second, it is an excise or duty and carries one rate everywhere. The Supreme Court wrote in 2024 that direct taxes are, in general, those laid on persons or property, and that Congress has rarely used them because of the formula.[2] The formula is awkward, since a poorer state would need a higher rate to raise its share. It is a formula and not a ban. Brooks and Gamage count five apportioned taxes on real property and enslaved persons that Congress passed from 1798, and find that none was ever challenged as unconstitutional.[5]

The founders did not read "direct" widely. In 1796 the Court upheld a tax on carriages, and the justices who gave reasons suggested that direct taxes meant head taxes and taxes on land.[2]

## What Pollock did, and what the Sixteenth Amendment undid {#pollock}

The objection rests on one old decision. In 1895 the Supreme Court held in *Pollock v. Farmers' Loan and Trust* that a tax on the income of land is a direct tax, and that so is a tax on personal property or the income from it. The income tax of 1894 was not divided among the states, so the Court voided it.[3] The same opinion left taxes on "business, privileges, or employments" alone, and noted that some had long been upheld as excises.[3]

The country answered with the Sixteenth Amendment in 1913, which lets Congress tax incomes "without apportionment." The Court says today that the Amendment rejected Pollock's habit of treating a tax on income from property as a tax on the property.[2] So the decision people cite has lost most of its force on income. What it still says is that a plain tax on owning property is direct and would have to be shared among the states. That is the only part a wealth tax needs to plan for, and the next sections do.

## What the Supreme Court said in 2024, and what it left open {#moore}

*Moore v. United States* was brought by a couple who paid $14,729 on their share of a foreign company's profits that they had never received.[2] On 20 June 2024 the Court upheld the tax, which was a one-time charge of 8% to 15.5% on American shareholders in foreign companies.[2] Five justices joined the opinion, two more agreed with the result, and two dissented.[2]

The Court was careful about what it was not saying. Its opinion states that it does not address taxes on holdings, wealth or net worth, nor taxes on appreciation.[2] It added that the government had told it an unapportioned tax on someone's wealth "might be considered a tax on property, not income."[2] That is the one exposed point for a plain annual tax on net worth, and the designs below are built around it.

Four justices went further on a different question, whether income must be realised, meaning sold or paid out, before it can be taxed without apportionment. Justice Barrett, joined by Justice Alito, wrote that it must, and so did Justice Thomas, joined by Justice Gorsuch. Justice Jackson wrote that nothing in the Constitution requires it. The other four did not decide.[2] That is a ruling about taxes on income, and a wealth tax is not one. An excise or an apportioned tax does not turn on it. Even for a tax on income, a gain taxed when the asset is sold is realised on every justice's reading.

## Three ways to draft it {#designs}

**An excise on the privilege.** The Court has upheld taxes measured by the value of property when they were laid on a privilege or activity. In 1899 it upheld a stamp tax on sales at the Chicago Board of Trade, assessed on the value of what was sold, and an estate tax, both as duties or excises. In 1911 it upheld a tax on the privilege of doing business as a corporation, measured by income.[5] Brooks and Gamage read these cases as a rule that courts defer to the form Congress chooses, if the label is reasonable. Their draft is a charge on the activity of holding extreme wealth, or on the income that wealth is presumed to earn, at one rate across the country.[5]

**A tax with the sharing formula built in.** Brooks and Gamage add a fallback: if the Court said the tax was direct, it would convert into an apportioned one. The unfairness to poorer states, which would face a higher rate, is repaired by handing back the excess to them in a rebate programme.[5] In their analysis a tax written this way should survive whichever way the Court rules, and Congress did pass apportioned taxes five times.[5]

**A minimum tax on income that counts unrealised gains, and a charge when the wealth moves.** *Moore* shows how much room Congress has to tax income that sits inside a company, since the Court upheld a tax on profits the shareholders never received.[2] The four justices on the realisation side are the risk to a minimum income tax. The answer is to build it so it does not depend on them: a gain taxed when the asset is sold is realised on anyone's reading, and a charge when an owner leaves the country or dies can be written the same way. How such a tax is valued and paid is covered in [the page on valuation](/arguments/you-cannot-value-it) and [the page on cash](/arguments/no-cash).

Law professors Dawn Johnsen and Walter Dellinger wrote in 2018 that the belief that the Constitution makes a national wealth tax impossible is wrong, and that it has been repeated carelessly, with harmful effects.[4] They asked that the choice be made by elected lawmakers on policy grounds.[4]

## California's vote {#california}

California put a wealth tax on billionaires to a vote. Proposition 40 was a one-time 5% tax on the net worth of residents with more than $1 billion on 1 January 2026, with real estate and many pensions left out.[6] On 3 November 2026 voters rejected it by 52.8% to 47.2%, the Associated Press reported on 9 November.[8][9] Proposition 42, which would ban new state taxes on personal property including shares and business interests, was ahead that evening by 51.6% to 48.4%. The count was not yet certified.[7][9]

That was a vote on policy, and no court ruled on Proposition 40. Two points about it matter here. The apportionment rule is a limit on Congress, written into Article I's list of what Congress may do, and the Court's 2024 opinion speaks only of Congress.[1][2] And Proposition 42 shows where a ban on wealth taxes has to come from when someone wants one: it is written into a constitution as an amendment.[7] Anyone who wants the United States to have no wealth tax has to write that rule too.

## What courts did in other countries {#abroad}

**Germany.** In 1995 the Federal Constitutional Court found one flaw in the wealth tax: the same rate was applied to land at values from 1964 and to other assets at current values. It declared that rule incompatible with the equality clause, left the law in force and gave parliament until 31 December 1996 to fix it.[10] It did not forbid the tax. Opponents quote a passage in the reasoning saying the total burden on a return should stay near half. In 2006 the Court wrote that this passage is not binding, that the operative part concerns only equality, and that there is no absolute ceiling near an even split.[10][11] What it asked for was equal valuation, a job for the tax office, and [the page on valuation](/arguments/you-cannot-value-it) shows how it is done.

**France.** In 2010 the Conseil constitutionnel found the articles of the wealth tax (the ISF) conform to the Constitution. It said the base may include assets that produce no income, and that taking ability to pay into account does not mean taxing only income-producing assets.[12] It looked again in 2012, at an ISF with a top rate of 1.5% on wealth above €10 million. It held that this rate respects ability to pay and does not breach property rights, and that capping the ISF and income tax together at 75% of income was acceptable.[13] It struck two rules. One counted, in that cap, gains the owner had not received. The other taxed a shareholder on the assets of a company in proportion to his stake, though it was not shown he could use them.[13] Both are lessons for drafting. A design that taxes the shares at their market value needs neither.

**Spain.** On 7 November 2023 the Constitutional Court dismissed Madrid's challenge to the temporary solidarity tax on great fortunes in full. Seven of the eleven judges voted to dismiss, and four dissented.[14] Madrid had argued that the rates were excessive for the return on the assets. The Court answered that a wealth tax is confiscatory only if it uses up the wealth, and not the income it earns, and Madrid had not argued that it did.[14] It added that the state, not the regions, has held the field of wealth taxation since 1977. Regions can cut their own wealth tax to zero, which does not take the national tax away.[14]

**Switzerland.** In 1980 the Federal Supreme Court heard a shareholder with about CHF 35 million in company shares who said his combined wealth and income tax was confiscatory. In 1973 and 1974 it had exceeded his investment income. The Court said no fixed rate decides the question. What counts is the rate, the base, how long it lasts and how it adds up with other taxes. A one-off burden did not hollow out his wealth, and only a high burden made permanent would. It also upheld valuing listed shares at their market price.[15]

**Colombia.** In February 2025 the Constitutional Court received two challenges to the wealth tax of Law 2277 of 2022 and declined to decide them, finding the claims too weakly argued. It noted that the challengers ignored the tax's built-in softeners: a threshold of 72,000 tax units, a 12,000-unit exclusion of the main home, and graduated rates.[16] The tax was not touched.

## The record in one paragraph {#case}

A constitution is a text with a procedure in it. The American one has two roads for a tax and a formula for one of them. Abroad, courts that looked at wealth taxes asked for equal valuation, an ability-to-pay test, and no confiscation, and all three can be met. When a design fails a court test, the fix is a change of design, or an amendment, as the United States made in 1913. What the record does not contain is a court holding that the idea of taxing wealth is itself forbidden.
