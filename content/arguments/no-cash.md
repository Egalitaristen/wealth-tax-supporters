The picture behind the objection is a farmer with land worth millions and a few thousand in the bank, or a retired couple in a paid-off house. A wealth tax, the objection says, makes them sell. Look instead at who the strongest wealth taxes are written for, and what their wealth does. The wealth of billionaires has earned 7.5% a year after inflation since the 1980s. A 2% minimum tax takes 27 cents of every dollar of that return and leaves them 5.5%.[1]

The cash is there. The 400 richest Americans own companies that pay out 19% of their pre-tax income as dividends when listed and 7.4% when private. The rest stays inside, where the owner controls it.[2] And the very richest already turn shares into spending with loans against them, which is how they live today.[1] A wealth tax asks them to find a sum that is small beside what they earn, from sources they already use. For the people the objection really has in mind, thresholds, discounts on the home and a right to defer do the work. The sections below take the owners in turn.

## The size of the bill {#size}

A tax of 2% of wealth sounds like a bite out of the capital. Measure it against what the capital earns. If the wealth earns 7.5% a year, the tax is 2 ÷ 7.5, or 27%, of one year's return.[1] The owner keeps nearly three quarters of the year's earnings, and the fortune still grows by 5.5%.

{{chart: return-split}}

Now measure it against what the company keeps. A firm that earns 7.5% of its owner's wealth and pays out 19% of it as dividends holds on to 6.1% of the wealth each year (7.5 × 0.81). A 2% tax is 33% of that. If the firm is private and pays out 7.4%, the share is 29%.[1][2] Either way the tax is about a third of the profit already sitting in the company, and the owner decides how much of it to move.

Or measure it in shares. Listed shares are 42% of the wealth of the 400 richest Americans, so an owner who sold shares alone to pay a 2% tax would sell 2 ÷ 42, or 4.8%, of them: about one share in twenty.[2] The wealth earns 7.5% a year, more than the share sold.

## Where the cash is {#cash}

Among the very rich, the money is kept in companies, and that is the owner's choice. In Norway and Sweden the companies of people in the top 0.0005% pay out only 15% to 20% of their profit as income to the owner over two decades. For people who are rich but not super rich, 50% to 60% is paid out.[3] The owners at the top are not short of money. They hold it in a form that is not taxed until it is paid out, and a wealth tax is the one tax that reaches it there.

Norway is the test, because it nearly doubled the wealth tax on business owners between 2021 and 2023. The firms owned by people who pay it paid out no more in dividends, invested no less and went bankrupt no more often than firms owned by foreigners and others outside the tax.[4] On average the tax was not paid by pulling money out of operating companies. Owners drew on their holding companies instead, which pay them directly.[4] A second Norwegian study compared neighbours on either side of a valuation boundary. Those who got the higher bill saved more, mostly by earning more, and there was no clear sign that they sold their homes.[5]

## What the richest do already {#borrow}

A billionaire who wants to spend does not have to sell. They can borrow against their shares and spend the proceeds, which are not taxed.[1] The access to cash is there. A wealth tax of 1% or 2% is a small draw on the same credit. The pillar page shows how this works: [what reaches the bank account](/arguments/why-tax-wealth#bank).

## When an owner has the wealth and not the cash {#deferral}

Some owners are different, and the law has answers ready for them.

- **Let the tax wait.** Norway lets a private person defer the wealth tax on business assets for up to three years, if the amount is NOK 30,000 or more. The deferral carries interest at the central bank's rate plus 5 points, which was 9.25% at a policy rate of 4.25%. So it is no free loan, and the first year it can be used is 2026.[7]
- **Spread it.** The design for billionaires in the report to the G20 lets them pay the minimum tax in five equal annual instalments. Because their returns are high, the instalments from good years cover the bad ones.[1]
- **Hold the tax against the company.** California's Proposition 40 would have taxed billionaires 5% once, payable in five instalments with a 7.5% yearly charge on the unpaid balance. For a founder whose tax would exceed all the publicly traded shares they own, it offered a deferral account: nothing is paid until money leaves the company, and then 5% of each withdrawal. The account binds the founder's estate and follows them if they leave the state.[9] Voters rejected the measure on 3 November 2026, 52.8% to 47.2%, so it is a drafted design and not a law.[11] It shows that the hardest case, a founder with a private company and no spare cash, has a written answer.
- **Cap the tax against income.** France once limited all taxes to 75% of income and Spain to 60%, with a floor of 20% of the wealth tax that must always be paid. Seven of Switzerland's 26 cantons have a limit of some kind.[6]

The G20 report is plain that liquidity problems can be real for owners of assets with a low return. That is why its minimum tax is aimed at billionaires, whose wealth earns 7.5%, and why the tools above exist for any broader tax.[1]

## The farmer, the pensioner and the small firm {#farmers}

The objection pictures a person far below the people a minimum tax reaches. A $1 billion threshold covers about 3,000 taxpayers on Earth.[1] A farm worth $5 million, a family firm worth $200 million and a start-up worth $50 million are all below it.

Where a wealth tax starts lower, the homes of ordinary people are treated specially. Norway counts a primary home at 25% of its value (a 75% discount) and subtracts the mortgage in full, so a household with a house and a large mortgage can fall out of the tax.[5][8] France gives a 30% allowance on the main residence, Spain one of up to €300,000, and Switzerland values housing at 60% of its market value. Several countries also treat agricultural land preferentially.[6] The OECD gives the reason in one line: owner-occupied housing does not generate the income needed to pay the tax.[6] It also says liquidity problems become less significant if the threshold is high enough, because they are mostly a problem of real property, and notes that retirees tend to sell their assets or spend their savings as they age.[6]

We already live with this case. A retired owner with a paid-off house and a small pension owes property tax in full every year. In Texas, anyone aged 65 or older can defer the tax on their home, the unpaid tax stays as a lien, it carries 5% interest and it falls due when they stop living there.[10] Nobody proposes scrapping property tax because some owners are house-rich and cash-poor. The same remedy, a lien and interest until the asset is sold, works for a tax on a larger fortune.

## What follows {#design}

An owner short of cash has five exits: pay out of the return the assets earn, sell a slice, borrow against the assets, take a dividend, or defer. For billionaires the tax is a quarter of the return, a third of the profit kept inside, or one share in twenty. For everyone below them, thresholds, home discounts and deferral keep the tax from forcing a sale. The design that gets this right sets a high threshold, counts every asset at its market value, and offers a deferral with interest to the few who have no cash.
