# Arguments research: growth, public funds, family firms

Fact sheet for the pages "It hurts growth", "It doesn't raise enough" and "It kills family firms".
Opened on 2026-11-08 unless an entry says otherwise. Not published content: nothing here may be copied into `content/` without a check against the source.

## How this was built

- Primary sources only: papers, working-paper PDFs, official reports. Each PDF was downloaded and converted to text, then searched and read in the passages cited. "PDF p." is the page of the PDF file, not the printed page.
- The abstract, introduction and the sections named in each entry were read. The papers were not read line by line from cover to cover. No AI chat tool's content was used or is credited.
- Where a site refused a direct download (IMF, OECD, DIW, archive.org full text), the entry says which copy was used. IMF and OECD PDFs were read from Internet Archive (web.archive.org) snapshots of the official URL.
- Quotes are short on purpose. Wording outside quotation marks is a paraphrase.
- The brief named "Zoutman, Seim, Londoño-Vélez and Ávila on Colombia". Londoño-Vélez and Ávila-Mahecha are the Colombia paper. Seim is Sweden. Zoutman is the Netherlands. Zoutman was not opened; he is reported only through Scheuer and Slemrod (entry 12).
- The brief says Kuznets published in The New Leader. Every citation found says The New Republic. See part 3.

## Part 1. Do wealth taxes reduce growth, investment, entrepreneurship, saving?

### 1.1 Studies with favourable or modest findings

**1.** Migration of the wealthy is real but the aggregate cost is small (Sweden and Denmark).
- Figure: a 1 pp rise in the top wealth tax rate cuts the stock of wealthy taxpayers by about 2%, and cuts aggregate employment by 0.02%, investment by 0.07% and value added by 0.10%.
- Quote (abstract, PDF p.2): "The aggregate effects are nevertheless modest".
- Design: Swedish repeal of 2006 (difference-in-differences) and two Danish reforms, with event studies of owner out-migration. Administrative data.
- Source: Jakobsen, Kleven, Kolsrud, Landais and Muñoz, "Taxing Top Wealth: Migration Responses and their Aggregate Economic Implications", NBER WP 32153, Feb 2024, revised Jan 2026. https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32153/w32153.pdf (full text read: abstract, introduction). NBER's web page says it was later published in the American Economic Review 116(10) (2026); that line comes from the NBER page and the AER version was not opened.
- Limits: working paper version; modelled aggregate effects, not observed GDP; Nordic countries with strong tax administration; effects are per 1 pp, so a high rate multiplies them.

**2.** Same paper, UNFAVOURABLE parts, stated exactly.
- Figures: when an entrepreneur subject to the wealth tax leaves, employment in their businesses falls 33%, gross investment 22%, value added 34%, tax payments 51% (introduction, PDF p.4). Driven mostly by firm closure. 45% of firms closed by owners on out-migration end up absorbed by other Swedish firms.
- Figure: "migration responses substantially increase the Marginal Cost of Public Funds (MCPF) associated with wealth taxes, from about 2.2 to 4.2" (PDF p.5). Of each extra dollar of revenue, 0.22 dollars are lost to migration and 0.54 dollars to intensive-margin responses (saving, investment, avoidance, evasion), using the Danish study (entry 3).
- Meaning: a wealth tax as designed in Denmark and Sweden had a high cost per krona raised. The authors still find repeal would not pay for itself.
- Also: individuals in the top 2% of wealth control close to 10% of Swedish employment and 15% of value added through private businesses (PDF p.2-3).

**3.** Wealth accumulation responds to the tax (Denmark 1989-97).
- Figures: taxable wealth effects of the 1989 cuts of about 30% after 8 years for the very wealthy (top 1% "ceiling" design) and about 10% after 8 years for the moderately wealthy (couples threshold design). Mechanical effects are about one quarter and one tenth of these (introduction). Model long run: about 70% (very wealthy) and about 20% (moderately wealthy) after 30 years (introduction).
- Quote: the very wealthy estimates "represent upper bounds on real wealth accumulation responses" (introduction).
- Setting: marginal rate 2.2% up to the late 1980s, exemption near the 98th percentile (PDF p.3); tax reduced from 1989, abolished 1997.
- Source: Jakobsen, Jakobsen, Kleven and Zucman, NBER WP 24371 (March 2018). Published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics 135(1): 329-388 (2020). https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24371/w24371.pdf (NBER version read; the QJE version was not opened, so numbers may differ slightly).
- Limits: includes avoidance and evasion at the top (authors say so); Denmark's tax had a ceiling and reliefs; this measures taxable wealth, not saving or growth directly.

**4.** Switzerland: declared wealth falls with higher cantonal rates, mostly not real saving.
- Figure: a 1 pp drop in a canton's wealth tax rate raises reported taxable wealth by at least 43% after 6 years. In Lucerne versus Bern, 24% of the effect is taxpayer mobility and 21% a concurrent rise in house prices.
- Quote (abstract): "Savings responses appear unable to explain more than a small fraction of the remainder".
- Source: Brülhart, Gruber, Krapf and Schmidheiny, "Behavioral Responses to Wealth Taxes: Evidence from Switzerland", AEJ: Economic Policy 14(4): 111-150 (2022); author version of 28 Oct 2021 read. https://www.aeaweb.org/content/file?id=15737
- Limits: declared wealth, not true wealth; Swiss cantons have low thresholds and no third-party reporting of financial wealth; mobility between cantons is easier than between countries. Scheuer and Slemrod (entry 12) say 96% for the subset of large reforms. Does not measure investment, firms or growth.

**5.** Spain (Catalonia 2011-15): avoidance, not lower saving.
- Figures: elasticity of taxable wealth to the net-of-tax return of 0.64; a 0.1 pp rise in the average rate cuts taxable wealth by 3.24% over 4 years; avoidance equals "a 4-year accumulated revenue loss of 2.6 times the 2011 estimated wealth tax revenues".
- Quote (abstract): "while facing higher wealth taxes did not have a negative effect on taxpayers' savings".
- Detail: taxpayers moved into exempt assets, mostly business assets, and used a cap linking income and wealth tax. The paper also reports that, if the tax is assumed paid out of savings, the most exposed taxpayers' saving rises (elasticity -0.1) and offers several possible explanations.
- Source: Durán-Cabré, Esteller-Moré and Mas-Montserrat, "Behavioural responses to the (re)introduction of wealth taxes. Evidence from Spain", IEB Working Paper 2019/04. http://ieb.ub.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/2019-IEB-WorkingPaper-04.pdf (abstract, introduction and results read).
- Limits: Catalonia only, unpublished working paper in the version read; design flaws (business exemption, liability cap) drive the avoidance, so it argues for a broad base.

**6.** Colombia: evasion at the very top; enforcement works.
- Figures: two-fifths of the wealthiest 0.01% evaded, hiding about one third of their wealth offshore; the wealthiest 0.01% are 55 times more likely to evade than the top 5%. Three years after disclosure, disclosers report 49.2% more wealth and pay 39% more income tax. The Panama Papers leak raised disclosure by 27 pp.
- Quote (abstract): "strengthening enforcement can have a significant impact on wealth tax compliance, tax revenue, and progressivity".
- Source: Londoño-Vélez and Ávila-Mahecha, "Enforcing Wealth Taxes in the Developing World: Quasi-Experimental Evidence from Colombia", AER: Insights 3(2): 131-148 (2021). https://eml.berkeley.edu/~saez/course/londonoAERI21.pdf (read: abstract, introduction). Earlier title: "Can Wealth Taxation Work in Developing Countries?".
- Limits: about evasion, not growth, investment or firms. Scheuer and Slemrod (footnote 9) report a short-run 2% increase in reported wealth per 1 pp wealth tax cut from an earlier version; not verified in the published paper.

**7.** Sweden 2000-06: small responses, mostly reporting.
- Figures: elasticities of taxable wealth with respect to the net-of-tax rate in the range [0.09, 0.27]; around a third is under-reporting. Responses "reflect evasion and avoidance rather than changes in saving" (abstract).
- Source: Seim, AEJ: Economic Policy 9(4): 395-421 (2017). https://eml.berkeley.edu/~saez/course/seimAEJ17wealth.pdf (abstract and introduction read).
- Limits: bunching at one kink (0% to 1.5%); measures reporting near the threshold.

**8.** Norway 2010s: wealth taxation raises saving.
- Figure: "Each additional NOK of wealth tax increases annual net financial saving by 3.76", mainly through higher labour earnings (abstract).
- Source: Ring, "Wealth Taxation and Household Saving: Evidence from Assessment Discontinuities in Norway", Review of Economic Studies 92(5): 3375-3402 (2025). Manuscript of 27 Sep 2024 read: https://restud.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/MS31276Manuscript.pdf
- Limits: households near geographic assessment breaks, mostly not the very rich; says income effects may dominate; saving is not investment or growth.

**9.** Norway 2021-25 reforms: limited migration, no investment drop, no extra bankruptcies.
- Figures: "at most 100 individuals left due to the reforms"; responses concentrated in the top 0.1% and mostly in 2022-23; wealth tax revenue up 75% 2021-24 without migration, 71.5% with it; "no evidence that Norwegian-owned firms pay out more dividends or invest less", nor "that the reform induced more bankruptcies" (abstract, PDF p.2).
- Source: Ring, "Taxing Entrepreneurial Wealth: Evidence from Norway, 2021-2025", NBER WP 35854, Oct 2026. https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w35854/w35854.pdf (abstract and introduction read).
- Limits: new working paper, short horizon (4 years); the author calls the costs found "short-run". Not peer reviewed.

### 1.2 The overview sources (read the cautious parts)

**10.** OECD 2018: the strongest official statement AGAINST a net wealth tax.
- Quote (executive summary): "there are limited arguments for having a net wealth tax in addition to broad-based personal capital income taxes and well-designed inheritance and gift taxes". It adds that net wealth taxes "tend to be more distortive and less equitable". Revenue "very low, with a few exceptions"; 12 OECD countries had them in 1990, four in 2017.
- On entrepreneurship (PDF p.65): "it is therefore difficult to firmly argue that wealth taxes would have negative effects on entrepreneurship." It also says the size of the effect "is also unclear as business assets are often excluded from the wealth tax base in practice".
- On growth: no estimate. It reports that recurrent taxes on immovable property are "the least damaging tax to long-run economic growth" (PDF p.25, citing OECD 2010); that is a different tax.
- Source: OECD, The Role and Design of Net Wealth Taxes in the OECD, Tax Policy Studies No. 26 (2018). https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2018/04/the-role-and-design-of-net-wealth-taxes-in-the-oecd_g1g89919/9789264290303-en.pdf ; summary https://www.oecd.org/ctp/tax-policy/role-and-design-of-net-wealth-taxes-in-the-OECD-summary.pdf (executive summary, chapters 1, 3, 4 read).
- Limits: written 2018, before the Danish, Swiss, Norwegian studies above; argues for capital income and inheritance taxes as alternatives, not for no tax.

**11.** OECD 2018 on tax design: unproductive versus productive assets.
- Quote (PDF p.64): "drawing a clear distinction between productive and unproductive assets is challenging."
- Meaning: a tax biased to housing can reduce investment in "unproductive" assets, but the report does not endorse this.

**12.** Scheuer and Slemrod, "Taxing Our Wealth", Journal of Economic Perspectives 35(1): 207-230 (2021); NBER WP 28150 (Nov 2020) read. https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28150/w28150.pdf
- Quote (abstract): "Studies of these taxes sometimes, but not always, find a substantial behavioral response". Impact "depends crucially on design features, especially the broadness of the base and enforcement provisions".
- Reports (PDF p.13-14, secondary to the studies above): Switzerland 43% (96% for large reforms); Denmark 21% after 8 years per 1 pp; Catalonia 32% over four years per 1 pp; Madrid 10% more wealthy residents after five years (Agrawal et al.); Netherlands (Zoutman 2018) 14% long-run per 1 pp; Sweden 0.10 to 0.27% (Seim).
- On entrepreneurship (PDF p.15-16): "the key effects may be on the incentives for entry into entrepreneurship" and on dilution of founders' control; the paper says an exposure only in low-probability high-outcome states could be "modest". No estimate.
- Limits: the 43%, 21%, 32% are different units of "per 1 pp": do not compare them to one another or to Danish 30%.
- Note: the Zoutman figure is second-hand and was not checked in Zoutman's paper.

### 1.3 What the evidence does not show

- No study opened estimates the effect of a wealth tax on national GDP growth. The closest are entry 1 (modelled employment, investment and value added from migration only), and entries 5, 8 and 9 (saving, dividends, investment, bankruptcy for firms).
- No study opened estimates entry rates for new firms in a wealth-tax country. Scheuer and Slemrod give theory only. Ring (entry 9) finds no fall in inflows of startup founders to Norway.
- Existing wealth taxes were mostly narrow-based with reliefs. Evidence about a broad, high-threshold tax is by extension.

## Part 2. Public revenue and spending raising growth and investment

**13.** IMF: public investment raises output; strongest with slack and efficient projects.
- Figure: "An unanticipated 1 percentage point of GDP increase in investment spending increases the level of output by about 0.4 percent in the same year and by 1.5 percent four years after the shock." That implies multipliers of about 0.4 and 1.4.
- Quote (chapter summary): "Increased public investment raises output, both in the short term because of demand effects and in the long term as a result of supply effects."
- Conditions: where project efficiency is low, "increased public investment leads to more limited long-term output gains". Quote (summary): "an increase in public investment that is debt financed could have larger output effects than one that is budget neutral", with a warning against a blanket recommendation because of financing costs at high debt.
- Source: IMF, World Economic Outlook, October 2014, Chapter 3 "Is It Time for an Infrastructure Push? The Macroeconomic Effects of Public Investment". Copy read: https://web.archive.org/web/2020/https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/weo/2014/02/pdf/c3.pdf (PDF p.8 for the estimate; summary at the start).
- Limits: advanced-economy panel; the +1.5% is for a debt-financed shock. Do not use as a multiplier for a tax-financed programme. Do not write that "every public project pays for itself": the chapter says self-financing only if multipliers, efficiency and the output elasticity are "sufficiently high".

**14.** IMF staff: redistribution is not generally bad for growth.
- Quote (PDF p.4): "redistribution appears generally benign in terms of its impact on growth; only in extreme cases is there some evidence that it may have direct negative effects on growth."
- Also: "lower net inequality is robustly correlated with faster and more durable growth, for a given level of redistribution."
- Source: Ostry, Berg and Tsangarides, "Redistribution, Inequality, and Growth", IMF Staff Discussion Note SDN/14/02 (Feb 2014). https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/sdn/2014/sdn1402.pdf (introduction and conclusion read).
- Limits: cross-country panel regressions on market versus net Gini; the authors say to "be cognizant of the inherent limitations". It disclaims IMF views ("does not necessarily represent IMF views"). It studies redistribution broadly (transfers and taxes), not wealth taxes. Peer-reviewed follow-up in the Journal of Economic Growth (2018) was not opened.

**15.** OECD: inequality lowers medium-term growth.
- Figures: a rise of 3 Gini points "would drag down economic growth by 0.35 percentage point per year for 25 years: a cumulated loss in GDP at the end of the period of 8.5 per cent" (OECD Focus on Inequality and Growth, Dec 2014, PDF p.2). In Cingano (2014): lowering inequality by 1 Gini point raises cumulative growth by 0.8 pp over the following 5 years (PDF p.18); the Focus text says the effect comes "not by the top income decile but actually by the bottom 40% of income earners" (PDF p.2).
- The Focus text says estimates "are meant to be illustrative and should not be interpreted as the causal effect".
- Sources: OECD, Focus on Inequality and Growth, Dec 2014: https://web.archive.org/web/2020/https://www.oecd.org/els/soc/Focus-Inequality-and-Growth-2014.pdf ; Cingano, "Trends in Income Inequality and its Impact on Economic Growth", OECD SEM Working Paper 163 (2014): https://web.archive.org/web/2020/https://www.oecd.org/els/soc/trends-in-income-inequality-and-its-impact-on-economic-growth-SEM-WP163.pdf (both copies read).
- Limits: association from country panels, measures income not wealth inequality, tested by others (contested). The OECD 2015 "In It Together" figure of "4.7 percentage points" appeared only in search-result summaries; the report itself was NOT opened. Use the 2014 numbers above or leave it out.

**16.** Child-focused public spending has the highest value per dollar (Hendren and Sprung-Keyser).
- Quote (PDF p.5): "In many cases, these policies actually pay for themselves in the long-run."
- Figures: four child health insurance expansions averaged $1.78 repaid to government per $1 of expenditure; three of four fully repaid. Marginal value of public funds of roughly 44 for Perry Preschool and 12 for Abecedarian (PDF p.5-6).
- Source: Hendren and Sprung-Keyser, "A Unified Welfare Analysis of Government Policies", NBER WP 26144 (Aug 2019 revision), published QJE 135(3): 1209-1318 (2020). https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26144/revisions/w26144.rev1.pdf (introduction read; QJE version not opened).
- Limits: US policies, 133 historical changes. Government-budget view: the 44 and 12 rest on small randomised programmes and long-run earnings. Policies for adults have lower values. These are American health and school programmes, not the spending a Uruguay or Danish tax would pay for.

**17.** R&D and innovation: large social returns.
- Quote (abstract): "Overall, our estimates suggest that the social returns are very large."
- Source: Jones and Summers, "A Calculation of the Social Returns to Innovation", NBER WP 27863 (2020). https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w27863/w27863.pdf (abstract and introduction read; the benefit-cost table was not extracted).
- Limits: returns to innovation investment broadly, not to public R&D funding specifically.

**18.** Spending that the country proposals earmark. Taken from this repository's own stories, which cite the proposals; the underlying proposal documents were NOT re-opened here.
- Uruguay (`content/stories/uy.md`): the PIT-CNT commission's 24 Nov 2025 surcharge, proceeds to "a fund against child poverty" (poverty reaches 17.3% of people and 32.2% of children under six); the March 2025 Frente Amplio senators' bill for a 25% surcharge on the Impuesto al Patrimonio would feed the Fondo Nacional de Vivienda (housing fund).
- Denmark (`content/stories/dk.md`): Frederiksen's 26 Feb 2026 proposal, 0.5% above 25 million kr., 22,000 people, 6 to 7 billion kr. a year "for smaller school classes".
- Bolivia and Colombia: no earmark found in the repository stories. COULD NOT VERIFY.
- Limits: no effect on growth is estimated for any of these uses. Only the general evidence in entries 13-17 applies.

## Part 3. Kuznets and what growth measures leave out

**19.** The Kuznets line. VERIFIED only second-hand.
- Citation found everywhere: Simon Kuznets, "How To Judge Quality", The New Republic, 20 October 1962 (vol. 147, no. 16), starting on p. 29. The Internet Archive holds that issue (https://archive.org/details/sim_new-republic_1962-10-20_147_16) but its text is lending-restricted and was NOT opened.
- Passage as quoted by Resilience.org (Reference 2 cites the New Republic piece): "Distinctions must be kept in mind between quantity and quality of growth, between costs and returns, and between the short and long run. Goals for more growth should specify more growth of what and for what." The Wikiquote entry gives the last sentence and cites The New Republic vol. 147 p. 29. Fioramonti (University of Pretoria, 2014) cites it as "p. 29".
- Source URLs: https://www.resilience.org/stories/2013-11-08/economic-growth-a-social-pathology/ ; https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Simon_Kuznets (both secondary).
- NOT verified: exact punctuation, whether the three sentences are consecutive (Resilience.org quoted them in separate pieces), page of each sentence, whether the piece is titled "How to Judge Quality" with that capitalisation. The brief says The New Leader; no source supports that. Do NOT publish the quotation with a page until someone reads the scan.
- Paraphrases to avoid: anything that has Kuznets saying GDP "should not be used" or "is a bad measure". The sentence asks what growth is for. The famous line that welfare of a nation "can scarcely be inferred from a measurement of national income" is from his 1934 report to the US Senate, a different source, and was not opened.

**20.** Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi (2009): GDP is not a measure of well-being.
- Quote (report summary, PDF p.13): GDP "has often been treated as if it were a measure of economic well-being. Conflating the two can lead to misleading indications about how well-off people are and entail the wrong policy decisions."
- Quote (PDF p.12): "the time is ripe for our measurement system to shift emphasis from measuring economic production to measuring people's well-being". Also: "Changing emphasis does not mean dismissing GDP".
- Example in the report: "traffic jams may increase GDP as a result of the increased use of gasoline, but obviously not the quality of life"; and it says averages can mislead: "most people can be worse off even though average income is increasing" if inequality rises enough.
- Source: Report by the Commission on the Measurement of Economic Performance and Social Progress, 14 Sep 2009. Copy read: https://web.archive.org/web/2013/http://www.stiglitz-sen-fitoussi.fr/documents/rapport_anglais.pdf (summary and recommendation 1 read).
- Limits: it does not say growth is unimportant, and does not discuss wealth taxes.

**21.** OECD on growth measures: NOT VERIFIED from a primary OECD document. Only search-result summaries describing the OECD Better Life Index (2011) and How's Life? as responses to Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi. Needs a read of the OECD report before use.

**22.** Which investment is not socially useful.
- Verified: OECD 2018 (PDF p.64) discusses "non-productive assets (e.g. housing)" versus productive ones and says the distinction is hard to draw (entry 11).
- Baumol (1990, JPE 98(5): 893-921) on productive, unproductive and destructive entrepreneurship: only the citation was found; NOT opened. Greenwood and Scharfstein on finance: not searched.
- Verdict: no primary source opened supports a claim that a category of investment is "not socially useful". Do not write one.

## Part 4. Who a wealth tax touches, and firms

**23.** Households affected depends on the threshold.
- OECD 2018 (PDF p.82-83): France 2016, 351,152 tax households, "slightly less than 1% of the total number of tax households"; Spain from 981,498 (2007) to 188,680 (2015) taxpayers after thresholds rose; Norway about 11% of taxpayers in 2016; Bern 2001-11, 30% of all taxpayers and 41% of married households had a non-zero wealth tax liability (low Swiss thresholds, CHF 25,000-200,000 in 2014).
- Saez and Zucman: "Less than 0.1% of US families would be liable for the Warren wealth tax." Source: Saez and Zucman, "Progressive Wealth Taxation", Brookings Papers on Economic Activity conference draft, 5-6 Sep 2019, PDF p.5, footnote 3. https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/Saez-Zucman_conference-draft.pdf
- Denmark: threshold around the 98th percentile (JJKZ, PDF p.3); about 35,000 families paid in the last year (repo story `dk.md`, from a Danish government source not re-opened).
- Limits: counts of taxpayers, not of firm owners. Swiss figures show a wealth tax with a low threshold hits the middle class: it must not be used to describe a high-threshold tax.

**24.** Share of top wealth in business assets.
- Verified: OECD 2018 (PDF p.37): "Households in top net wealth deciles also have high shares of gross wealth held in the form of self-employed businesses." No percentage in that passage.
- Verified: top 2% of the Swedish wealth distribution control close to 10% of total Swedish employment and 15% of value added through private businesses (entry 2).
- NOT verified: a DIW figure for Germany (DIW pages refused download; a DIW discussion paper DP 1502 exists, not read) and an SSB figure for Norway (not found). A search summary of an IMF Germany report said private business wealth is 25% of national wealth and 95% of it is held by the top 10%; that was a snippet only, the IMF document was not read. Do not use.

**25.** Did owners' exit or firm closure rise after wealth taxes?
- Sweden and Denmark: out-migration events by wealthy owners cut firm employment 33%, investment 22%, value added 34%, mostly by closure (entry 2). A one pp higher tax rate means about 2% fewer wealthy taxpayers (entry 1). Closure here follows the owner leaving, not the tax directly.
- Norway 2021-25: no rise in bankruptcies, no fall in investment or dividends (entry 9).
- Spain: higher tax pushed owners to hold more business assets that are exempt (entry 5): it protects firms, and costs revenue.
- Switzerland: NOT FOUND. No study opened measures firm closure or start-ups after a Swiss wealth tax change.
- Colombia: NOT FOUND. The Colombian paper (entry 6) measures evasion only.
- Norway, firm payouts: the BI Norwegian Business School web summary says firms whose owners pay more wealth tax pay higher dividends and hold more cash. The paper itself (Berzins, Bøhren and Stacescu) was NOT opened. Ring (entry 9) finds no extra payouts for the 2021-25 reforms. The two are not in conflict only if they study different periods; not checked.

## Safe to write (each is in an opened primary source)

1. Migration responses to wealth taxes exist but are small in aggregate: a 1 pp higher top rate cuts the stock of wealthy taxpayers about 2% and investment about 0.07% (Jakobsen et al., entry 1). Say "in Sweden and Denmark".
2. Norway's 2021-25 increases: at most 100 people left; revenue up about 71.5%; no extra bankruptcies, no fall in investment (Ring 2026, entry 9). Label as a working paper.
3. Studies on saving: Spain found no negative effect on savings (entry 5), Sweden found responses reflect evasion and avoidance not saving (entry 7), Norway found more saving (entry 8).
4. The big measured responses are in what is declared, tied to design and enforcement (Swiss 43%, Spain 2.6x revenue, Sweden a third under-reporting, Colombia two-fifths of the top 0.01% evading): the argument is for a broad base, few reliefs, third-party reporting.
5. IMF: 1% of GDP in public investment raises output about 0.4% in the same year and 1.5% after four years, larger with slack and good projects (entry 13).
6. IMF staff: "redistribution appears generally benign in terms of its impact on growth" (entry 14).
7. OECD: inequality lowers medium-term growth, driven by the bottom 40% (entry 15), with its own caveat that estimates are illustrative.
8. Hendren and Sprung-Keyser: US child health insurance expansions repaid $1.78 per $1 on average (entry 16).
9. Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi: GDP "has often been treated as if it were a measure of economic well-being" (entry 20).
10. Thresholds decide the reach: under 0.1% of US families under Warren's design; under 1% in France (entries 23).

## Be ready to answer (unfavourable, accurate wording)

- OECD 2018 recommends against a net wealth tax on top of capital income and inheritance taxes (entry 10).
- Marginal cost of public funds 2.2, rising to 4.2 with migration, in Denmark and Sweden (entry 2).
- Per extra dollar of revenue, 0.54 dollars lost to intensive-margin responses and 0.22 to migration (entry 2).
- When a wealthy entrepreneur leaves, their firm's employment falls 33% (entry 2); the answer: 45% of closed firms are absorbed by others, aggregate effects are small.
- Danish taxable wealth +30% after 8 years of lower tax at the top, partly avoidance (entry 3).
